Introduction — An Ecosystem Is a Self-Running Unit
• An ecosystem is nature's working unit: a patch of the world — as small as a pond or as large as a forest — where living organisms interact among themselves and with the physical environment around them, the whole system running on sunlight, air, water and soil.
• DEFINITION — An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature in which living organisms (the biotic community) interact among themselves and also with the surrounding physical (abiotic) environment. Neither half works alone: the organisms shape the environment, and the environment decides which organisms can live there.
• DEFINITION — Every ecosystem has a structure and a function. Structure is what it is made of — species composition and the physical arrangement of components. Function is what it does — the four great processes: productivity, decomposition, energy flow and nutrient cycling.
• WHY this chapter studies processes, not lists — the four processes let you analyse any ecosystem on Earth, crop field to open ocean, with one toolkit; every section that follows examines one closely.
• EXAMPLE — A man-made aquarium and a natural forest are both ecosystems. The aquarium needs feeding and cleaning from outside; the forest runs itself — natural ecosystems are self-sustaining, while artificial ones like crop fields lean on human subsidy.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any ecosystem a question throws at you, ask four questions in order: Who produces food here, and how fast? Who consumes it? Who decomposes the dead, and how? How do energy and nutrients move through it? These four answers are a complete ecological audit of the system.
• YOUR TURN — A sealed glass bottle with water, algae, snails and bacteria sits on a sunlit shelf for a year, nothing added. Is it an ecosystem? Answer: yes — producers, consumers and decomposers interacting with water, light and dissolved gases; interactions, not size or wildness, make an ecosystem.
✔ KEY POINT — Ecosystem = biotic community + abiotic environment + their interactions, functioning as one unit. Its four functional processes are productivity, decomposition, energy flow and nutrient cycling — the skeleton of this entire chapter.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — A habitat is only the place where an organism lives; an ecosystem is the place PLUS the community PLUS all the interactions and flows. Options that reduce an ecosystem to a location or to a list of species are describing something smaller than an ecosystem.

The Cast and the Stage — Components and Stratification
• DEFINITION — The abiotic components are the non-living physical and chemical factors: inorganic substances (carbon, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, water), temperature, light and soil characteristics. They decide which organisms can survive in a place and how well they can grow.
• DEFINITION — The biotic components are the living members, sorted by their job: producers (autotrophs, chiefly green plants, that make food), consumers (heterotrophs that eat other organisms — herbivores, carnivores and others), and decomposers (chiefly fungi and bacteria that digest the dead).
• DEFINITION — Stratification is the vertical distribution of different species occupying different levels in a community. In a forest, trees occupy the top vertical stratum or layer, shrubs the second, and herbs and grasses occupy the bottom layers.
• WHY stratification exists — light is captured from above, so plants stack in storeys to share it: tall trees take full sun, shrubs manage with filtered light, herbs survive in floor shade. Layering packs more species into the same ground area.
• EXAMPLE — Look up, then down, in a forest: canopy trees, a shrub layer beneath, herbs and grasses on the floor — three storeys of life in one vertical column. A pond layers the same way: floating plants at the surface, submerged plants below, decomposers at the bottom.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify any named factor in two steps. Is it alive? If not, it is abiotic. If alive, what is its job? Makes food — producer; eats others — consumer; digests the dead — decomposer. Two questions classify everything in the chapter.
• YOUR TURN — Classify these: sunlight, phytoplankton, a frog, soil bacteria, dissolved oxygen. Answer: sunlight and dissolved oxygen are abiotic; phytoplankton are producers; the frog is a consumer; soil bacteria are decomposers.
✔ KEY POINT — Ecosystem structure = species composition + abiotic factors + stratification (vertical layering: trees on top, shrubs in the middle, herbs and grasses at the bottom in a forest). Components interact — that interaction is what makes structure characteristic of each ecosystem type.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Stratification means VERTICAL distribution. Horizontal spread of species across a landscape is a different pattern; any option calling stratification the horizontal arrangement of a community is wrong.

The Pond — A Complete Ecosystem in Miniature
• DEFINITION — The pond is NCERT's model ecosystem: a shallow water body that is a self-contained unit displaying every structural and functional feature of ecosystems in general. If you understand the pond, you understand the architecture of them all.
• The abiotic component is the water itself, with all inorganic and organic substances dissolved in it, plus the rich soil deposit at the bottom. Solar input, temperature cycle, day-length and other climatic conditions regulate the rate of function of the entire pond.
• The producers include the phytoplankton, some algae, and the floating, submerged and marginal plants at the edges. Tiny phytoplankton suspended in the lit surface water do most of the pond's photosynthesis.
• The consumers are the zooplankton, the free-swimming forms such as fishes, and the bottom-dwelling forms. The decomposers are fungi, bacteria and flagellates, especially abundant at the bottom, where dead matter settles.
• EXAMPLE — The pond performs every function of the biosphere in miniature: producers convert inorganic material into organic material using the sun's radiant energy; consumers use and convert it; decomposers return inorganic nutrients to the producers. These events repeat over and over again.
• WHY the decomposers crowd the bottom — dead plankton, leaves and faecal matter sink, so the raw material collects in the sediment; the fungi and bacteria gather where the work lands.
• WORKED PATTERN — Use the pond as a template: name any ecosystem's producers, consumers, decomposers, abiotic base and energy source, then state the loop — fixation, consumption, decomposition, reuse. Forest, lake and grassland questions are the same five slots with a different cast.
• YOUR TURN — A pond receives no outside food, yet its fishes survive for years. Where does a bottom-dwelling fish's energy ultimately come from? Answer: from sunlight, captured by the pond's own phytoplankton and plants and passed up the food chain and through detritus.
✔ KEY POINT — Pond blueprint: abiotic base = water + dissolved substances + bottom soil deposit; producers = phytoplankton, algae, floating/submerged/marginal plants; consumers = zooplankton, free-swimming and bottom-dwelling animals; decomposers = fungi, bacteria and flagellates, concentrated at the bottom.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Zooplankton are consumers, not producers, and phytoplankton are producers, not consumers — the two plankton groups swap roles in wrong options every year. Also: in the pond it is the DECOMPOSERS that are concentrated at the bottom, not the producers.

Primary Productivity — GPP, NPP and the Minus Sign
• DEFINITION — Primary production is the amount of biomass or organic matter produced per unit area over a time period by plants during photosynthesis. It is expressed in terms of weight (g m-2) or energy (kcal m-2).
• DEFINITION — Productivity is the rate of biomass production, expressed as g m-2 yr-1 or (kcal m-2) yr-1 so that different ecosystems can be compared. A rate always carries a per-unit-time tail — that is what separates productivity from a mere standing quantity of biomass.
• DEFINITION — Gross primary productivity (GPP) is the rate of production of organic matter during photosynthesis: the total rate at which producers fix energy and build biomass, before any of it is spent.
• DEFINITION — Net primary productivity (NPP) is what remains after the producers pay their own running costs: NPP = GPP − R, where R is the energy lost to respiration. NPP is the available biomass for consumption by heterotrophs — herbivores and decomposers.
• WHY the minus sign is the whole story — a plant must burn a large share of its own produce in respiration to stay alive, grow roots and repair tissue. Only the surplus, NPP, can feed the animal world: every food chain on Earth is funded by NPP, not GPP.
• EXAMPLE — A grassland fixes at GPP = 3000 kcal m-2 yr-1 and respires R = 1200. Its NPP = 3000 − 1200 = 1800 kcal m-2 yr-1 — and that 1800, only that, is the budget for the plot's grasshoppers, cattle and decomposers.
• WORKED PATTERN — Given any two of GPP, R and NPP, the third is arithmetic: NPP = GPP − R; GPP = NPP + R; R = GPP − NPP. Keep units identical, and sanity-check: NPP never exceeds GPP, because R is never negative.
• YOUR TURN — A forest stand shows NPP = 1200 g m-2 yr-1 while its plants respire R = 800 g m-2 yr-1. What is its GPP? Answer: GPP = NPP + R = 1200 + 800 = 2000 g m-2 yr-1 — the gross fixation is the net surplus plus what the plants themselves burned.
✔ KEY POINT — GPP is the total rate of organic matter production in photosynthesis; NPP = GPP − R (R = respiration losses) is the biomass actually available to heterotrophs. Productivity is a rate: weight or energy per unit area per unit time.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — NPP is NOT "GPP minus decomposition". The subtraction is respiration (R) — the producers' own metabolism. Decomposition is a separate process carried out by different organisms, and it belongs to a later section, not to this equation.

The Planetary Ledger — 170 Billion Tons, Mostly From Land
• DEFINITION — The annual net primary productivity of the whole biosphere is approximately 170 billion tons (dry weight) of organic matter — the food budget produced for every heterotroph on the planet.
• The ocean paradox: despite occupying about 70 per cent of Earth's surface, the oceans produce only 55 billion tons of that 170. The rest — about 115 billion tons — is produced on land, on barely 30 per cent of the surface.
• WHY the oceans underperform — ocean water is mostly nutrient-poor, and sunlight fades with depth, so most of the ocean's volume is dark and unfed. Per square metre the open ocean produces far less than fertile land; its huge total comes from area, not rich production.
• What primary productivity depends on: the plant species inhabiting the area, environmental factors, availability of nutrients, and the photosynthetic capacity of the plants. As these differ from place to place, productivity varies across ecosystems.
• WORKED PATTERN — Percentage drill: oceans contribute 55 of 170 billion tons, about 32 per cent of world NPP, from about 70 per cent of the surface; land contributes 115 of 170, about 68 per cent, from about 30 per cent. Any option claiming oceans dominate productivity because of area falls to this ratio.
• EXAMPLE — Put the four dependencies to work: a tropical rainforest (dense species, warm, wet, fast nutrient cycling) out-produces a desert (sparse species, water-starved) and the open ocean (nutrient-starved) per square metre by a wide margin — same sun, different conditions.
• YOUR TURN — If the biosphere's NPP is 170 billion tons and oceans give 55 billion tons, state the land contribution and the land share. Answer: 170 − 55 = 115 billion tons, which is 115/170, roughly 68 per cent of the total, from roughly 30 per cent of Earth's surface.
✔ KEY POINT — Biosphere NPP is approximately 170 billion tons (dry weight) per year; oceans contribute only 55 billion tons despite covering about 70 per cent of the surface; land contributes the remaining 115. Primary productivity depends on plant species, environment, nutrient availability and photosynthetic capacity.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The 170 billion tons is NET primary productivity (not GPP) and it is dry weight. And the split is 55 ocean : 115 land — options that hand the oceans 115 billion tons, or call 170 the land figure, are the standard plants.

Secondary Productivity — The Consumers' Account
• DEFINITION — Secondary productivity is the rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers: the rate at which herbivores, carnivores and other heterotrophs convert the food they eat into their own body substance — growth of tissues, production of offspring, storage.
• Keep the ledger straight: primary productivity is the producers' rate, at the base of the food chain; secondary productivity is the consumers' rate, at every level above the base — biomass formation by two different teams.
• WHY secondary productivity is always far smaller than primary — a consumer loses energy at every conversion: some food is never eaten, some eaten food leaves undigested as faeces, and much absorbed energy is burned in respiration. Only the small remainder becomes new consumer biomass.
• EXAMPLE — A herd of cattle turns only a small fraction of grass energy into beef biomass; farmers call this poor feed-conversion, ecologists call it why consumer biomass is always a small shadow of producer productivity — and why meat is an expensive way to harvest sunlight.
• WORKED PATTERN — Spot the owner of the rate: production of organic matter in photosynthesis — GPP; available biomass for heterotrophs — NPP; formation of new organic matter by consumers — secondary productivity. The organism named in the definition decides the term.
• YOUR TURN — A goat grazing a pasture gains 2 kg of body mass in a month. Which productivity does this 2 kg per month measure? Answer: secondary productivity — it is new organic matter formed by a consumer, at a rate per unit time.
✔ KEY POINT — Secondary productivity = the rate at which consumers form new organic matter from their food. It is a consumer-side rate, distinct from both GPP (producer total) and NPP (producer surplus available to consumers).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — NPP is the biomass AVAILABLE TO heterotrophs; it is not the biomass heterotrophs actually build. The biomass consumers succeed in building is secondary productivity — a smaller, later quantity. Options that equate the two hand consumers a 100 per cent conversion efficiency that never exists.

Decomposition — The Cleanup That Feeds the Soil
• DEFINITION — Decomposition is the breakdown of complex organic matter into inorganic substances like carbon dioxide, water and nutrients, carried out by decomposers — the process that unlocks elements trapped in dead bodies for reuse.
• DEFINITION — Detritus is the raw material of decomposition: dead plant remains such as leaves, bark and flowers, and dead remains of animals, including faecal matter. Everything an ecosystem produces eventually becomes detritus.
• DEFINITION — Detritivores are animals that feed on detritus and break it into smaller particles — the earthworm is NCERT's example. They are decomposition's mechanical crew: chewing, shredding and mixing prepare the material for chemical attack.
• The chemical crew is microscopic: bacteria and fungi, the decomposers proper, secrete enzymes onto detritus, digest it outside their bodies, absorb the soluble products and release inorganic nutrients. Decomposition is largely an oxygen-requiring process — most of the crew are aerobic respirers.
• WHY decomposition is indispensable — imagine it stopping: every leaf, corpse and dropping would pile up forever, carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus would stay locked inside the dead, and producers would starve under the world's own remains. Decomposition is the recycling department without which production halts.
• EXAMPLE — A fallen leaf is shredded by earthworms and other detritivores, then colonised by fungi whose pale threads spread through it, until within a season it has vanished into soil — its minerals now feeding the roots of the tree it fell from.
• WORKED PATTERN — Divide the labour correctly: detritivores fragment (a physical, animal act); bacteria and fungi catabolise (a chemical, enzyme act). If an option makes the earthworm secrete digestive enzymes, or a bacterium chew a leaf, the roles are swapped.
• YOUR TURN — Waterlogged cold bogs accumulate peat — undecomposed plant matter. Which property of decomposition explains this? Answer: decomposition is largely oxygen-requiring, and waterlogged, anaerobic conditions inhibit the decomposers, so dead matter builds up.
✔ KEY POINT — Decomposition breaks complex organic matter in detritus (dead plant and animal remains, faecal matter) down to carbon dioxide, water and inorganic nutrients. Detritivores like the earthworm fragment it; bacteria and fungi digest it; the process is largely oxygen-requiring.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The earthworm is a detritivore, not a decomposer in NCERT's classification: it breaks detritus into smaller particles but does not itself mineralise it. And decomposition does not need light — the crew works in dark soil, powered by the chemical energy of the dead matter itself.

The Five Steps of Decomposition

• DEFINITION — Decomposition runs in five steps, in order: fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification, mineralisation. The first three act on the detritus; the last two act in the soil on their products.
• Step 1 — Fragmentation: detritivores such as the earthworm break detritus into smaller particles, multiplying the surface exposed to enzymes and microbes.
• Step 2 — Leaching: water-soluble inorganic nutrients go down into the soil horizon and get precipitated as unavailable salts. EXAMPLE — rain through a leaf pile carries minerals down and locks them away, instead of delivering them to roots.
• Step 3 — Catabolism: bacterial and fungal enzymes degrade detritus into simpler inorganic substances — the chemical heart of the process.
• Steps 1 to 3 operate simultaneously on the detritus: a leaf is shredded, soaked and enzyme-digested at the same time by different members of the crew.
• Step 4 — Humification: forms humus, a dark-coloured amorphous substance highly resistant to microbial action, decomposing extremely slowly and, being colloidal, serving as a reservoir of nutrients in the soil.
• Step 5 — Mineralisation: humus is further degraded by some microbes, releasing inorganic nutrients into the soil solution. WHY it matters — this step finally hands the nutrients back to roots.
• WORKED PATTERN — Hold the chain as one picture: Fragmentation (shred it) → Leaching (soak salts down) → Catabolism (enzymes simplify it) → Humification (bank it) → Mineralisation (release the minerals). First three process fresh detritus; humification stores; mineralisation withdraws.
• YOUR TURN — Why does dark soil feed plants for years while fresh litter feeds them for a season? Answer: the dark material is humus (built by humification, extremely decay-resistant); mineralisation is the slow step that finally releases its nutrients.
✔ KEY POINT — Five steps in order: fragmentation, leaching, catabolism (simultaneous, on detritus), then humification and mineralisation in soil. Humus is dark, amorphous, colloidal, decay-resistant, a nutrient reservoir; mineralisation releases its inorganic nutrients.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Humification BUILDS the resistant store (humus accumulates); mineralisation RELEASES nutrients (humus is degraded) — the most-swapped pair in the chapter. And leaching precipitates nutrients as UNAVAILABLE salts, the opposite of washing them to roots.

What Controls Decomposition — Chemistry and Climate
• DEFINITION — The rate of decomposition is controlled by two sets of factors: the chemical composition of the detritus itself, and climatic factors acting on the decomposers. Same crew, different speeds — the menu and the weather decide.
• The chemistry rule: decomposition is slower if detritus is rich in lignin and chitin, quicker if rich in nitrogen and water-soluble substances like sugars. Lignin (wood's tough cement) and chitin (fungal and insect armour) resist enzymes; nitrogen and sugars are microbe food.
• The climate rule: temperature and soil moisture are the most important climatic factors, regulating decomposition through their effects on soil microbes — warmth speeds their enzymes, moisture keeps them alive and moving.
• WHY bogs preserve and tropics recycle — a warm and moist environment favours decomposition, whereas low temperature and anaerobiosis inhibit it, resulting in build-up of organic materials.
• EXAMPLE — In the same forest, soft sugar-rich fruit pulp disappears in days while a lignin-armoured woody branch persists for years. Same microbes, same climate — only the chemistry of the detritus differs, and the rate differs with it.
• WORKED PATTERN — Predict-and-explain is the standard shape: nitrogenous, sugary detritus — faster (chemistry). Chitin-rich detritus — slower (chemistry). Frozen, dry or waterlogged-anaerobic soil — slower (climate and oxygen). Warm, moist, aerated soil — faster. Name the controlling factor for each prediction.
• YOUR TURN — Identical leaf litter sits in three chambers: warm and moist, cold and moist, warm and dry. Rank the rates. Answer: warm-moist fastest (both climatic factors favourable); warm-dry and cold-moist both slow — dryness starves microbes of water, cold slows their enzymes — and cold waterlogged litter is slowest of all once oxygen runs out.
✔ KEY POINT — Decomposition is slower for lignin- and chitin-rich detritus, quicker for nitrogen- and sugar-rich detritus. Temperature and soil moisture are the key climatic controls, acting through soil microbes: warm and moist favours decomposition; low temperature and anaerobiosis inhibit it and let organic matter build up.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "More moisture always means faster decomposition" is false at the extreme: fully waterlogged soil turns anaerobic, and anaerobiosis inhibits decomposition even when warm and wet — that is exactly how organic matter builds up in bogs and swamps.

Energy Flow — A One-Way River
• DEFINITION — Energy flow is the unidirectional transfer of energy through an ecosystem: sunlight captured by producers, passed to consumers level by level, finally dissipated as heat. Energy enters, is used, and leaves degraded — never recycled.
• The source: except for the deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystem, the sun is the only source of energy for all ecosystems on Earth. Around dark ocean-floor vents, chemosynthetic bacteria fix food from chemical energy — the one exception proving the solar rule.
• The first law at work: plants and photosynthetic bacteria fix the sun's radiant energy and make food from simple inorganic substances; producers are eaten by consumers; when organisms die, their dead biomass becomes the decomposers' energy source.
• The second law at work: ecosystems are not exempt from the second law of thermodynamics — every transfer increases disorder and sheds energy as heat, so ecosystems need a constant energy supply to build molecules and counteract disorder. Cut off the input and the system runs down.
• WHY energy cannot cycle while nutrients can — energy degrades at every step into dispersed heat no organism can gather again. Nutrients are matter: a phosphorus atom is the same atom forever, usable, released and reusable endlessly. Flow for energy, cycle for matter — the chapter's deepest contrast.
• EXAMPLE — A forest returns heat to the sky for a century without recycling a joule of energy, while its nitrogen atoms circle leaf → deer → dung → soil → leaf hundreds of times in the same century.
• WORKED PATTERN — Templates: "Why must producers keep photosynthesising?" — energy flows one way and is lost as heat at every transfer; the system needs constant input. "Why do ecosystems not run out of minerals?" — nutrients are recycled by decomposers. Matching the question to flow-versus-cycle settles most theory questions here.
• YOUR TURN — If the sun were switched off, which fails first in a forest: energy supply or nutrient supply? Answer: energy — photosynthesis and the food chains it funds stop at once, while nutrients in soil and biomass keep circulating for a while through decomposition.
✔ KEY POINT — Energy flow is unidirectional (sun → producers → consumers → heat) and obeys the laws of thermodynamics: ecosystems need constant energy input. The sun powers all ecosystems except the deep-sea hydrothermal system. Nutrients cycle; energy does not.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Decomposers do not "return energy to the producers" or "recycle energy back to the sun's account". They release NUTRIENTS for reuse; the energy in dead matter is spent by the decomposers themselves and lost as heat, like everywhere else.

The PAR Filter — How Little Sunlight Runs the World
• DEFINITION — PAR, photosynthetically active radiation, is the portion of incoming solar radiation plants can actually use for photosynthesis. Not all sunlight is usable: of total incident solar radiation, less than 50 per cent is PAR.
• The second filter is harsher: plants capture only 2 to 10 per cent of the PAR. Reflection, transmission through leaves and saturation of the photosynthetic machinery let the rest slip past unfixed.
• Multiply the filters and feel the squeeze: from 100 units of incident radiation, fewer than 50 are even usable, and plants fix only 2 to 10 per cent of those — roughly 1 to 5 units of the original 100 enter the living world as gross production. This small amount of energy sustains the entire living world.
• WHY this matters for everything downstream — the smallness of the capture explains the shape of ecology: why producers must carpet every lit surface, why consumers are always outnumbered in energy terms, and why food chains stay short. The pyramids later in this chapter are this bottleneck, drawn.
• EXAMPLE — A sunlit hectare of grassland receives a flood of radiation, yet the grass fixes only a sliver of its usable half. Every grasshopper, frog, snake and hawk on that hectare must be paid out of that sliver — and the ten per cent law spends it further at every link.
• WORKED PATTERN — Filter arithmetic: captured = incident × PAR fraction × capture fraction. Incident 1000 units, PAR 45 per cent, capture 4 per cent of PAR: PAR = 450; captured = 18 units. Apply the two filters in sequence — never apply 2 to 10 per cent to the full incident radiation.
• YOUR TURN — Incident solar radiation on a pond is 2000 kcal m-2 yr-1, of which 48 per cent is PAR, and the phytoplankton capture 5 per cent of the PAR. How much energy enters gross production? Answer: PAR = 2000 × 0.48 = 960; captured = 960 × 0.05 = 48 kcal m-2 yr-1 — only 2.4 per cent of the original radiation, inside the expected squeeze.
✔ KEY POINT — Less than 50 per cent of incident solar radiation is PAR, and plants capture only 2 to 10 per cent of the PAR. This tiny captured fraction sustains the entire living world — it is the narrow gate through which all ecosystem energy must pass.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The 2 to 10 per cent is a fraction OF PAR, not of total incident solar radiation — options that let plants capture "2 to 10 per cent of all sunlight" understate the filter, and options claiming "less than 50 per cent of sunlight is captured" confuse the PAR share with the capture share. Two different percentages, two different bases.

Food Chains, Trophic Levels and Standing Crop

• DEFINITION — A food chain is the sequence of organisms through which food energy passes: each organism eats the one before it and is eaten by the one after. A trophic level is one step in that chain, shared by all organisms feeding the same way.
• DEFINITION — The grazing food chain (GFC) begins with producers: green plants eaten by herbivores, herbivores by carnivores — grass to grasshopper to frog to snake.
• DEFINITION — The detritus food chain (DFC) begins with dead organic matter, worked on by decomposers — heterotrophs, mainly fungi and bacteria — whose enzymes break dead matter into simpler inorganic materials they absorb.
• The two chains interlink: animals of one chain are prey to animals of the other, weaving a food web. The distinction is only the starting point — living producers or dead detritus.
• WHY the detritus chain dominates on land — which chain carries more energy depends on the ecosystem: in aquatic ecosystems the GFC is the major conduit; on land a much larger fraction flows through the DFC, because most plant matter is never grazed alive and dies into detritus.
• The trophic ladder: producers T1; herbivores (primary consumers) T2; small carnivores (secondary consumers) T3; large or top carnivores (tertiary consumers) T4. Energy decreases at successive levels, so the ladder has only a few rungs.
• DEFINITION — Standing crop is the mass of living material at a trophic level at a particular time, measured as biomass or as number of organisms per unit area — a photograph, not a film: a snapshot, not a rate.
• EXAMPLE — Pond chain: phytoplankton (T1) grazed by zooplankton (T2), eaten by small fish (T3), eaten by large fish (T4). Beside the pond, fallen leaves feed a detritus chain of fungi, bacteria and earthworms — and birds eat the earthworms, stitching the chains.
• WORKED PATTERN — Assign a trophic level by asking what it eats, counting from producers: eats producers — T2; eats herbivores — T3; eats carnivores — T4. Tell chains apart by the first link only: living plant — GFC; dead matter — DFC.
• YOUR TURN — In grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk, give the trophic levels of frog and snake. Answer: frog — secondary consumer, T3; snake — tertiary consumer, T4 (the hawk above reaches a rare T5).
✔ KEY POINT — GFC starts from producers and is the major energy conduit in aquatic ecosystems; DFC starts from detritus and carries the larger energy fraction on land. Levels: producers T1, herbivores T2, carnivores T3, top carnivores T4. Standing crop is the living mass at a level at a particular time.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Standing crop is not productivity: one is an amount at an instant (a stock), the other a rate per unit time (a flow). And tertiary consumer names the FOURTH trophic level, not the third — count producers as level one.

The Ten Per Cent Law — Lindeman's Toll
• DEFINITION — The ten per cent law of energy transfer, given by Lindeman, states that only 10 per cent of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to (incorporated into) the next trophic level; the remaining roughly 90 per cent is lost to the environment, mostly as heat, besides what is never eaten, never digested, or spent in respiration.
• WHY the toll is unavoidable — the second law of thermodynamics doing bookkeeping: digestion is incomplete, metabolism leaks heat, movement and body warmth are paid for in energy. No transfer between living compartments is perfectly efficient, and 10 per cent is nature's observed average.
• The consequence: because energy decreases at successive levels, grazing food chains rarely exceed four or five trophic levels — beyond that, too little energy arrives to support a breeding population of consumers.
• EXAMPLE — The rarity of tigers is arithmetic. A forest's plants may fix millions of units, but deer receive a tenth, and tigers a tenth of that tenth — one per cent of the producer energy. Top predators therefore need enormous productive territory, and they are always few.
• WORKED PATTERN — Chain arithmetic: multiply by 0.1 per step upward: producers 80,000 kcal → T2 = 8,000 → T3 = 800 → T4 = 80. Count arrows, not organisms — the exponent of 0.1 is the number of transfers to the level asked. Given a level's energy and asked for producers, divide by 0.1 per step.
• YOUR TURN — A peacock eats snakes, which eat mice, which eat plant produce. If the producer level trapped 20,000 J, how much energy is available to the peacock as food? Answer: three transfers — plant → mice → snake → peacock — so 20,000 × 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.1 = 20 J.
✔ KEY POINT — Only about 10 per cent of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next (Lindeman's ten per cent law); the rest is lost, largely as heat. Energy therefore decreases at successive trophic levels, limiting food chains to about four or five levels.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The ten per cent law is the single most examinable number in this chapter: it powers questions on chain arithmetic, pyramid shape, food-chain length and predator rarity. When stuck, draw the ladder and toll each arrow by a tenth.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The law governs energy TRANSFER between levels, not the share of standing crop, numbers or biomass. And it is "10 per cent of the energy at the lower level reaches the next" — not "each level keeps 10 per cent of the original producer energy"; the tenth applies afresh at every step, which is why the decay is so steep.

Ecological Pyramids — Pictures of Trophic Structure
• DEFINITION — An ecological pyramid is a graphical representation of trophic structure: stacked bars, one per trophic level, producers at the base (first trophic level) and top consumers at the apex, bar length carrying the measurement.
• Three kinds of pyramid measure three different things: the pyramid of numbers counts individual organisms per level; the pyramid of biomass weighs the total living mass (standing crop) per level; the pyramid of energy measures energy per level per unit area per unit time — a rate, not a snapshot.
• The usual shape: in most ecosystems all the pyramids — of number, energy and biomass — are upright: producers exceed herbivores in number and biomass, and herbivores exceed carnivores, up a tapering stack. It is the transfer losses of the earlier sections, drawn as geometry.
• WHY the units betray the type — counts per unit area mean numbers; grams per unit area mean biomass; kcal per unit area per year mean energy. Read the unit line of any diagram and you know which pyramid it is before reading a bar.
• EXAMPLE — A grassland: millions of grass plants (base) support a smaller number of grasshoppers, fewer frogs, and a handful of snakes — numbers, biomass and energy pyramids all upright. This is the default picture; the next section collects the instructive exceptions.
• WORKED PATTERN — Reading any pyramid: identify the measurement from its units, then read the bars level by level from the base. If bars shrink steadily upward — upright. If any higher bar exceeds the one below it — that pyramid is inverted at that step, and the question is really asking why (the next section answers for the two classic cases).
• YOUR TURN — A diagram shows trophic bars measured in kcal m-2 yr-1, shrinking from 20,000 at the base to 2,000 and then 200. Which pyramid is it, and could its shape ever invert? Answer: it is a pyramid of energy, and it can never invert — energy flow must decrease at successive levels because of the losses at every transfer.
✔ KEY POINT — Ecological pyramids express trophic structure as numbers, biomass or energy per level, producers at the base. In most ecosystems all three are upright. Units identify the type: counts, mass per area, or energy per area per time.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The pyramid of energy is measured per unit TIME because it represents flow; the pyramids of numbers and biomass measure standing quantities at an instant. Options that give energy pyramids a per-instant unit, or call standing crop a rate, have crossed the chapter's stock-versus-flow line.

Pyramid Puzzles — Inversions and Honest Limitations

• EXAMPLE — The tree puzzle (numbers): count the organisms on one big tree — the producer level is a single individual, yet it supports many insects, which support some small birds, which support a few larger birds. The pyramid of numbers is spindle-shaped: narrow at the base, bulging at the insects, narrowing upward.
• The sea puzzle (biomass): the pyramid of biomass in the sea is generally inverted — at a given moment, fish biomass exceeds the phytoplankton supporting them.
• WHY the sea inversion breaks no law — phytoplankton are microscopic, short-lived and reproduce extremely fast: a tiny standing crop with huge turnover feeds a much larger, longer-lived fish stock. Standing crop is a snapshot; productivity is the sustaining flow — and the sea's energy pyramid stays upright, because flow still decreases level by level.
• The energy pyramid can never invert, anywhere: at each transfer most energy is lost as heat, so energy through a higher level is always less than through the level below — the one pyramid with no exceptions, guaranteed by the second law.
• DEFINITION — The limitations, honestly stated: a pyramid does not take into account that the same species may belong to two or more trophic levels — an omnivore like man, or a sparrow eating seeds and insects, sits on several levels at once.
• Second, a pyramid assumes a simple food chain, something that almost never exists in nature; it does not accommodate a food web. Third, saprophytes and decomposers get no place in pyramids, though they play a vital role.
• WORKED PATTERN — Answer inversion questions by naming the quantity: numbers can invert when one producer is huge (tree); biomass can invert when producer turnover is very fast (sea); energy never inverts. Reject any inverted energy pyramid instantly.
• YOUR TURN — A sparrow eats seeds in winter and insects in summer. Which levels does it occupy, and which limitation does this show? Answer: seed-eater — primary consumer (T2); insect-eater — secondary consumer (T3): one species can occupy two or more levels, which a simple pyramid cannot represent.
✔ KEY POINT — Numbers can bulge or partly invert (one tree, many insects); biomass is inverted in the sea (fish standing crop exceeds fast-turnover phytoplankton); energy is always upright. Limitations: multi-level species, food webs ignored, decomposers given no place.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The inverted sea biomass pyramid does NOT violate the ten per cent law: the law governs energy flow (a rate), the pyramid shows standing biomass (a stock). Fusing flow and stock is the exam's favourite trick here.

Ecological Succession — Nature Rebuilds in Sequence

• DEFINITION — Ecological succession is the gradual and fairly predictable change in the species composition of a given area: community after community replaces the previous one until a stable community develops.
• DEFINITION — The entire sequence of communities that successively change in a given area is called a sere; the transitional communities are its seral stages. During succession some species colonise and grow numerous, while others decline and disappear.
• DEFINITION — Pioneer species are the species that invade a bare area first — hardy specialists of bare rock, open water or scorched ground, where nothing else yet can survive.
• DEFINITION — The climax community is the final community of the sequence, in near equilibrium with the environment; it persists while the environment stays unchanged.
• WHY succession is orderly rather than random — each community modifies its own environment: plants shade the ground, add litter, build soil, hold moisture. The changed environment suits the pioneers less and the next stage more, so replacement follows a predictable script.
• Primary succession starts where no community ever existed — newly cooled lava, bare rock, a newly created pond. Soil must be built from nothing, so it is very slow.
• Secondary succession starts where a community was disturbed or destroyed but soil and substratum remain — abandoned farmlands, burned or cut forests, flooded lands. With soil and seeds already present, it is much faster than primary succession.
• EXAMPLE — An abandoned farm field near a forest: weeds and grasses within a season, shrubs over years, young trees after, forest in time — secondary succession replaying at field scale what primary succession performs on bare land far more slowly.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify any succession story with one question: was there soil and a community legacy at the start? Yes — secondary (fast). No — primary (slow). Then name pioneers and climax: bare rock — lichens first, forest last; new water — phytoplankton first, forest last.
• YOUR TURN — A volcanic island rises from the sea, sterile and rocky; a century later it carries shrubs. What succession is running, what were its pioneers, and its climax in a moist climate? Answer: primary; pioneers lichens; climax forest.
✔ KEY POINT — Succession is the gradual, predictable change in species composition, from pioneer species through seral stages (the whole sequence: a sere) to a climax community in near equilibrium with the environment. Primary starts bare and is slow; secondary starts on soil-bearing disturbed sites, much faster.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The climax is in NEAR equilibrium, not frozen forever: change the climate or disturb the site and succession resumes. And pioneers: lichens on bare rock, phytoplankton on open water — swapping them is a planted error.

Hydrarch and Xerarch — Two Roads to One Forest

• DEFINITION — Hydrarch succession is plant succession starting in water (ponds, lakes), progressing from hydric (very wet) to mesic (medium water) conditions and converting the water body into land.
• The hydrarch sequence: pioneers are phytoplankton, replaced by submerged plants, then floating plants, then reed-swamp plants, then marsh-meadow plants, then scrub, and finally trees. The forest is the climax; sediment and plant remains fill the water body in.
• DEFINITION — Xerarch succession is plant succession starting on bare rock or dry surfaces, progressing from xeric (very dry) to mesic conditions. Its pioneers are lichens.
• The xerarch sequence: lichens invade bare rock first, secreting acids that dissolve rock and start soil formation; they pave the way for mosses, which hold the thin soil; then herbs, shrubs, and finally trees — forest is again the climax.
• EXAMPLE — The convergence: both hydrarch and xerarch lead to medium water conditions — mesic — neither too dry nor too wet. From opposite extremes, a pond and a rock face converge on the same endpoint: a mesophytic forest.
• WHY they converge: the wet site dries as it fills with sediment and organic matter; the dry site moistens as soil deepens and vegetation shelters it. Each sequence engineers its environment towards the middle — and the middle supports forest.
• WORKED PATTERN — Name the succession from its substrate: open water with phytoplankton pioneers → hydrarch; bare rock with lichen pioneers → xerarch; disturbed land with soil intact → secondary succession. For both named series the endpoint is forest under mesic conditions.
• YOUR TURN — A new pond and a fresh lava flow appear in one district; centuries later both carry young woodland. Explain the shared endpoint. Answer: hydrarch and xerarch both progress towards mesic conditions, so both converge on forest-type climaxes despite opposite moisture extremes.
✔ KEY POINT — Hydrarch: water → phytoplankton (pioneers) → submerged → floating → reed-swamp → marsh-meadow → scrub → trees. Xerarch: bare rock → lichens (pioneers, acid-secreting) → mosses → herbs → shrubs → forest. Both lead to mesic conditions and a forest climax.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Xerarch pioneers are LICHENS, not mosses — mosses come second, on soil lichens helped create. And the hydrarch climax is not an aquatic community: the sequence ends with forest on land, the pond filled and converted.

The Carbon Cycle — A Cycle With an Ocean Vault

• DEFINITION — Nutrient cycling is the movement of nutrients through the components of an ecosystem — the recycling of matter between soil, water, air and living bodies. Nutrient cycles are also called biogeochemical cycles.
• DEFINITION — The standing state is the amount of nutrients — such as carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium — present in the soil at any given time: a snapshot quantity, not a rate.
• Cycles are sorted by their reservoir: gaseous cycles keep it in the atmosphere (or oceans) — carbon is the model; sedimentary cycles keep it in the Earth's crust — phosphorus, next section, is the model.
• The reservoir numbers: 71 per cent of total global carbon is dissolved in the oceans, which regulate atmospheric carbon dioxide. The atmosphere contains only about 1 per cent of total global carbon. Fossil fuels are another reservoir.
• EXAMPLE — The input gate in action: nearly 4 × 10^13 kg of carbon is fixed through photosynthesis annually, withdrawn from air and ocean surface into organic matter travelling through food chains.
• The return gates: much carbon returns as carbon dioxide through respiration of producers and consumers, and decomposers contribute substantially to the CO2 pool, processing waste and dead matter of land and oceans. Some fixed carbon is lost to sediments, out of circulation.
• WHY the vault leaks back — burning of wood, forest fires, fossil-fuel combustion and volcanic activity also release carbon dioxide, and human activities (deforestation, massive fossil-fuel burning) have significantly increased the release rate.
• WORKED PATTERN — Trace one carbon atom for any long answer: ocean or atmospheric CO2 → fixed by a producer → eaten by a consumer → returned by respiration, or released after death by decomposers → back to the air. Every arrow of the cycle is one leg of this journey.
• YOUR TURN — Where is most of Earth's carbon, and how much does the atmosphere hold? Answer: about 71 per cent is dissolved in the oceans; the atmosphere holds only about 1 per cent — the ocean, not the air, is the vault.
✔ KEY POINT — Carbon cycle (gaseous type): 71 per cent of global carbon is dissolved in the oceans, which regulate atmospheric CO2; the atmosphere holds about 1 per cent; photosynthesis fixes nearly 4 × 10^13 kg annually; respiration, decomposition, burning and volcanic activity return it.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The 71 per cent belongs to the OCEANS, the 1 per cent to the atmosphere; options swap them constantly. Photosynthesis withdraws carbon; respiration and decomposition return it — an option sending carbon into organisms through respiration has the arrow backwards.

The Phosphorus Cycle — The Cycle With No Sky

• DEFINITION — The phosphorus cycle is the sedimentary nutrient cycle of phosphorus: its natural reservoir is rock containing phosphorus in the form of phosphates, and it moves between rock, soil and organisms with no gaseous phase at all.
• WHY phosphorus earns its own cycle: it is a major constituent of biological membranes, nucleic acids and cellular energy transfer systems — it sits inside ATP itself — and many animals need large quantities for shells, bones and teeth.
• The route: rocks weather, minute amounts of phosphates dissolve in soil solution, and roots absorb them; animals obtain phosphorus from plants; waste and dead organisms are decomposed by phosphate-solubilising bacteria, releasing phosphorus back to soil.
• The great contrast with carbon: (1) atmospheric inputs of phosphorus through rainfall are much smaller than carbon inputs; (2) gaseous exchanges of phosphorus between organism and environment are negligible. Plainly, phosphorus has no sky route — there is no respiratory release of phosphorus into the atmosphere.
• WHY the missing sky matters — with no atmospheric reservoir, a phosphorus-poor soil is restocked only by slow rock weathering, so phosphorus readily limits growth. Carbon can be pulled straight from the air by every leaf — which is why farmers buy phosphate fertiliser, never carbon fertiliser.
• EXAMPLE — A phosphate atom's biography: locked in rock for ages, weathered into soil solution, taken up by a grass root, eaten by a deer, dropped in dung, released by phosphate-solubilising bacteria, taken up by grass again — never entering the atmosphere.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify a cycle with two questions: where is the reservoir, and is there gaseous exchange? Atmosphere/ocean plus gas exchange → gaseous (carbon); rock plus negligible exchange → sedimentary (phosphorus). The two NCERT differences are these two answers.
• YOUR TURN — Judge: like carbon dioxide, phosphorus is released into the atmosphere when organisms respire. Answer: false — gaseous exchanges of phosphorus are negligible; there is no respiratory release of phosphorus.
✔ KEY POINT — Phosphorus cycle (sedimentary): reservoir is phosphate rock; weathering feeds soil solution; plants absorb, animals obtain from plants, phosphate-solubilising bacteria release it from waste and the dead. Differences from carbon: rainfall inputs much smaller; gaseous exchanges negligible — no respiratory release.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Never give phosphorus a gaseous phase, an atmospheric reservoir or a respiratory return — all three belong to carbon. And this cycle's bacteria are phosphate-SOLUBILISING bacteria, not nitrogen-fixing bacteria from a different cycle.

Ecosystem Services — Nature's Unpaid Invoice

• DEFINITION — Ecosystem services are the products of ecosystem processes — the benefits healthy ecosystems provide human life free of cost: clean air and water, fertile soil, pollination, climate moderation and much more.
• The famous estimate: Robert Costanza and his colleagues put an average price tag of about 33 trillion US dollars per year on these fundamental services, performed free of cost.
• The comparison that makes the number bite: global gross national product (GNP) is only about 18 trillion US dollars per year — nature's unpaid work is worth nearly twice everything humanity officially produces, a subsidy appearing in no budget.
• The break-up: of the total cost of services, soil formation accounts for about 50 per cent; recreation and nutrient cycling contribute less than 10 per cent each; climate regulation and wildlife habitat about 6 per cent each.
• The services themselves, as NCERT lists them: healthy ecosystems purify air and water, mitigate droughts and floods, cycle nutrients, generate fertile soils, provide wildlife habitat, maintain biodiversity, pollinate crops, store carbon, and give aesthetic, cultural and spiritual values.
• WHY price the priceless — not to sell the services, but to make their scale visible in the language budgets understand: a wetland filtering water free looks worthless beside a priced treatment plant, until its service is valued and the comparison reverses.
• EXAMPLE — A farmer's crop is pollinated by wild insects from a nearby forest patch: no invoice, no purchased input, yet a slice of the harvest is the forest's free service. Fell the patch, and the service must suddenly be bought — the 33 trillion logic in miniature.
• WORKED PATTERN — Quote the anchors as a set: services 33 trillion dollars per year; global GNP 18 trillion; soil formation about 50 per cent; recreation and nutrient cycling under 10 per cent each; climate regulation and wildlife habitat about 6 per cent each. Options disturb one digit.
• YOUR TURN — Which single service accounts for about half the total value in the Costanza estimate? Answer: soil formation — the slowest, least glamorous process is the most valuable line on the invoice.
✔ KEY POINT — Ecosystem services are the free products of ecosystem processes. Costanza and colleagues valued them at about 33 trillion US dollars per year, nearly twice the global GNP of about 18 trillion; soil formation alone is about 50 per cent of the total.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Keep the pair the right way round: services 33 trillion, GNP 18 trillion — nature's free work is worth MORE than world production. Options shrinking services below GNP, or giving the 50 per cent share to pollination, are planted errors.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen

✔ KEY POINT — An ecosystem is a functional unit where biotic organisms interact among themselves and with the abiotic environment; structure = species composition + stratification (vertical layering), function = productivity, decomposition, energy flow and nutrient cycling. Pond crew: producers — phytoplankton, algae, floating, submerged, marginal plants; consumers — zooplankton, free-swimming, bottom-dwelling animals; decomposers — fungi, bacteria, flagellates, at the bottom.
✔ KEY POINT — GPP is the total rate of production of organic matter in photosynthesis; NPP = GPP − R (R = respiration losses) is the biomass available to heterotrophs; secondary productivity is the consumers' rate of formation of new organic matter. Biosphere NPP is about 170 billion tons (dry weight) yearly — oceans 55 billion tons from about 70 per cent of the surface, land 115.
✔ KEY POINT — Decomposition breaks detritus (dead plant and animal remains, faecal matter) into carbon dioxide, water and nutrients; it is largely oxygen-requiring. Five steps in order: fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification, mineralisation. Detritivores (earthworm) fragment; bacteria and fungi digest; humus — dark, amorphous, colloidal, decay-resistant — is a nutrient reservoir.
✔ KEY POINT — Decomposition is slower for lignin- and chitin-rich detritus, quicker for nitrogen- and sugar-rich detritus. Temperature and soil moisture are the main climatic controls: warm and moist favours decomposition; low temperature and anaerobiosis inhibit it.
✔ KEY POINT — Energy flow is unidirectional (sun to producers to consumers to heat); ecosystems need constant input, and the sun powers all ecosystems except the deep-sea hydrothermal system. Under 50 per cent of incident solar radiation is PAR; plants capture only 2 to 10 per cent of the PAR — the fraction sustaining the entire living world. Nutrients cycle; energy does not.
✔ KEY POINT — The grazing food chain starts from producers and is the major energy conduit in aquatic ecosystems; the detritus food chain starts from dead organic matter and carries the larger energy fraction on land. Levels: producers T1, herbivores T2, carnivores T3, top carnivores T4; standing crop is the living mass at a level at a time.
✔ KEY POINT — Lindeman's ten per cent law: only 10 per cent of energy transfers from one trophic level to the next, the rest lost largely as heat — so energy decreases at successive levels and grazing food chains rarely exceed four or five levels.
✔ KEY POINT — Pyramids of number, biomass and energy are upright in most ecosystems, but numbers can bulge on a single tree and biomass is inverted in the sea, where tiny phytoplankton turn over very fast. The energy pyramid is always upright; pyramids ignore multi-level species, food webs and decomposers.
✔ KEY POINT — Succession is the gradual, predictable change in species composition, from pioneer species through seral stages to a climax community in near equilibrium with the environment. Hydrarch (water; phytoplankton pioneers) and xerarch (bare rock; lichen pioneers) converge on mesic conditions and forest; secondary succession on soil-bearing sites is much faster than primary.
✔ KEY POINT — Carbon cycle (gaseous type): 71 per cent of global carbon is dissolved in the oceans, which regulate atmospheric CO2; the atmosphere holds only about 1 per cent; photosynthesis fixes nearly 4 × 10^13 kg annually; respiration, decomposition, burning and volcanic activity return it.
✔ KEY POINT — Phosphorus cycle (sedimentary type): reservoir is phosphate rock; rainfall inputs are much smaller than carbon's and gaseous exchanges are negligible — no respiratory release of phosphorus. Costanza valued ecosystem services at about 33 trillion US dollars yearly, nearly twice the global GNP of about 18 trillion, soil formation about 50 per cent.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps

• Mistake 1: Writing NPP = GPP − decomposition loss. Why it is wrong: the quantity subtracted from GPP is R, the producers' own respiratory loss; decomposition, done by other organisms on dead matter, never enters this equation. Correct principle: NPP = GPP − R, and NPP is the biomass available to heterotrophs.
• Mistake 2: Applying the 2 to 10 per cent capture to total incident solar radiation. Why it is wrong: the capture applies to PAR, itself under 50 per cent of incident radiation — two filters in sequence. Correct principle: each percentage has its own base; total capture is roughly 1 to 5 per cent.
• Mistake 3: Claiming oceans are the biosphere's most productive part because they cover 70 per cent of the surface. Why it is wrong: coverage is not productivity — oceans give only 55 of 170 billion tons; land gives 115 from 30 per cent of the surface. Correct principle: the split is 55 ocean : 115 land.
• Mistake 4: Calling the earthworm a decomposer. Why it is wrong: the earthworm is a detritivore — it fragments detritus but does not mineralise it; bacteria and fungi secrete enzymes and release the nutrients. Correct principle: detritivores shred, microbes digest.
• Mistake 5: Swapping humification and mineralisation. Why it is wrong: humification accumulates humus, highly resistant to microbes; mineralisation is its later degradation, releasing inorganic nutrients. Correct principle: humification banks nutrients, mineralisation spends them.
• Mistake 6: Saying energy is recycled like nutrients. Why it is wrong: energy flows one way and is dissipated as heat at every transfer; nothing gathers it back. Only matter cycles. Correct principle: energy flows, nutrients cycle; an option reversing the pair is wrong.
• Mistake 7: Concluding that the inverted sea biomass pyramid violates the ten per cent law, or that an energy pyramid could invert. Why it is wrong: the sea pyramid graphs standing crop at an instant — small, fast-turnover phytoplankton sustain a larger fish stock — while the law and the energy pyramid concern flow per unit time, always decreasing. Correct principle: stock can invert; flow cannot.
• Mistake 8: Treating standing crop and productivity as one quantity. Why it is wrong: standing crop is living mass at a level at a particular time (a stock); productivity is a rate per unit area per unit time (a flow). Correct principle: a per-year in the unit means productivity; a one-time weighing means standing crop.
• Mistake 9: Assigning 71 per cent of global carbon to the atmosphere. Why it is wrong: 71 per cent is dissolved in the oceans; the atmosphere holds only about 1 per cent. Correct principle: the ocean is the vault (71 per cent), the air a corridor (about 1 per cent).
• Mistake 10: Naming mosses as xerarch pioneers, or a water community as the hydrarch climax. Why it is wrong: lichens pioneer bare rock (their acids start soil formation; mosses follow); hydrarch ends with forest on land, the water body filled in. Correct principle: rock — lichens first; water — phytoplankton first; both — forest last.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging

• Example 1 — Problem: A forest plot has GPP of 2400 g m-2 yr-1, and its producers respire away 40 per cent of what they fix. Find the NPP and state what it means.
• Given: GPP = 2400 g m-2 yr-1; R = 40 per cent of GPP.
• Principle: NPP = GPP − R, and NPP is the biomass available to heterotrophs.
• Steps: R = 0.40 × 2400 = 960. NPP = 2400 − 960 = 1440 g m-2 yr-1.
• Answer: NPP = 1440 g m-2 yr-1.
• Reasoning: Producers keep 960 for their own metabolism; only the 1440 surplus feeds herbivores and decomposers — consumer calculations start from 1440, not 2400.
• Mistake to avoid: Reporting 960 as NPP — that is R. If your NPP exceeds GPP, the subtraction went the wrong way.
• Example 2 — Problem: Incident solar radiation on a grassland is 500,000 kcal m-2 yr-1; 45 per cent is PAR, and grasses capture 5 per cent of the PAR. Estimate the energy entering gross primary production.
• Given: Incident = 500,000; PAR fraction 0.45 (under NCERT's 50 per cent); capture 0.05 of PAR (within the 2 to 10 per cent).
• Principle: Apply the two filters in sequence: captured = incident × PAR fraction × capture fraction.
• Steps: PAR = 500,000 × 0.45 = 225,000. Captured = 225,000 × 0.05 = 11,250 kcal m-2 yr-1.
• Answer: About 11,250 kcal m-2 yr-1 enters gross production.
• Reasoning: Only 2.25 per cent of the original radiation was fixed — the double filter in action.
• Mistake to avoid: Applying 5 per cent directly to 500,000 (25,000) — that skips the PAR filter.
• Example 3 — Problem: In the chain grass → grasshopper → frog → snake, the grass level incorporates 80,000 kcal. Using the ten per cent law, how much energy reaches the snake?
• Given: Producer energy = 80,000 kcal; three transfers lead to the snake (T4).
• Principle: Each transfer passes on 10 per cent — multiply producer energy by 0.1 once per transfer.
• Steps: Grasshopper (T2) = 8,000. Frog (T3) = 800. Snake (T4) = 80 kcal.
• Answer: 80 kcal is available to the snake.
• Reasoning: Three 90-per-cent tolls leave one-thousandth of the original energy — why food chains stay short and top carnivores stay scarce.
• Mistake to avoid: Counting four transfers by counting organisms — producers to snake spans three arrows: 0.1 cubed.
• Example 4 — Problem: Biosphere NPP is about 170 billion tons (dry weight); oceans contribute 55 billion tons from about 70 per cent of the surface. Find the land contribution and each realm's share of NPP.
• Given: Total = 170; ocean = 55; ocean area share 70 per cent, land 30 per cent.
• Principle: Land NPP = total − ocean; shares are parts divided by the whole.
• Steps: Land = 170 − 55 = 115 billion tons. Ocean share = 55/170, about 32 per cent. Land share = 115/170, about 68 per cent.
• Answer: Land contributes 115 billion tons — about 68 per cent of world NPP from 30 per cent of the surface.
• Reasoning: Area and productivity are decoupled — per unit area, land out-produces oceans nearly fivefold, because most ocean volume is dark and nutrient-poor.
• Mistake to avoid: Assigning 115 billion tons to the oceans because they are bigger — the NCERT split is 55 ocean, 115 land.
• Example 5 — Problem: In a sea sample, phytoplankton standing crop is 5 g m-2 while fishes feeding (through zooplankton) on them stand at 15 g m-2. Describe the biomass pyramid, and explain why it does not contradict the rule that energy decreases at successive levels.
• Given: Standing crops at one instant: producers 5, consumers 15 g m-2 — no time term.
• Principle: The biomass pyramid graphs standing crop (a stock); the energy rule graphs flow (a rate per unit time) — fast producer turnover can sustain a larger consumer stock.
• Steps: The producer bar (5) is narrower than the consumer bar (15): the biomass pyramid is inverted. But phytoplankton reproduce and are eaten continuously, so their productivity still exceeds the consumers' — the energy pyramid stays upright.
• Answer: Inverted biomass pyramid; no contradiction — stock and flow are different quantities.
• Reasoning: A small shop with fast restocking can outsell a large warehouse — inventory at an instant is not sales per day.
• Mistake to avoid: Citing the ten per cent law against the data — it constrains energy transfer, not standing-biomass ratios at one moment.
• Example 6 — Problem: Two detritus samples sit in identical warm, moist, aerated chambers: A is fruit pulp rich in sugars and nitrogen; B is woody twigs rich in lignin and chitin. Which decomposes faster, and what happens to both rates in a freezer?
• Given: Same climate; different chemistry — A nitrogen/sugar-rich, B lignin/chitin-rich.
• Principle: Detritus chemistry controls rate (lignin/chitin slow; nitrogen/sugars speed); temperature controls microbial activity.
• Steps: Warm and moist — A faster: its sugars and nitrogen feed microbes directly, while B's lignin and chitin resist enzymes. Frozen — both slow sharply: low temperature suppresses soil microbes and organic matter builds up.
• Answer: A faster than B when warm; both slow when frozen.
• Reasoning: The design separates the controllers — with climate equal, chemistry decides; with chemistry equal, temperature decides.
• Mistake to avoid: Blaming B's slowness on climate — the chambers share one climate, so the difference must be compositional.
• Example 7 — Problem: Three undisturbed sites: (i) a newly created pond, (ii) a fresh bare lava flow, (iii) an abandoned farm field. For each, name the succession type, the pioneers and the climax.
• Given: Starting substrates — open water; bare lifeless rock; disturbed land with soil intact.
• Principle: Water start = hydrarch; bare rock = xerarch (both primary, converging on mesic forest); disturbed but soiled = secondary, much faster.
• Steps: Pond — hydrarch; pioneers phytoplankton; stages: submerged, floating, reed-swamp, marsh-meadow, scrub, trees; climax forest. Lava — xerarch; pioneers lichens; then mosses, herbs, shrubs; climax forest. Field — secondary; grasses and herbs, shrubs, trees; climax forest, fastest.
• Answer: Hydrarch (phytoplankton → forest); xerarch (lichens → forest); secondary succession (grasses → forest, fastest).
• Reasoning: The starting substrate selects the series — plankton in water, lichens on rock, weeds on ready soil — and all three roads run to mesic forest.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling the field's regrowth primary succession — surviving soil is what makes it secondary and fast.

• A5. Biomass inverted: at an instant, fish biomass exceeds the phytoplankton standing crop, because tiny fast-reproducing phytoplankton are cropped as fast as they grow — a small stock with huge turnover. • Summary: An ecosystem is the functional unit of a biotic community interacting with its abiotic environment, structured by species composition and stratification, running four processes — productivity, decomposition, energy flow, nutrient cycling — all visible in the pond.
• Summary: Productivity obeys NPP = GPP − R and one ledger: about 170 billion tons of NPP a year, 55 from oceans, 115 from land, consumers adding organic matter far more slowly (secondary productivity). Decomposition returns the dead in five steps — fragmentation, leaching, catabolism, humification, mineralisation — paced by chemistry, temperature and moisture.
• Summary: Energy flows one way through the PAR bottleneck (under half of sunlight is PAR; plants capture 2 to 10 per cent of it) and down the trophic ladder under the ten per cent law, shaping the pyramids — upright in most ecosystems, inverted for sea biomass, never for energy. Succession rebuilds communities from pioneers to a mesic forest climax.
• Summary: Nutrients cycle while energy flows: carbon through its ocean vault (71 per cent in oceans, about 1 per cent in air, nearly 4 × 10^13 kg fixed yearly), phosphorus through rock and soil with no gaseous phase. Services are worth about 33 trillion US dollars a year, nearly twice global GNP, soil formation about half.
