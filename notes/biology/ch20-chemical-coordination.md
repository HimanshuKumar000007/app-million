Introduction — Messengers That Travel by Blood
• A nerve impulse reaches one cell and vanishes in a millisecond; a hormone reaches a million cells and acts for hours. Chemical coordination is the body's slow broadcast control system — and only cells with the matching receptor respond.
• DEFINITION — Hormones are non-nutrient chemicals that act as intercellular messengers and are produced in trace amounts. Three ideas in one NCERT sentence: a hormone is not food, it carries information rather than energy, and it works in extremely small quantities.
• WHY the body needs a second control system — nerves are point-to-point and short-lived, ideal for reflexes, but growth, metabolism, blood pressure, blood sugar and reproduction need continuous, lifelong regulation. Blood-borne hormones are built for that: slow to arrive, long to act, reaching every tissue.
• EXAMPLE — One event, two systems: a sudden fright. The nervous system reacts in a split second (you jump), but adrenal adrenaline, poured into the blood within seconds, keeps your heart pounding and liver releasing glucose for minutes. Nerves start the response; hormones sustain it.
• WORKED PATTERN — Every section of this chapter answers four questions about a gland: where is it, what does it secrete, what does that hormone do, and what breaks when there is too much or too little? Learn each gland as a row of that table and the chapter becomes one structured list.
• YOUR TURN — A substance travels in blood, acts on distant organs in trace amounts, but is also a nutrient used as fuel. Is it a hormone? Answer: no — hormones are non-nutrient messengers; a fuel molecule like glucose fails the first word of the definition.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The chapter in one breath: hypothalamus commands, pituitary relays, and thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, adrenals, pancreas and gonads execute — while heart, kidney and gut add local messengers, and negative feedback keeps every level in range.

Endocrine Glands — Ductless Release, Distant Targets
• DEFINITION — The classical endocrine glands are ductless: they have no ducts of their own, so their secretions — the hormones — are poured directly into the blood, which distributes the message. Endocrine signalling is therefore also called chemical coordination and integration.
• DEFINITION — An exocrine gland is the opposite design: it keeps a duct and delivers its secretion onto a surface or into a cavity — salivary glands pour saliva into the mouth, sweat glands onto the skin, and the pancreas (in its exocrine role) pours digestive juice into the intestine.
• WHY ductless forces the blood route — with no delivery tube, the circulation is the only courier visiting every gland and every target. The price is loss of aim: the hormone reaches all tissues, so aim is recovered at the receiving end — only cells with that hormone's specific receptor respond.
• EXAMPLE — The organised endocrine glands are the pituitary, pineal, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, adrenal, pancreas and gonads. The heart, kidney and gut also produce hormones without being classical endocrine glands.
✔ KEY POINT — Organs outside the classical endocrine list — atrial wall of the heart, juxtaglomerular cells of the kidney, endocrine cells of the gut — still secrete true hormones (ANF, erythropoietin, gastrin, secretin, CCK, GIP). Not an endocrine gland does not mean makes no hormones.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sort glands into three boxes: endocrine (ductless, hormone into blood), exocrine (ducted, juice onto a surface), heterocrine or composite (both jobs in one organ — pancreas and gonads are the standard examples). Ask: duct or no duct, then: does it do a second job too?
• YOUR TURN — The liver pours bile through a duct and also releases substances into blood. Is it an endocrine gland in the NCERT scheme? Answer: no — the organised list is pituitary, pineal, thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, adrenal, pancreas and gonads.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The defining feature of an endocrine gland is absence of ducts with release into blood, not merely secreting a hormone. And the pancreas is the reverse trap: it is composite, so any option calling it purely endocrine or purely exocrine is wrong.

The Hypothalamus — Where the Brain Speaks Hormone
• DEFINITION — The hypothalamus is the basal part of the diencephalon (floor of the forebrain) and the supreme link between the nervous and endocrine systems. Groups of its nerve cells — neurosecretory nuclei — manufacture hormones, so the body's first endocrine gland is made of neurons.
• DEFINITION — Hypothalamic hormones are of two kinds. Releasing hormones stimulate the pituitary to synthesise and release its hormones; inhibiting hormones order it to stop. GnRH (gonadotrophin releasing hormone) is the named releasing example, driving the anterior pituitary to release the gonadotrophins LH and FSH.
• DEFINITION — Somatostatin is the named inhibiting example: from the hypothalamus, it inhibits the release of growth hormone from the pituitary. One gland, two opposite instructions — release more, release less — and the pituitary obeys the balance.
• WHY a portal system is needed — releasing and inhibiting hormones must reach the anterior pituitary concentrated, without diluting into the general circulation. A portal circulatory system, a short private blood route from hypothalamus to anterior pituitary, delivers them door to door.
• DEFINITION — The posterior pituitary is regulated differently: it is under direct neural regulation of the hypothalamus. Oxytocin and vasopressin are synthesised in the hypothalamus and transported axonally to be stored in and released from the posterior pituitary. The posterior lobe is a warehouse, not a factory.
✔ KEY POINT — Two routes, one commander: hypothalamus to anterior pituitary runs through blood (portal system, releasing and inhibiting hormones); hypothalamus to posterior pituitary runs through axons (hormones made above, stored below). Swapping the two routes is the most-tested confusion here.
• EXAMPLE — When the body needs more thyroid hormone, the chain starts here: hypothalamus signals, anterior pituitary answers with TSH, thyroid answers with thyroxine. Every long chain in this chapter begins with a hypothalamic neuron.
• WORKED PATTERN — For a posterior pituitary hormone, ask: made where, released from where? Made in the hypothalamus, released from the posterior pituitary. For an anterior pituitary hormone, ask: who ordered its release? A hypothalamic releasing or inhibiting hormone, arriving by portal blood.
• YOUR TURN — A tumour destroys the hypothalamic neurons producing GnRH; pituitary and gonads are normal. Predict the defect. Answer: no GnRH signal reaches the anterior pituitary, so LH and FSH fall, the gonads go unstimulated, and sex hormones and gamete maturation suffer.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The hypothalamus is part of the brain (diencephalon) that also acts as an endocrine gland. Options placing it inside the pituitary, or claiming the posterior pituitary synthesises oxytocin and ADH, are both wrong — synthesis is hypothalamic; storage and release are pituitary.

The Pituitary — The Master Gland and Its Two Lobes
• DEFINITION — The pituitary gland sits in a bony cavity called the sella turcica and hangs from the hypothalamus by a stalk. It is divided into the adenohypophysis and the neurohypophysis. The adenohypophysis has two parts: pars distalis (anterior pituitary) and pars intermedia. The neurohypophysis is the posterior pituitary.
• DEFINITION — The anterior pituitary (pars distalis) produces six hormones: growth hormone (GH), prolactin (PRL), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH), luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH). Four are tropic — TSH, ACTH, LH and FSH switch on other endocrine glands (thyroid, adrenal cortex, gonads) — hence master gland.
• DEFINITION — Growth hormone (GH) drives the growth of the body — the building of tissues and bones. Prolactin (PRL) regulates the growth of the mammary glands and the formation of milk in them. Neither is tropic: GH builds the body, PRL prepares it to feed the next generation.
• DEFINITION — LH and FSH are together the gonadotrophins. In males, LH stimulates synthesis and secretion of androgens from the testis, and FSH acts on Sertoli cells, stimulating factors that help spermiogenesis. In females, FSH grows the ovarian follicles, and LH induces ovulation of the mature Graafian follicle and maintains the corpus luteum.
• DEFINITION — The pars intermedia produces only one hormone: melanocyte stimulating hormone (MSH), which acts on melanocytes and regulates pigmentation of the skin. In humans the pars intermedia is almost merged with the pars distalis, so MSH's role in us is minor — a fact NCERT states and examiners quote.
• DEFINITION — The posterior pituitary stores and releases two hormones it did not make: oxytocin and vasopressin. Oxytocin acts on smooth muscles: vigorous uterine contraction at child birth, and milk ejection. Vasopressin acts mainly at the kidneys, stimulating resorption of water and electrolytes by the distal tubules — hence its other name, ADH (anti-diuretic hormone).
✔ KEY POINT — Division of labour in one line: the anterior lobe manufactures six hormones of its own; the intermediate lobe makes one (MSH); the posterior lobe manufactures none — it stores and releases two hypothalamic hormones, oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH).
• WHY master gland is only half the truth — the pituitary commands thyroid, adrenal cortex and gonads, but itself obeys the hypothalamus, through releasing and inhibiting hormones and direct neural control of its posterior lobe. The headquarters is the hypothalamus; the pituitary is the relay.
• EXAMPLE — Read tropic hormones as orders: TSH orders the thyroid, ACTH the adrenal cortex, LH and FSH the gonads. When a question names a tropic hormone, the real target is the gland inside its name.
• WORKED PATTERN — Solve pituitary questions lobe first, hormone second. Step 1: which lobe — anterior (GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH), intermediate (MSH), posterior (releases oxytocin, ADH)? Step 2: what does that hormone do? Most wrong options place a correct hormone in the wrong lobe.
• YOUR TURN — The pituitary stalk is cut, so portal blood cannot reach the anterior pituitary, though the gland is alive. What suffers first? Answer: the tropic hormones — TSH, ACTH, LH and FSH fall without their releasing signals, so thyroid, adrenal cortex and gonads slow together.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Oxytocin and vasopressin are released from the posterior pituitary but synthesised in the hypothalamus. And ADH and vasopressin are two names for one hormone, not two — milk ejection belongs to oxytocin, water resorption to vasopressin; examiners happily swap the pair.

Pituitary Disorders — When the Growth Signal Misbehaves
• DEFINITION — Low secretion of GH from early age — during childhood — stops the growth of the body: bones and tissues do not lengthen and build on schedule. This stunted growth is called pituitary dwarfism. The gland is underactive, the signal too weak, the body stays small.
• DEFINITION — Over-secretion of GH paints the mirror image. Excess GH in childhood causes gigantism — the whole body grows abnormally large. The same excess in adults, especially middle age, causes acromegaly: fused bones cannot lengthen, so they thicken instead, producing severe disfigurement, especially of the face.
• WHY age decides which disease excess GH causes — long bones lengthen only while their growth zones are active. Before fusion, extra GH means extra length everywhere (gigantism). After fusion, length is locked, so the hormone can only add thickness to bones that still remodel — face, hands, feet (acromegaly).
✔ KEY POINT — The GH trio in one breath: childhood deficiency is dwarfism, childhood excess is gigantism, adult excess is acromegaly. NCERT adds that acromegaly is hard to diagnose early and, unchecked, can lead to serious complications and death.
• DEFINITION — Diabetes insipidus is this chapter's posterior-pituitary disorder: a deficiency of ADH — failure of its synthesis or release — reduces the kidney's ability to conserve water. The tubules resorb too little water, so large volumes are lost in urine and dehydration follows, with blood sugar completely normal.
• WHY two different diabetes share a name — diabetes names the symptom of passing too much urine. In insipidus the urine is copious because water cannot be reclaimed without ADH; in mellitus it is copious because excess glucose drags water out with it. Same symptom, opposite ends of the endocrine system.
• EXAMPLE — A middle-aged man notices his jaw, hands and shoe size growing and his features coarsening over years. His skeleton can no longer lengthen, so GH excess is expressing itself as thickness — acromegaly, not gigantism, purely because of his age.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any GH question, decide in order: too little or too much? Then: child or adult? Child plus too little: dwarfism. Child plus too much: gigantism. Adult plus too much: acromegaly. Adult GH deficiency has no famous NCERT disorder — if an option invents one, distrust it.
• YOUR TURN — Two brothers develop excess GH secretion, one at age 9, one at 39. Name each condition. Answer: the 9-year-old develops gigantism (bones still lengthening); the 39-year-old develops acromegaly (bones can only thicken).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Diabetes insipidus is an ADH disorder, not an insulin disorder, and its urine carries no excess glucose. Any option linking insipidus to the pancreas, insulin or blood sugar has confused the two diabetes — the trap this chapter sets most often.

The Pineal Gland — Melatonin and the Daily Rhythm
• DEFINITION — The pineal gland is a small gland located on the dorsal side of the forebrain. It secretes one famous hormone: melatonin. Small gland, one hormone, one big job — telling the body what time it is.
• DEFINITION — Melatonin regulates the 24-hour (diurnal) rhythm of the body. It helps maintain the normal rhythm of the sleep-wake cycle and of body temperature: secretion rises in darkness and falls in light, so the hormone is effectively a chemical signal of night.
• WHY a hormone for timekeeping — body processes are not meant to run flat-out around the clock: sleep, temperature, alertness and hormone releases follow a daily cycle. Light information reaches the pineal, and melatonin converts the light-dark pattern into a blood-borne instruction every tissue can read.
✔ KEY POINT — NCERT gives melatonin four further influence zones: it also influences metabolism, pigmentation, the menstrual cycle and the defence capability (immunity) of the body. A question asking what melatonin influences can legitimately include any of the five — rhythm plus these four.
• EXAMPLE — Jet lag is pineal physiology in action: after a long flight your melatonin cycle is still set to home time, so you feel sleepy at noon and awake at midnight in the new city, until light resets the rhythm.
• WORKED PATTERN — Pineal questions are one-fact questions in disguise: dorsal side of forebrain; hormone melatonin; theme rhythm. If a stem mentions sleep-wake cycle, body temperature rhythm or diurnal variation, route straight to pineal and melatonin before reading the options.
• YOUR TURN — A night-shift worker sleeps in a brightly lit room by day and finds her sleep shallow. Connect this to the pineal. Answer: light suppresses melatonin secretion; low melatonin during her rest hours disturbs the normal sleep-wake rhythm the hormone maintains.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Two forebrain endocrine landmarks, worth learning as a pair: hypothalamus at the base (ventral floor) and pineal on the dorsal side. Ventral equals hypothalamus and command; dorsal equals pineal and rhythm.

The Thyroid — The Metabolic Thermostat
• DEFINITION — The thyroid gland is composed of two lobes on either side of the trachea, joined by a thin flap of connective tissue called the isthmus. It is built of follicles and stromal tissue; the follicular cells synthesise thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Both are iodothyronines — built with iodine — and iodine is essential for their synthesis.
• WHY T4 and T3 carry those numbers — the number counts the iodine atoms in the molecule: T4 carries four, T3 carries three. A diet short of iodine cannot be compensated by eating more of anything else — the raw material of the hormone itself is missing.
• DEFINITION — Thyroid hormones regulate the basal metabolic rate (BMR), the resting body's energy burn rate. They support red blood cell formation, control metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins and fats, and influence water and electrolyte balance. Nearly every cell owns a thyroid receptor, so the thermostat sets the idle speed of the whole body.
• DEFINITION — The thyroid also secretes a protein hormone, thyrocalcitonin (TCT), made by its parafollicular cells, which lowers the calcium level of the blood. The division matters: follicular cells make T3 and T4 for metabolism; parafollicular cells make TCT for calcium. One gland, two product lines.
✔ KEY POINT — Thyroid output in one line: T4 and T3 set the metabolic rate and support RBC formation and carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism, while thyrocalcitonin (TCT) pulls blood calcium down. Metabolism versus calcium is the sorting test in every thyroid question.
• EXAMPLE — An overactive thyroid makes a person feel hot in a cool room, lose weight despite eating well and develop a racing pulse: BMR is set too high. An underactive thyroid gives the photographic negative — cold intolerance, weight gain, slowness.
• WORKED PATTERN — Trace any thyroid effect back to BMR. Increased heart rate, weight loss, heat intolerance: metabolic rate up. Low energy, weight gain, slow pulse: metabolic rate down. The symptoms are never random; they are the thermostat reading translated into the body.
• YOUR TURN — Why does the thyroid enlarge in iodine-deficient regions when it is the hormone, not the gland, that is lacking? Answer: less iodine means less T3 and T4; the pituitary answers falling thyroxine with more TSH, and constant TSH drive makes the gland grow — straining for a missing raw material.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — TCT belongs to the thyroid but works on calcium, not metabolism, and it lowers blood calcium — the exact opposite of parathyroid hormone. Options assigning calcium-raising to the thyroid or metabolism-setting to TCT have swapped the gland's two product lines.

Thyroid Disorders — When the Thermostat Breaks
• DEFINITION — Hypothyroidism is the underactive state: too little T3 and T4. Its commonest cause worldwide is iodine deficiency in the diet, which also enlarges the thyroid gland — the condition called simple goitre. Note the paradox: the gland is big, but its output is small.
• DEFINITION — Cretinism is hypothyroidism striking during pregnancy and early development: defective development of the growing baby leads to stunted growth, mental retardation, low intelligence quotient, abnormal skin and deaf-mutism. Thyroid hormones build the brain and skeleton, so absence during construction leaves permanent defects.
• DEFINITION — In adults, hypothyroidism presents as myxoedema, also called Gull's disease: weight gain, puffiness, slowness, cold intolerance and low metabolic rate. In adult women it may also make the menstrual cycle irregular. Same deficiency as cretinism, but arriving after the body is built, so it disturbs function rather than development.
• DEFINITION — Hyperthyroidism is the overactive state, and its named NCERT form is exophthalmic goitre, also called Graves' disease: thyroid enlargement, protrusion of the eyeballs, increased BMR and weight loss. It arises when the gland secretes excess hormone, due to cancer or nodules developing in it.
✔ KEY POINT — The disorder map is a two-by-two: too little hormone in a developing child is cretinism; too little in an adult is myxoedema (Gull's disease); iodine deficiency with enlargement is simple goitre; too much hormone with protruding eyeballs is exophthalmic goitre (Graves' disease).
• WHY the eyeballs protrude in Graves' disease — tissues behind the eyes swell as part of the same overactive process driving the gland, pushing the eyeballs forward. Exophthalmos is the visible signature separating Graves' disease from every other thyroid disorder in a question stem.
• EXAMPLE — Two patients, two enlargements: a woman from an iodine-poor district has a goitre, gains weight and feels the cold — deficiency, simple goitre. A man has bulging eyes, trembling hands and weight loss — excess with exophthalmos, Graves' disease. The necks look similar; the hormone levels are opposite.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sort thyroid cases by two questions. First: fast symptoms (weight loss, heat, racing heart) or slow (weight gain, cold, lethargy)? Fast means excess, slow means deficiency. Second: child or adult — and are the eyes protruding? Child plus deficiency: cretinism. Adult plus deficiency: myxoedema. Excess plus protruding eyes: Graves'.
• YOUR TURN — A newborn in an iodine-deficient region grows up untreated with stunted growth, low IQ and deaf-mutism. Why can thyroxine at age 20 not reverse this? Answer: the damage is developmental (cretinism) — hormone absent while brain and skeleton were built; later replacement restores metabolic rate but cannot rebuild closed construction windows.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Goitre means enlargement, not overactivity: simple goitre is a deficiency disease with an enlarged gland, while Graves' couples enlargement with excess. And cretinism is not merely childhood myxoedema: cretinism is the pregnancy-and-development condition, myxoedema the adult one.

The Parathyroid — Four Tiny Guards of Blood Calcium
• DEFINITION — The parathyroid glands are four small glands present on the back side of the thyroid gland, one pair embedded in each of its two lobes. They secrete the peptide hormone parathyroid hormone (PTH). Position is the trap detail: behind the thyroid, but separate glands with a separate hormone and the opposite calcium job.
• DEFINITION — PTH is the hypercalcemic hormone: it increases the calcium level of the blood, and its secretion is regulated by the circulating calcium level itself — calcium falls, PTH is released; calcium rises, PTH switches off. The controlled variable controls the gland: a feedback loop in miniature.
• DEFINITION — PTH raises blood calcium by three routes. It acts on bones, stimulating resorption — dissolution of bone mineral — releasing calcium into blood. It stimulates reabsorption of calcium by the renal tubules, so less escapes in urine. And it increases calcium absorption from digested food. Bone, kidney, gut: one hormone emptying every store into the blood.
✔ KEY POINT — Calcium balance is a two-hormone seesaw: PTH (parathyroid) raises blood calcium and is hypercalcemic; thyrocalcitonin, TCT (thyroid parafollicular cells), lowers it. NCERT states that PTH and TCT together play a significant role in maintaining calcium balance in the body.
• WHY blood calcium is guarded so tightly — calcium ions run nerve conduction, muscle contraction and blood clotting minute by minute, while the diet supplies calcium irregularly. The reservoir is bone, holding nearly all body calcium, and PTH is the hormone authorised to withdraw from that bank.
• EXAMPLE — A patient whose parathyroids were damaged during thyroid surgery develops cramps and tingling as blood calcium falls: with less PTH, bone resorption slows, the kidney wastes more calcium and the gut absorbs less — all three routes weakened at once.
• WORKED PATTERN — Calcium questions are direction questions. Raises calcium: PTH. Lowers calcium: TCT. Organ releasing calcium under PTH: bone. Organ conserving it under PTH: kidney. Organ absorbing more dietary calcium under PTH: intestine. Fix the arrows first; the options then sort themselves.
• YOUR TURN — Blood calcium rises above normal after a calcium-rich meal. Which hormone's secretion should increase, and which should decrease? Answer: thyrocalcitonin (TCT) increases to pull calcium down, and PTH secretion falls — high circulating calcium switches PTH off.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — PTH increases blood calcium; students regularly reverse it because the gland sounds secondary. Anchor by logic: PTH is secreted when calcium is LOW, so everything it does must RAISE calcium. And TCT comes from the thyroid, not the parathyroid, despite both names circling calcium.

The Thymus — Training School of the Immune System
• DEFINITION — The thymus is a lobular gland located between the lungs, behind the sternum and on the ventral side of the aorta. It plays a major role in the development of the immune system, and it is as much an immune organ as an endocrine gland.
• DEFINITION — The thymus secretes peptide hormones called thymosins. They play a major role in differentiating T-lymphocytes — the cells of cell-mediated immunity — and also promote antibody production, supporting humoral immunity. One gland trains both arms of immunity.
• WHY T-lymphocytes are named after this gland — the T in T-lymphocyte stands for thymus: these lymphocytes mature and differentiate under thymosin instruction inside the thymus before taking up defence posts elsewhere. Thymosins assist even the other arm by promoting antibody production.
✔ KEY POINT — The life-story fact the exam loves: the thymus degenerates as age advances. In old individuals it has largely shrunk, thymosin production decreases, and immune responses become weak. Childhood gland large and active; old-age gland degenerated and quiet.
• EXAMPLE — This is part of why elderly people catch infections more easily, recover more slowly, and respond more weakly to vaccines: the training school of their T-lymphocytes has been winding down for decades.
• WORKED PATTERN — Thymus questions reduce to three linked facts: location (between the lungs, behind the sternum), hormone (thymosins), function (T-lymphocyte differentiation, plus promotion of antibody production). A stem mentioning ageing and weak immunity hides thymus degeneration in the middle.
• YOUR TURN — A child's thymus fails to develop from birth. Which defence suffers most, and which is also weakened? Answer: cell-mediated immunity suffers most — T-lymphocytes cannot differentiate without thymosins; humoral immunity is also weakened, since thymosins promote antibody production.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The location trap-cluster, learned as one map: pineal is dorsal side of forebrain, thymus is behind the sternum between the lungs, parathyroids are on the back of the thyroid, adrenals sit at the anterior part of each kidney.

The Adrenal Medulla — Emergency Hormones, the 3F Response
• DEFINITION — The adrenal glands are a pair, one sitting at the anterior part of each kidney like a cap. Each gland has two tissues: the centrally located adrenal medulla and the outer adrenal cortex. Two glands in one shell, with different hormones, different triggers and different speeds.
• DEFINITION — The medulla secretes two catecholamine hormones: adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine). They are commonly called the emergency hormones, or hormones of fight or flight, because they are secreted rapidly in response to stress and emergency situations.
• DEFINITION — The emergency package they deliver: increased alertness, dilation of the pupils, piloerection (raising of body hairs), sweating, stronger heart beat, faster respiration, and stimulation of glycogen breakdown, raising blood glucose. They also stimulate breakdown of lipids and proteins — every stored fuel mobilised at once.
✔ KEY POINT — The 3F shorthand — fight, flight, fright — captures the medulla's personality: fast (seconds), short-lived and whole-body, preparing muscles, senses, heart, lungs and fuel supply for immediate action. Never answer cortisol for an emergency question — cortisol is the cortex's slower hormone.
• WHY the medulla is fast while the cortex is slow — the medulla is triggered almost directly by the nervous system and dumps pre-made catecholamines into the blood. The cortex answers to ACTH from the pituitary and manages minutes-to-days problems like salt balance. Same gland, two gears.
• EXAMPLE — A snake crosses your path: before you have decided anything, your heart hammers, palms sweat, hair rises, pupils widen and your liver converts glycogen to glucose. That inventory is adrenaline and noradrenaline at work — NCERT's list, felt from inside.
• WORKED PATTERN — Emergency-symptom questions match against the NCERT list: alertness, pupil dilation, piloerection, sweating, stronger faster heart beat, faster respiration, glycogen breakdown raising glucose, breakdown of lipids and proteins. A listed symptom plus acute stress means adrenal medulla.
• YOUR TURN — At a surprise test announcement, a seated student's heart rate jumps within seconds and her hands sweat. Which hormones, from which tissue, triggered by what? Answer: adrenaline and noradrenaline from the adrenal medulla, released in response to stress — fight-or-flight firing with no physical fight at all.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Adrenaline's fuel effect is glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis), raising blood glucose — the opposite of insulin. And the synonyms are pairs, not four hormones: epinephrine is adrenaline, norepinephrine is noradrenaline.

The Adrenal Cortex — Three Layers, Three Hormone Families
• DEFINITION — The adrenal cortex is divided into three layers: the inner zona reticularis, the middle zona fasciculata and the outer zona glomerulosa. It secretes steroid hormones collectively called corticoids, each layer specialising — outside to inside: glomerulosa, fasciculata, reticularis, G, F, R.
• DEFINITION — The zona glomerulosa (outermost) makes the mineralocorticoids, chiefly aldosterone. Aldosterone acts mainly at the renal tubules, stimulating reabsorption of sodium and water and excretion of potassium and phosphate ions. Holding sodium and water maintains electrolytes, fluid volume, osmotic pressure and blood pressure.
• DEFINITION — The zona fasciculata (middle and thickest) makes the glucocorticoids, chiefly cortisol. Cortisol handles carbohydrate metabolism: it stimulates gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and proteolysis, and inhibits cellular uptake of amino acids. It also maintains the cardiovascular system and kidney functions, is anti-inflammatory, suppresses the immune response, and stimulates RBC production.
• DEFINITION — The zona reticularis (innermost) secretes a small amount of androgenic steroids — adrenal androgens — which play a role in the growth of axial hair, pubic hair and facial hair during puberty. The same family the testis makes in bulk; the adrenal share is minor but a named NCERT point.
✔ KEY POINT — The layer-product map: glomerulosa to mineralocorticoids (aldosterone), salt and water; fasciculata to glucocorticoids (cortisol), glucose and stress metabolism; reticularis to androgens, pubertal hair. Mnemonic: salt, sugar, sex — outer to inner.
• DEFINITION — Addison's disease is the cortex's underproduction state: underproduction alters carbohydrate metabolism, causing acute weakness and fatigue. NCERT names exactly this pairing.
• DEFINITION — Cushing's syndrome is the mirror outline: too much cortisol over a long period — blood sugar stays high, protein breakdown wastes muscle, fat redistributes to the face and trunk, and blood pressure rises. Addison's is the cortex gone quiet; Cushing's is cortisol that will not switch off.
• EXAMPLE — A trekker loses salt in sweat for days. Her cortex answers with aldosterone: renal tubules reclaim more sodium and water, urine volume shrinks, blood pressure holds. No emergency, no adrenaline — slow housekeeping in the background.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any adrenal question, choose the tissue first: sudden stress — medulla (adrenaline, noradrenaline); salt, water or pressure — glomerulosa (aldosterone); glucose metabolism or immune suppression — fasciculata (cortisol); pubertal hair — reticularis (androgens). Tissue first, hormone second.
• YOUR TURN — A patient on cortisol-like medicine for months develops frequent infections. Side effect or surprise? Answer: it is cortisol's own NCERT action — glucocorticoids suppress the immune response; long use trades inflammation control for lowered defence.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The medulla is inside and the cortex outside — students invert them. The kidney runs the same way (cortex outside, medulla inside). And ACTH orders only the cortex; the medulla takes its trigger from the nervous system, not the pituitary.

The Pancreas — Two Glands in One, One Sugar Seesaw
• DEFINITION — The pancreas is a composite (heterocrine) gland, both exocrine and endocrine. The exocrine part pours digestive juice through a duct; the endocrine part is scattered inside it as the islets of Langerhans: about 1 to 2 million islets, yet only 1 to 2 percent of the tissue. A tiny fraction of the organ runs the body's glucose economy.
• DEFINITION — The two main islet cell types are the alpha cells, secreting glucagon, and the beta cells, secreting insulin. Both are peptides and functional opposites: glucagon hyperglycemic (raises blood glucose), insulin hypoglycemic (lowers it). The chapter's seesaw has exactly two seats.
• DEFINITION — Glucagon acts mainly on liver cells (hepatocytes), stimulating glycogenolysis — breakdown of glycogen — and raising blood sugar. It also stimulates gluconeogenesis, synthesis of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources. Both routes push glucose into the blood: hence hyperglycemic.
• DEFINITION — Insulin plays the major role in the regulation of glucose homeostasis. It acts mainly on hepatocytes and adipocytes (fat cells), enhancing cellular uptake and utilisation of glucose, so glucose moves rapidly from blood into cells and the level falls. Insulin also stimulates storage of glucose as glycogen (glycogenesis) in the target cells.
✔ KEY POINT — The seesaw in one line: glucose high, beta-cell insulin pushes it into cells and glycogen; glucose low, alpha-cell glucagon orders the liver to release it. Glucagon releases glucose, insulin puts it in.
• DEFINITION — Diabetes mellitus is the insulin-deficiency disease: prolonged hyperglycemia. It brings loss of glucose through urine and formation of harmful ketone bodies, and is treated with insulin therapy. The defect is too little insulin action, and urine carries glucose because the blood carries too much.
• WHY glucose appears in urine in diabetes mellitus — the kidney filters blood and normally reclaims every glucose molecule, but reclamation has a capacity limit. When insulin fails and blood glucose climbs far above normal, the filtered load exceeds tubular capacity; the surplus leaves in urine, dragging water with it — hence the large urine volume and thirst.
• EXAMPLE — After a large sweet meal, a healthy person's glucose rises, beta cells release insulin, liver and fat cells store glycogen, and within a couple of hours the level is normal again. In an untreated diabetic the same peak stays high for hours and spills into urine.
• WORKED PATTERN — Pancreas questions are seesaw questions: decide which way glucose must move, then pick the hormone — fall: insulin (beta cells); rise, especially fasting: glucagon (alpha cells). Targets: insulin on hepatocytes and adipocytes; glucagon mainly on hepatocytes.
• YOUR TURN — A fasting person's blood glucose begins to dip. Which islet cells respond, with which hormone, acting where? Answer: alpha cells release glucagon, acting mainly on hepatocytes for glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, raising blood glucose towards normal.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Diabetes mellitus (insulin, glucose in urine, high blood sugar) versus diabetes insipidus (ADH, no glucose in urine, normal blood sugar) is the chapter's master trap. Second trap: glycogenesis (building glycogen, insulin) versus glycogenolysis (breaking glycogen, glucagon) — genesis builds, lysis breaks.

The Gonads — Testis and Ovary as Endocrine Glands
• DEFINITION — The testis is a dual organ: a primary sex organ producing sperm, and an endocrine gland producing hormones. The pair sits in the scrotal sac outside the abdominal cavity; each testis is composed of seminiferous tubules and stromal (interstitial) tissue. The hormone cells are the Leydig cells, in the intertubular spaces, producing androgens, mainly testosterone.
• DEFINITION — Androgens regulate the development, maturation and functions of the male accessory sex organs — epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate gland, urethra. They stimulate muscular growth, facial and axillary hair, aggressiveness and low-pitched voice, play a major role in spermatogenesis and libido, and produce anabolic effects on protein and carbohydrate metabolism.
• WHY the pituitary link matters here — LH stimulates the Leydig cells to secrete androgens, while FSH works on Sertoli cells inside the tubules for spermiogenesis. The testis is not autonomous: LH to the hormone cells, FSH to the sperm apparatus.
• DEFINITION — The ovary is likewise dual: it produces the ovum and two groups of steroid hormones, estrogen and progesterone. It is composed of ovarian follicles and stromal tissues. Estrogen is secreted mainly by the growing ovarian follicles; progesterone mainly by the corpus luteum, formed from the ruptured follicle after ovulation.
• DEFINITION — Estrogens stimulate the growth and activities of the female secondary sex organs, development of growing ovarian follicles, the appearance of female secondary sexual characters (high pitch of voice) and the development of the mammary glands. Estrogens also regulate female sexual behaviour.
• DEFINITION — Progesterone mainly supports pregnancy — it is the hormone of gestation, named for it. It also acts on the mammary glands, stimulating formation of alveoli — the sac-like structures storing milk — and milk secretion. Estrogen builds and cycles the system; progesterone maintains pregnancy and prepares the breast for feeding.
✔ KEY POINT — Gonad products in one line: testis (Leydig cells) makes androgens, mainly testosterone — male organs, muscle, hair, deep voice, spermatogenesis, libido; ovary (follicles) makes estrogen — female organs, secondary characters, mammary development; ovary (corpus luteum) makes progesterone — pregnancy support, alveoli and milk secretion.
• EXAMPLE — Puberty is the gonads switching on under pituitary orders: rising LH and FSH wake the testis and ovary, testosterone and estrogen climb, and secondary sexual characters appear — voice change and facial hair in boys, mammary development and high-pitched voice in girls. Every puberty fact is this section happening on schedule.
• WORKED PATTERN — Gonad questions name an effect and ask the source. Three-step matching — effect to hormone, hormone to tissue (Leydig cells, growing follicle, corpus luteum), tissue to pituitary commander (LH for Leydig and corpus luteum, FSH for follicles) — answers nearly all of them.
• YOUR TURN — After ovulation, which structure secretes the hormone that maintains a possible pregnancy, and what is it? Answer: the corpus luteum secretes progesterone, which supports pregnancy and prepares the mammary alveoli for milk.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Leydig cells make testosterone but sit OUTSIDE the seminiferous tubules (intertubular spaces); Sertoli cells sit inside and respond to FSH. And estrogen comes mainly from the growing follicles — not the corpus luteum, which is progesterone's source. Both swaps are standard options.

Hormones from Heart, Kidney and the Gut
• DEFINITION — Not every hormone comes from a classical gland. The atrial wall of the heart secretes a peptide hormone, atrial natriuretic factor (ANF). When blood pressure increases, ANF is secreted and dilates blood vessels, reducing the pressure. The heart senses the pressure it generates and releases its own pressure-lowering signal.
• DEFINITION — The juxtaglomerular (JG) cells of the kidney produce erythropoietin, a peptide hormone stimulating erythropoiesis — formation of red blood cells. Filtering the blood, the kidney judges its oxygen-carrying state and orders more RBCs when needed. The same JG apparatus yields renin, the enzyme starting the pressure-raising angiotensin cascade.
• DEFINITION — Endocrine cells of the gastrointestinal tract secrete four peptide hormones. Gastrin acts on the gastric glands, stimulating hydrochloric acid and pepsinogen secretion. Secretin acts on the exocrine pancreas, stimulating water and bicarbonate secretion. CCK acts on pancreas and gall bladder, stimulating enzymes and bile. GIP (gastro inhibitory peptide) inhibits gastric secretion and motility.
✔ KEY POINT — The gut's four divide by target: gastrin wakes the stomach's own glands (acid and pepsinogen); secretin makes the pancreas pour out water and bicarbonate; CCK releases pancreatic enzymes and bile; GIP switches the stomach down. Gastrin excites, GIP inhibits — the G pair is the tested contrast.
• WHY organs moonlight as glands — each is ideally placed to sense the variable it controls: the heart feels blood pressure in its own walls, the kidney filters the blood whose cell count it regulates, the gut meets the food whose digestion it coordinates. Sensor and signal live in the same tissue.
• EXAMPLE — A protein-rich meal reaches the stomach: gastrin rises, glands secrete acid and pepsinogen; as acidic food enters the intestine, secretin and CCK organise bicarbonate, enzymes and bile, while GIP slows the stomach. Four hormones run the meal like a shift schedule.
• WORKED PATTERN — Non-endocrine hormone questions are source-matching: ANF to heart atria, erythropoietin to kidney JG cells, gastrin-secretin-CCK-GIP to the GI tract. Learn them as organ-hormone pairs; the function follows the organ's job — pressure, RBC supply, digestion.
• YOUR TURN — At high altitude the air is thin and tissues receive less oxygen. Which non-endocrine hormone should rise, from where, with what result? Answer: erythropoietin from the juxtaglomerular cells of the kidney rises and stimulates erythropoiesis, increasing the RBC count so each litre of blood carries more oxygen.
★ HIGHLIGHT — These local hormones also include the growth factors secreted by several non-endocrine tissues, essential for the normal growth of tissues and their repair and regeneration — NCERT's reminder that the directory of messengers is bigger than the gland list.

How Hormones Act — Receptors and Second Messengers
• DEFINITION — Hormones produce their effects by binding to specific proteins called hormone receptors, located in the target tissues only. A hormone circulating through the whole body therefore speaks only to tissues carrying its receptor — receptor the lock, hormone the one specific key.
• DEFINITION — Receptors sit in two places: membrane-bound receptors on the cell membrane of the target cell, and intracellular receptors inside the cell, mostly in the nucleus. The hormone's chemistry decides: water-soluble hormones cannot cross the lipid membrane and use membrane receptors; lipid-soluble ones slip through to intracellular receptors.
• DEFINITION — Hormones interacting with membrane-bound receptors normally do not enter the target cell. Instead, surface binding generates second messengers inside — cyclic AMP, IP3 and calcium ions, the NCERT trio — which regulate cellular metabolism. The hormone is the first messenger; it knocks, and the second messengers do the work inside.
• DEFINITION — Hormones interacting with intracellular receptors — steroid hormones and iodothyronines are the named examples — mostly regulate gene expression: the hormone-receptor complex interacts with the genome, switching genes on or off, and the cumulative biochemical actions produce physiological and developmental effects.
✔ KEY POINT — The mechanism map examiners test: hormones that cannot enter the cell (insulin, glucagon, PTH, pituitary hormones) act through membrane receptors and second messengers — cAMP, IP3, Ca2+; steroids (cortisol, aldosterone, sex hormones) and iodothyronines (T3, T4) enter and act on gene expression via intracellular, mostly nuclear, receptors.
• WHY second messengers make surface signalling powerful — one hormone molecule at one receptor can generate many second-messenger molecules, each activating many enzymes in turn. The cascade amplifies a trace signal into a full cellular response — why hormones can work in trace amounts at all.
• EXAMPLE — Adrenaline meeting a liver cell does not enter it: it binds a membrane receptor, cyclic AMP rises, cascades switch on glycogen breakdown, and glucose pours out within seconds. Cortisol instead crosses in, binds an intracellular receptor and changes which genes are read — slower to start, longer to fade.
• WORKED PATTERN — Ask one question of any hormone: can it cross a lipid membrane? Steroids and thyroid hormones: yes — intracellular receptor, gene expression, slower and longer. Peptides and other non-crossers: no — membrane receptor, second messengers, fast and brief. NCERT itself names steroids and iodothyronines for the intracellular route.
• YOUR TURN — A hormone changes the amounts of specific proteins a cell produces over several hours. Which mechanism is it using? Answer: the intracellular-receptor mechanism — the hormone enters, its complex with a nuclear receptor interacts with the genome, and gene expression changes over hours.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Membrane-receptor hormones do NOT enter the target cell; options claiming insulin or glucagon enters the cell and binds DNA are wrong. And the second messengers are cAMP, IP3 and Ca2+ — glucose, ATP and the hormone itself are not second messengers in NCERT's list.

Negative Feedback — The Thermostat Logic of Control
• DEFINITION — In negative feedback, the final product of a hormone chain switches off the earlier steps of its own chain: when the product rises enough, it inhibits the glands that ordered its production; when it falls, inhibition lifts and production resumes. The loop holds the level in a narrow range instead of letting it drift.
• WORKED PATTERN — Practise on the thyroid axis: hypothalamus signals, anterior pituitary answers with TSH, TSH drives the thyroid to release T4 and T3 — and rising T4 and T3 feed back to inhibit pituitary and hypothalamus, so TSH falls. As they dip, inhibition lifts, TSH rises, and the cycle rebalances.
• WHY the thermostat comparison is exact — a thermostat switches the heater off at the set temperature and on again as the room cools; the thyroid axis switches TSH off when thyroxine reaches its set level and on again when it falls. In both, the output controls its own input: the feedback opposes the change.
• EXAMPLE — The iodine-deficiency case is this loop running openly: low iodine, low T4; low T4 means weak feedback inhibition, so TSH stays high; high TSH drives the thyroid continuously and it enlarges into a goitre — the visible shape of missing negative feedback.
✔ KEY POINT — Negative feedback explains three testable facts: hormone levels stay stable despite changing demand; target-gland deficiency raises its tropic hormone (low T4, high TSH); and giving a hormone from outside suppresses the body's own production. Product and tropic hormone move in opposite directions in a healthy loop.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any feedback question, draw the chain as arrows, mark the last arrow as a brake, and test options against one rule: product and tropic hormone move oppositely. High thyroxine must come with low TSH; a TSH-secreting tumour breaks the rule, giving high TSH with high thyroxine — an exception that itself becomes a diagnosis question.
• YOUR TURN — A patient takes large doses of thyroxine tablets for months. Predict her TSH level and her own thyroid's output. Answer: TSH will be low — high circulating thyroxine feeds back and inhibits the pituitary — and her own thyroid output will be suppressed, its TSH drive withdrawn.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Negative feedback is not the hormone stopping working: the target gland stays fully capable — the order from above is withdrawn. And the feedback acts on the hypothalamus and pituitary, the commanders; an option saying thyroxine inhibits the thyroid directly reverses the loop's geography.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Hormones are non-nutrient intercellular messengers produced in trace amounts; endocrine glands are ductless and release them into blood, exocrine glands deliver through ducts, and pancreas and gonads are composite (heterocrine) glands
✔ KEY POINT — Hypothalamus (basal diencephalon) commands two ways: releasing and inhibiting hormones (GnRH, somatostatin) reach the anterior pituitary by a portal system; oxytocin and ADH are made in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary under direct neural control
✔ KEY POINT — Anterior pituitary makes six hormones: GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH; pars intermedia makes only MSH; posterior pituitary makes none — it stores and releases hypothalamic oxytocin (uterine contraction, milk ejection) and vasopressin or ADH (water resorption)
✔ KEY POINT — GH disorders: childhood deficiency is dwarfism, childhood excess is gigantism, adult excess is acromegaly; ADH deficiency is diabetes insipidus — copious dilute urine, normal blood sugar
✔ KEY POINT — Pineal gland (dorsal forebrain) secretes melatonin: it maintains the 24-hour diurnal rhythm of sleep-wake cycle and body temperature, and influences metabolism, pigmentation, menstrual cycle and defence capability
✔ KEY POINT — Thyroid follicular cells make T4 and T3 (iodine essential) — basal metabolic rate, RBC formation, carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism; parafollicular cells make thyrocalcitonin (TCT), which lowers blood calcium
✔ KEY POINT — Thyroid disorders: iodine deficiency — hypothyroidism with simple goitre; in pregnancy and development — cretinism; in adults — myxoedema (Gull's disease); excess secretion — exophthalmic goitre (Graves' disease): protruding eyeballs, high BMR, weight loss
✔ KEY POINT — Parathyroids (four, behind the thyroid) secrete PTH, the hypercalcemic hormone — bone resorption, renal calcium reabsorption, intestinal absorption; PTH and TCT together maintain calcium balance
✔ KEY POINT — Thymus (behind the sternum) secretes thymosins: differentiation of T-lymphocytes (cell-mediated immunity) and promotion of antibody production; it degenerates with age, weakening immunity
✔ KEY POINT — Adrenal medulla secretes adrenaline and noradrenaline — emergency hormones of fight or flight: alertness, pupil dilation, piloerection, sweating, stronger faster heart beat, faster breathing, glycogen breakdown, breakdown of lipids and proteins
✔ KEY POINT — Adrenal cortex, outside to inside: glomerulosa — aldosterone (salt and water balance at renal tubules); fasciculata — cortisol (gluconeogenesis, anti-inflammatory, immune suppression); reticularis — small amounts of androgens (pubertal hair)
✔ KEY POINT — Cortical disorders: underproduction disturbs carbohydrate metabolism — Addison's disease (acute weakness and fatigue); chronic cortisol excess — Cushing's pattern (high blood sugar, muscle wasting, high blood pressure)
✔ KEY POINT — Pancreatic islets (1 to 2 million, 1 to 2 percent of the organ): alpha cells — glucagon (hyperglycemic); beta cells — insulin (hypoglycemic); insulin failure is diabetes mellitus — hyperglycemia, glucose in urine, ketone bodies, insulin therapy
✔ KEY POINT — Testis: Leydig cells secrete testosterone — accessory sex organs, muscle, facial hair, deep voice, spermatogenesis, libido; ovary: follicles secrete estrogen, corpus luteum secretes progesterone (pregnancy support, milk secretion)
✔ KEY POINT — Non-endocrine tissue hormones: heart atria — ANF (lowers raised blood pressure); kidney JG cells — erythropoietin (erythropoiesis) and renin; gut — gastrin, secretin, CCK and GIP for digestive coordination
✔ KEY POINT — Mechanism: membrane-receptor hormones generate second messengers (cyclic AMP, IP3, Ca2+) without entering the cell; steroids and iodothyronines enter and regulate gene expression via intracellular receptors; negative feedback holds every axis in range

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Saying the posterior pituitary makes oxytocin and vasopressin. Why it is wrong: both are synthesised by the hypothalamus and transported axonally; the posterior pituitary only stores and releases them. Correct principle: posterior lobe is a warehouse under direct neural control; the anterior lobe is the factory, working to hypothalamic orders by portal blood.
• Mistake 2: Treating ADH deficiency as sugar diabetes. Why it is wrong: in insipidus the urine is huge but blood glucose is normal and urine has no glucose; mellitus is insulin failure, high blood glucose spilling into urine. Correct principle: same symptom, different hormone and gland — insipidus is ADH and pituitary, mellitus is insulin and pancreas.
• Mistake 3: Using gigantism and acromegaly interchangeably for GH excess. Why it is wrong: age decides — in childhood the growing bones lengthen (gigantism); in adults the fused bones can only thicken (acromegaly, disfigured face). Correct principle: ask deficiency or excess, then child or adult; dwarfism, gigantism and acromegaly fall out of that grid.
• Mistake 4: Assigning calcium-raising to thyrocalcitonin because it sounds like the region's calcium hormone. Why it is wrong: TCT lowers blood calcium; the raiser is PTH from the parathyroids behind the thyroid. Correct principle: PTH is hypercalcemic — secreted when calcium is low, so it must raise it; TCT is its opposite partner.
• Mistake 5: Making the adrenal cortex the source of adrenaline. Why it is wrong: adrenaline and noradrenaline come from the medulla, the inner tissue; the cortex makes steroid corticoids — aldosterone, cortisol and androgens. Correct principle: medulla inside, catecholamines and emergencies; cortex outside in three zones, salt, sugar, sex steroids.
• Mistake 6: Swapping the cortex zones, especially putting aldosterone in the fasciculata. Why it is wrong: the order is fixed — glomerulosa (outer) mineralocorticoids, fasciculata (middle) glucocorticoids, reticularis (inner) androgens. Correct principle: run G, F, R from outside in and read salt, sugar, sex in the same direction.
• Mistake 7: Believing glucagon lowers blood sugar. Why it is wrong: glucagon is hyperglycemic — it orders hepatocytes to break down glycogen and build new glucose; insulin is the hypoglycemic partner. Correct principle: glucagon releases glucose from stores; insulin puts glucose into cells and glycogen. Alpha-glucagon raises, beta-insulin lowers.
• Mistake 8: Placing Leydig cells inside the seminiferous tubules as sperm-making cells. Why it is wrong: Leydig (interstitial) cells lie in the intertubular spaces and secrete androgens under LH; Sertoli cells inside the tubules respond to FSH and support spermiogenesis. Correct principle: outside the tubule — hormone and LH; inside — sperm support and FSH.
• Mistake 9: Claiming steroids act through membrane receptors and cyclic AMP. Why it is wrong: steroids and iodothyronines are lipid-soluble, cross the membrane and bind intracellular, mostly nuclear, receptors regulating gene expression; the surface route (cAMP, IP3, Ca2+) belongs to hormones that cannot enter, like insulin and glucagon. Correct principle: crossers use genes, non-crossers use second messengers.
• Mistake 10: Reading a high TSH as proof of an overactive thyroid. Why it is wrong: in a healthy loop, TSH is high precisely when thyroxine is low — classically in iodine deficiency, where the pituitary drives an under-producing thyroid until it enlarges into simple goitre. Correct principle: tropic and target hormones move in opposite directions under negative feedback; read the pair, never one value alone.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A 6-year-old is markedly short for age, with normal intelligence. Blood tests show very low growth hormone. Name the condition, the gland and the lobe at fault.
• Given: Childhood onset; GH deficiency; growth failure without the brain damage of cretinism.
• Principle: Low GH secretion from early age stunts body growth — pituitary dwarfism; GH comes from the anterior pituitary (pars distalis).
• Steps: Direction first: too little hormone, not excess. Age second: a child, so growth plates waited for a signal that never came. Match the pair: childhood GH deficiency is dwarfism.
• Answer: Pituitary dwarfism, from under-secretion of growth hormone by the anterior pituitary.
• Reasoning: The GH grid solves it mechanically, and normal intelligence separates it from cretinism, where thyroid deficiency in development damages the brain as well.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling every short child a cretin — cretinism needs hypothyroidism in pregnancy or early development and carries mental retardation, low IQ and deaf-mutism.
• Example 2 — Problem: A patient passes 12 litres of pale urine a day and drinks constantly. Blood glucose is normal, urine has no glucose, and ADH is very low. Diagnose, and name the true site of synthesis of the missing hormone.
• Given: Excess urine volume; normal blood glucose; no urinary glucose; deficient ADH.
• Principle: Without ADH the distal tubules resorb too little water, so water is lost in urine and dehydration threatens — diabetes insipidus. ADH is synthesised in the hypothalamus, released from the posterior pituitary.
• Steps: Glucose in urine would mean mellitus; normal glucose rules the pancreas out. The missing hormone is ADH, so the disorder is insipidus. Its synthesis site is the hypothalamus — the posterior pituitary only stores and releases it.
• Answer: Diabetes insipidus from ADH deficiency; the hormone is synthesised in the hypothalamus.
• Reasoning: Two laboratory facts — normal blood glucose, absent urinary glucose — eliminate the famous disease and force the pituitary answer; the synthesis-versus-release distinction earns the second mark.
• Mistake to avoid: Diagnosing diabetes mellitus from urine volume alone, or answering posterior pituitary for the synthesis site.
• Example 3 — Problem: In an iodine-deficient village, many adults have enlarged thyroids, yet tests show low thyroxine and high TSH. Explain the picture through feedback.
• Given: Iodine deficiency; low T4; high TSH; enlarged thyroid.
• Principle: Iodine is essential for T3 and T4 synthesis; falling thyroxine weakens negative feedback on the pituitary, so TSH rises; sustained high TSH drives thyroid growth.
• Steps: No iodine, so follicular cells cannot build enough T4 and T3. Low T4 removes the brake on the anterior pituitary. TSH climbs and stays high. The whipped gland enlarges — but enlargement cannot fix a missing raw material, so output stays low.
• Answer: Simple goitre — a deficiency disease in which high TSH from failed negative feedback enlarges an under-producing thyroid.
• Reasoning: The paradox (big gland, low hormone) dissolves once the loop is drawn: gland size reports the TSH drive, not the hormone output.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling the enlargement hyperthyroidism — in Graves' disease the hormone itself is high; here it is low, and the high value is the tropic hormone, TSH.
• Example 4 — Problem: Two hours after a glucose-rich meal, a healthy person's insulin is high and glucagon low; after an overnight fast the pattern reverses. Explain both states hormonally.
• Given: Fed state (high blood glucose) and fasting state (falling blood glucose) in a healthy pancreas.
• Principle: Insulin from beta cells lowers glucose (uptake by hepatocytes and adipocytes, glycogenesis); glucagon from alpha cells raises it (glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in hepatocytes); the glucose level itself switches each secretion.
• Steps: Fed: glucose high, beta cells release insulin, cells store glycogen, glucose falls, insulin declines. Fasting: glucose dips, alpha cells release glucagon, the liver breaks down glycogen and makes new glucose, glucose rises, glucagon declines.
• Answer: The fed state is insulin-dominant storage; the fasting state is glucagon-dominant release — one seesaw, two stable ends.
• Reasoning: Every arrow follows the hormone's NCERT job description, and the switching is negative feedback with blood glucose as the controlled variable.
• Mistake to avoid: Crediting glycogen breakdown to insulin — breakdown (glycogenolysis) belongs to glucagon; insulin builds glycogen (glycogenesis).
• Example 5 — Problem: A patient has high blood calcium and a tumour in one parathyroid gland. Predict its hormone output, two body effects, and the expected behaviour of thyrocalcitonin.
• Given: Parathyroid tumour; hypercalcemia (raised blood calcium).
• Principle: Parathyroid tissue secretes PTH, the hypercalcemic hormone acting on bone, kidney and intestine; high calcium normally suppresses PTH and favours the opposing TCT.
• Steps: The tumour secretes excess PTH autonomously. Effect one: bone resorption increases — bones demineralise as calcium leaves for the blood. Effect two: kidneys reabsorb more calcium and intestinal absorption rises. The high calcium should favour TCT secretion from the thyroid as counter-response, though the tumour ignores the normal switch-off.
• Answer: Excess PTH; bones demineralise while renal and intestinal calcium entry increases; TCT secretion is favoured as the counter-response.
• Reasoning: The case is the PTH section read backwards — from the controlled variable (calcium high) to the hormone (PTH), its source (parathyroid) and its three routes (bone, kidney, gut).
• Mistake to avoid: Blaming the thyroid because the tumour sits near it — the parathyroids lie on the back of the thyroid but are separate glands with the opposite calcium hormone.
• Example 6 — Problem: A student argues that since adrenaline raises blood glucose like glucagon, adrenaline must also bind an intracellular receptor and switch on genes in the liver. Evaluate the claim.
• Given: Adrenaline and glucagon both raise blood glucose; the claim concerns receptor location.
• Principle: Mechanism follows chemistry, not effect. Hormones that cannot enter the cell act through membrane receptors and second messengers (cyclic AMP, IP3, Ca2+); only lipid-soluble steroids and iodothyronines use intracellular, mostly nuclear, receptors and gene expression.
• Steps: Similar outcome does not imply similar route. Adrenaline is a catecholamine acting at the cell surface, relayed inside by second messengers — fast enough for an emergency. Gene-expression mechanisms take hours — the wrong tool for fight or flight.
• Answer: The claim fails: adrenaline signals through membrane receptors and second messengers; the intracellular gene-expression route belongs to steroids and iodothyronines.
• Reasoning: NCERT sorts hormones by entry, not outcome — surface and second messengers for non-entering hormones, nucleus and genome for entering ones.
• Mistake to avoid: Sorting hormones into mechanisms by what they do (both raise glucose) instead of by what they are (catecholamine versus steroid or iodothyronine).
• Example 7 — Problem: A woman on cortisol-like steroid tablets for a year has frequent infections, thinning muscles and high blood sugar — and her own adrenal cortisol output is low. Explain all four findings.
• Given: Long-term external glucocorticoid; immune suppression; proteolysis signs; hyperglycemia; suppressed natural cortisol.
• Principle: Cortisol suppresses the immune response, stimulates proteolysis and gluconeogenesis, and — like any axis product — feeds back negatively on ACTH release.
• Steps: Infections: glucocorticoids suppress immune responses. Thin muscles: cortisol stimulates proteolysis. High blood sugar: gluconeogenesis builds new glucose while amino-acid uptake is inhibited. Low natural cortisol: the external hormone feeds back on the pituitary, ACTH falls, and the zona fasciculata loses its drive.
• Answer: All four findings are cortisol's textbook actions plus negative feedback on its own axis.
• Reasoning: One hormone's function list explains three findings; the chapter's closing principle — feedback — explains the fourth.
• Mistake to avoid: Attributing the low natural cortisol to adrenal damage — the gland is intact but unstimulated, because ACTH was switched off by feedback, the same loop as thyroxine and TSH.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. GnRH (gonadotrophin releasing hormone) stimulates the pituitary to release the gonadotrophins LH and FSH; somatostatin inhibits the release of growth hormone. Misconception watch: both are hypothalamic and reach the anterior pituitary through the portal blood — somatostatin does not act on GH target tissues directly.
• A2. Oxytocin and vasopressin (ADH). They are synthesised in the hypothalamus and transported axonally to the posterior pituitary, which stores and releases them. Misconception watch: released from is not made in — the posterior pituitary manufactures no hormone of its own.
• A3. Dwarfism is GH deficiency in childhood; acromegaly is GH excess in adults; cretinism is thyroid deficiency during pregnancy and development; exophthalmic goitre is thyroid excess (Graves' disease). Misconception watch: gigantism, the missing fifth match, is GH excess in childhood — examiners include it among the options anyway.
• A4. Blood glucose (with a check for glucose in urine): in diabetes mellitus blood glucose is high and glucose appears in the urine; in diabetes insipidus both are normal, and the deficient hormone is ADH. Misconception watch: urine volume alone cannot separate the two — both pass excess urine for entirely different reasons.
• A5. Zona glomerulosa (outer): aldosterone — sodium and water reabsorption at renal tubules, holding blood pressure. Zona fasciculata (middle): cortisol — gluconeogenesis, anti-inflammatory, immune-suppressing. Zona reticularis (inner): androgens — axial, pubic and facial hair at puberty. Misconception watch: the order is G, F, R outside-in, matching salt, sugar, sex; reversing the stack reverses every answer.
• A6. The parathyroid glands respond with PTH, the hypercalcemic hormone. Three routes: bone resorption (dissolution of bone mineral), reabsorption of calcium by the renal tubules, and increased absorption of calcium from digested food. Misconception watch: TCT moves calcium the other way and is suppressed, not secreted, when calcium falls.
• A7. Fast surface route: glucagon from the alpha cells of the islets (or adrenaline from the adrenal medulla) — membrane receptors and second messengers such as cyclic AMP. Slow nuclear route: cortisol from the zona fasciculata — an intracellular receptor regulating gene expression. Misconception watch: a shared effect does not mean a shared mechanism; the route follows the hormone's chemistry.
• A8. Negative feedback: high circulating thyroxine, the axis product, inhibits the anterior pituitary and reduces TSH release; with less TSH the patient's own thyroid is driven less. Misconception watch: the tablets do not damage the thyroid or pituitary — both are intact and obeying the loop, and the axis restarts when the tablets stop.

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: The endocrine system is a broadcast network: ductless glands release non-nutrient messengers into the blood in trace amounts, and only receptor-bearing cells respond. The hypothalamus commands, the pituitary relays with six anterior hormones plus MSH, and thyroid, parathyroid, thymus, adrenals, pancreas and gonads execute, with heart, kidney and gut adding local hormones.
• Summary: Pituitary disorders turn on GH dose and age plus ADH; the thyroid runs metabolism with T3 and T4 and calcium with TCT, against PTH; the adrenals split into a fast medulla and a three-zone cortex (salt, sugar, sex); the pancreas runs the insulin-glucagon seesaw failing as diabetes mellitus; the gonads, under LH and FSH, deliver testosterone, estrogen and progesterone.
