Introduction — Your Body’s Telephone Network That Outruns Thought
• Your body runs two control systems. The endocrine system broadcasts hormones through the blood — slow, widespread, long-lasting. The neural system is the opposite: point-to-point wiring delivering an electrical message to one exact address in milliseconds. This chapter is the wiring manual.
• DEFINITION — The neural system is the network of neurons providing an organised, point-to-point connection for quick coordination of body functions. A neuron is its structural and functional unit, and the signal it carries is the nerve impulse.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — the paper recycles a small set of scenes: a pump moving ions, a membrane flipping its charge, a vesicle emptying into a cleft, a named brain part matched to its job, a chain of ossicles, a missing spot in the retina. Master each scene once and questions become recognitions.
• EXAMPLE — Touch a hot pan and your hand pulls back before you consciously feel the heat. One signal raced up, a command raced back, and the brain was informed afterwards. That event previews the reflex arc.
• WORKED PATTERN — The master key: follow the signal through four questions. Where is it generated? How does it travel? Where is it handed over? What does it finally trigger? Test any topic against the four and gaps show themselves at once.
• YOUR TURN — Why can neural coordination react in milliseconds while hormonal coordination takes seconds to minutes? Answer: neurons are dedicated cables delivering to a specific target almost instantly; hormones must be secreted, ride the bloodstream everywhere, and wait for a target to respond.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The chapter in one breath: a resting neuron holds about −70 mV; a threshold stimulus fires an action potential that races along the axon (leaping between nodes if myelinated), crosses a synapse chemically, and reaches a CNS that thinks with its forebrain and rescues with spinal reflexes — while eyes and ears convert light and sound into the same language.

The Neural System — Two Divisions, One Command Network
• DEFINITION — The central neural system (CNS) consists of the brain and spinal cord: the processing headquarters where incoming information is integrated and decisions are formed.
• DEFINITION — PNS fibres run in two directions. Afferent fibres transmit impulses from tissues and organs to the CNS; efferent fibres transmit regulatory impulses from the CNS out to the tissues. Afferent arrives, efferent exits.
• DEFINITION — The somatic neural system relays impulses from the CNS to skeletal muscles — the voluntary division: reaching, walking, writing. The autonomic neural system transmits impulses from the CNS to the involuntary organs and smooth muscles of the body.
• DEFINITION — The autonomic system has two opposing divisions. The sympathetic division mobilises the body for emergencies (faster heartbeat, widened airways); the parasympathetic restores routine housekeeping (digestion, slowed heart). Accelerator and brake on the same organs.
• DEFINITION — The visceral nervous system is the part of the PNS built of fibres and ganglia through which impulses travel from the CNS to the viscera and back — the wiring of the internal organs, with the autonomic system as its outgoing arm.
• WHY two control systems at all — coordination needs a sprinter and a marathon runner. The neural system gives speed and precision for immediate dangers; hormones give slow, sustained, body-wide adjustment. Neither alone could do both jobs.
• EXAMPLE — At a sprint start, the somatic system fires your leg muscles on command, while the sympathetic system raises your heart rate and visceral nerves adjust your gut — three divisions cooperating in one second.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify any pathway by destination and direction: skeletal muscle means somatic; smooth muscle, glands or viscera mean autonomic-visceral; towards the CNS means afferent; away from it, efferent. Two labels fix any fibre.
• YOUR TURN — An impulse travels from the CNS to the smooth muscle of the intestine. Name direction and system. Answer: efferent (leaving the CNS) and autonomic, within the visceral network — gut muscle is involuntary, so somatic is excluded.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The autonomic system is not a third system beside somatic and visceral; it is the motor arm of the visceral network. Do not list CNS, PNS, somatic, autonomic and visceral as five parallel systems — the last three are nested subdivisions of the PNS.

The Neuron — Anatomy of the Signal Cell
• DEFINITION — A neuron is a microscopic structure of three parts: cell body, dendrites and axon. It is the structural and functional unit of the neural system — the cell specialised to generate and conduct impulses.
• DEFINITION — The cell body contains cytoplasm with the cell nucleus and characteristic granular bodies called Nissl granules — the protein-making machinery that maintains this enormous cell.
• DEFINITION — Dendrites are short fibres that branch repeatedly and project out of the cell body. They also contain Nissl granules, and they transmit impulses towards the cell body — the neuron’s receiving antennae.
• DEFINITION — The axon is a long fibre whose distal end is branched; each branch terminates in a bulb-like synaptic knob containing synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter. It transmits impulses away from the cell body.
✔ KEY POINT — Direction is built into the anatomy: dendrites carry impulses towards the cell body, the axon away from it towards a synapse. Nissl granules mark the receiving end (cell body and dendrites); synaptic knobs mark the sending end.
• WHY a neuron needs such an extreme shape — a signal starting in your toe must reach your spinal cord without being re-sent by a relay of ordinary cells. One cell stretched over the whole distance removes handovers, and handovers are where speed and fidelity die.
• EXAMPLE — A sensory neuron serving the skin of your foot has its cell body beside the spinal cord and a single axon running the full length of your leg — over a metre of one cell, among the longest in the body.
• WORKED PATTERN — Run the identity test in order: Nissl granules present means cell body or dendrite; a synaptic knob means axon terminal; a myelin sheath or great length means axon shaft. Any fragment is identifiable from one feature.
• YOUR TURN — A short, repeatedly branched fibre containing Nissl granules is shown. Name it and its traffic direction. Answer: a dendrite — granules exclude the axon, and branching excludes the shaft; dendrites carry impulses towards the cell body.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The classic swap: “Nissl granules are present in the axon” is false — they occupy the cell body and dendrites and are absent from the axon. Granule location is a favourite one-mark discriminator.

Myelin — Schwann Cells, Oligodendrocytes and the Nodes of Ranvier
• DEFINITION — Neurons are myelinated or non-myelinated, depending on whether the axon is wrapped in an insulating myelin sheath. Spinal and cranial nerves are typically built of myelinated fibres.
• DEFINITION — In the peripheral nervous system the sheath is formed by Schwann cells, which wrap and envelop the axon. In the CNS the same job is done by oligodendrocytes. One fibre type, two different builders — sorted by address.
• DEFINITION — The gaps between two adjacent myelin sheaths are called nodes of Ranvier. At a node the axon membrane is exposed to the extracellular fluid — the only places where ion exchange and regeneration can occur.
• DEFINITION — Non-myelinated fibres are also enclosed by a Schwann cell, but the cell does not produce a myelin sheath around them. They are common in autonomic and somatic fibres of the visceral network.
✔ KEY POINT — The myelin map in one line: sheath built by Schwann cells (PNS) or oligodendrocytes (CNS); gaps between sheaths are nodes of Ranvier; Schwann cells also wrap fibres that have no sheath at all.
• WHY insulation speeds a biological wire — signals leak through bare membrane and fade. Myelin seals the leak, so the electrical effect of one node reaches the next at full strength and only the nodes need the slow work of regeneration.
• EXAMPLE — The white matter of the brain and cord owes its pale colour to masses of myelinated axons; regions rich in cell bodies and dendrites form the darker grey matter. Colour on a brain section is a myelin map.
• WORKED PATTERN — Myelin questions are matching questions: builder (Schwann or oligodendrocyte) to territory (PNS or CNS), and gap (node) to function (regeneration site). Territory given — answer builder; gap given — answer function.
• YOUR TURN — A disease destroys myelin sheaths around CNS axons. Which cell has failed, and what happens to conduction? Answer: oligodendrocytes; impulses lose their leaping mode and slow down — insulation loss, not neuron death, is the first problem.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Schwann cells are not exclusive to myelinated fibres: they also enclose non-myelinated fibres without making a sheath. “Schwann cell present, therefore myelinated” is an invalid inference the paper loves to test.

The Resting Potential — A Charged Battery at Rest (−70 mV)
• DEFINITION — A resting axon conducts no impulse, yet its membrane is electrically polarised: a potential difference of about −70 mV, inside negative relative to outside. This is the resting potential.
• DEFINITION — The permeability profile creating this state: the resting membrane is comparatively highly permeable to K+ (potassium leaks out readily), nearly impermeable to Na+, and impermeable to the negatively charged proteins trapped in the axoplasm.
• DEFINITION — The gradients: axoplasm holds a high concentration of K+ and negatively charged proteins and a low concentration of Na+; the fluid outside holds the reverse — high Na+, low K+.
• DEFINITION — The gradients are actively maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, an ATP-driven carrier that transports 3 Na+ out of the axon for every 2 K+ pumped in. Three out, two in — the most quoted ratio in the chapter.
✔ KEY POINT — The resting state in one line: outside positive, inside negative at about −70 mV; K+ leaks out, Na+ is kept out, trapped protein anions stay in, and the 3-out-2-in pump maintains the gradients.
• WHY the inside ends up negative if K+ is a positive ion — because K+ leaves. Each departing K+ abandons an unmatched negative charge (the trapped proteins) inside. Charge separation across a thin membrane is all a voltage ever is.
• EXAMPLE — Think of the resting axon as a charged battery on standby: no current flows, yet the terminals hold a voltage, ready to discharge the moment a circuit is completed. A stimulus does not create the energy; it releases what the pump stored.
• WORKED PATTERN — Every resting-potential question checks three facts in rotation: value (−70 mV), cause (K+ leak out through a Na+-tight membrane plus trapped anions), and maintenance (3-out-2-in pump). Name the trio and the mark follows.
• YOUR TURN — A poison blocks the sodium-potassium pump. Over time, what happens to excitability? Answer: gradients run down as leaks continue unopposed, the resting potential shrinks, and impulses eventually fail. The pump is maintenance, not the spark.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The pump direction is the most-reversed fact in the chapter: 3 Na+ OUT, 2 K+ IN. If an option pumps sodium inwards, it is describing depolarisation’s channel flow, not the pump — eliminate it instantly.

The Action Potential — Depolarisation Fires the Signal
• DEFINITION — When a stimulus of sufficient strength (a threshold stimulus) is applied at a site on the polarised membrane, that site becomes freely permeable to Na+. Sodium channels open and Na+ rushes inwards.
• DEFINITION — The influx reverses the polarity at that site: the outer surface becomes negatively charged and the inner side positively charged. The membrane is depolarised, and the potential at that site is the action potential.
✔ KEY POINT — An action potential is not cargo carried along the axon; it is a wave of polarity reversal regenerated at each successive patch: Na+ in (depolarisation), then K+ out (repolarisation), site after site.
• DEFINITION — The reversal is extremely short-lived. Almost immediately the membrane’s permeability to K+ rises; K+ diffuses out, restoring the resting polarity — repolarisation. The whole spike occupies a tiny fraction of a second at any one patch.
• WHY the impulse moves forward — at the depolarised site, currents flow on either side of the membrane between this site and the neighbouring resting sites. These local currents depolarise the next patch to threshold, while the patch behind is busy repolarising and cannot fire again at once.
• EXAMPLE — Picture a line of dominoes: each falling domino is one patch depolarising, and its fall is what topples the next. The wave is the pattern of falls, not any single domino travelling.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sequence questions are solved by ion identity: influx of Na+ means depolarisation is happening; efflux of K+ means repolarisation. If a step names the wrong ion for the phase, the whole option falls — check ions before checking order.
• YOUR TURN — At one instant, patch A of an axon shows inside positive while patch B ahead is still inside negative. Describe each. Answer: A is depolarised, firing now (Na+ rushed in). B is at rest, polarised near −70 mV, and about to be triggered by local currents.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Depolarisation is Na+ moving IN; repolarisation is K+ moving OUT. Any option that depolarises with K+ influx or repolarises with Na+ efflux has swapped the ions — wrong, however polished the sentence sounds.

Conduction Along the Axon — Continuous versus Saltatory
• DEFINITION — In a non-myelinated fibre, the action potential is regenerated at every successive patch of membrane along the whole length — continuous conduction: reliable, but slow, and costly in ions to pump back.
• DEFINITION — In a myelinated fibre, the insulated segments cannot exchange ions, so the action potential is regenerated only at the nodes of Ranvier and appears to jump from node to node — saltatory conduction: fast, and cheap in ions pumped back.
✔ KEY POINT — Saltatory conduction is faster and cheaper: faster because few regenerations are needed along the fibre, cheaper because ion exchange happens only at the nodes, so the pump restores far less afterwards.
• WHY the signal does not die out under the sheath — local currents are electrical effects: the charge separation at one node spreads passively under the sheath and reaches the next node still strong enough for threshold. Insulation stops the spread leaking away.
• EXAMPLE — Compare crossing a stream two ways: stepping on every stone (continuous) versus long leaps between a few big boulders (saltatory). The leaper crosses faster with far fewer wet steps — the same economy the myelinated axon achieves.
• WORKED PATTERN — Any stem mentioning jumping, leaping or nodes asks about saltatory conduction and expects the myelinated fibre. Any stem about point-by-point regeneration expects the non-myelinated fibre. The vocabulary of the stem gives the answer away.
• YOUR TURN — Two axons have the same length; X is myelinated, Y is not. Which delivers its impulse sooner and spends less ATP? Answer: X on both counts — few regenerations mean a short journey, and few ion crossings mean little pump work to restore them.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The impulse does not travel inside the myelin or through the Schwann cell; it is regenerated at the nodes, in the axon membrane itself. Myelin is insulation around the wire, never the wire.

The Synapse — Where One Neuron Talks to the Next
• DEFINITION — A nerve impulse is transmitted from one neuron to another through junctions called synapses, formed by the membranes of a pre-synaptic and a post-synaptic neuron, which may or may not be separated by a synaptic cleft. Two types: electrical and chemical.
• DEFINITION — In an electrical synapse, the membranes of the pre- and post-synaptic neurons are in very close proximity, and electrical current flows directly from one neuron into the other — no middleman.
• DEFINITION — In a chemical synapse, the two membranes are separated by a fluid-filled synaptic cleft, and transmission uses chemicals called neurotransmitters. The impulse cannot jump the gap electrically, so it is converted into a chemical message and back.
✔ KEY POINT — Speed versus flexibility is the trade: electrical synapses transmit faster (direct current), chemical synapses transmit slower but can be gated, graded and inhibited. The human system is built mostly of the chemical kind.
• WHY our system mostly chose the slower design — a purely electrical network would be an all-or-nothing grid: every signal passing everywhere, impossible to grade, block or reroute. Chemical junctions add control: thresholds, amplification, inhibition, modulation.
• EXAMPLE — The junction between a motor neuron and a muscle fibre runs the chemical design: one impulse releases acetylcholine, and the whole fibre contracts. A single control point also means a single point where fatigue, poisons and medicines can act.
• WORKED PATTERN — Identify the type from the evidence: very close membranes and direct current flow mean electrical; cleft, vesicles and transmitters mean chemical. Exam stems always leak one of these clues — hunt for the furniture, not the label.
• YOUR TURN — Electrical synapses are rare in the human system despite being faster. Give the trade-off. Answer: they pass current directly and cannot be gated, graded or inhibited; chemical synapses sacrifice a little speed for control, which a complex system needs more.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — “Impulse transmission across an electrical synapse is always faster than across a chemical synapse” is a TRUE NCERT statement — do not eliminate it for sounding absolute. The trap runs the other way: options calling chemical transmission faster are false.

Chemical Transmission — Acetylcholine and the Postsynaptic Potential
• DEFINITION — The axon terminals contain synaptic vesicles packed with neurotransmitter. When an impulse arrives, the vesicles fuse with the membrane and release their neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft.
• DEFINITION — The released neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds to specific receptors on the post-synaptic membrane. Binding opens ion channels, ions enter, and a new potential is generated in the post-synaptic neuron.
✔ KEY POINT — The five-step chain, in the only order that works: impulse arrives at the knob; vesicles fuse and release transmitter; transmitter diffuses across the cleft; it binds post-synaptic receptors; ion channels open and a new potential is generated.
• DEFINITION — The new potential may be either excitatory or inhibitory. An excitatory potential pushes the post-synaptic cell towards threshold, raising the chance it fires; an inhibitory one pushes it away. The synapse decides, not merely relays.
• DEFINITION — Acetylcholine is the classic neurotransmitter of this chapter: released at many synapses and at the junction between motor neurons and skeletal muscle. It is rapidly broken down in the cleft by acetylcholinesterase, which terminates the signal and readies the junction for the next impulse.
• WHY transmission is strictly one-way here — the sending equipment (vesicles) exists only in the pre-synaptic knob, and the receiving equipment (receptors) only on the post-synaptic membrane. A signal cannot travel backwards through furniture that exists on one side only.
• EXAMPLE — Watch the chain fire at a neuro-muscular junction: impulse arrives, vesicles fuse, acetylcholine crosses the cleft, receptors open channels, the muscle fibre depolarises and contracts — then acetylcholinesterase clears the cleft so the fibre can relax and be re-commanded.
• WORKED PATTERN — Order questions give four or five synaptic events in scrambled order. Anchor the two ends first — impulse arrival must be first, post-synaptic potential last — then place fusion before diffusion and diffusion before binding. The middle orders itself.
• YOUR TURN — A chemical prevents acetylcholinesterase from working. Predict the effect at a junction using acetylcholine. Answer: the transmitter lingers, receptors stay activated, and the post-synaptic cell is stimulated repeatedly — one command becomes a stuck signal.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Neurotransmitter is released from the PRE-synaptic knob and acts on the POST-synaptic membrane. MCQs routinely put vesicles in the post-synaptic cell or receptors in the pre-synaptic one — both are furniture in the wrong room.

The Central Neural System — Brain and Spinal Cord
• DEFINITION — The CNS (brain plus spinal cord) is where incoming sensory information is processed and integrated, and from where motor commands are issued. The human brain is the central information-processing organ — the command and control system of the body.
• DEFINITION — Protection is layered. The brain sits inside the bony cranium, wrapped in three meninges: the outer dura mater, the middle arachnoid, and the inner pia mater in contact with the brain. Cerebrospinal fluid fills the inner spaces and cushions the tissue.
✔ KEY POINT — The meninges from outside to inside: dura mater (outer, tough), arachnoid (middle, thin), pia mater (inner, in contact with the brain). Cerebrospinal fluid cushions the CNS inside this wrapping.
• DEFINITION — The brain divides into forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. Forebrain: cerebrum, thalamus, hypothalamus. Midbrain: the dorsal corpora quadrigemina. Hindbrain: pons, cerebellum, medulla — which continues below as the spinal cord.
• WHY the processors sit inside armour — neurons barely regenerate, so damage to the CNS is largely permanent. Bone, membranes and fluid are cheap insurance for irreplaceable tissue; the PNS, which can repair itself better, travels with far less protection.
• EXAMPLE — A banged head hurts, yet the brain usually escapes damage that a similar blow would do to exposed tissue: skull, meninges and fluid share and spread the impact. The packaging is functional, not decorative.
• WORKED PATTERN — For protection questions, walk the stack outside-in: cranium, dura mater, arachnoid, pia mater, brain. For division questions, sort the named part into forebrain, midbrain or hindbrain first — most wrong options are right parts in the wrong division.
• YOUR TURN — Which meninx would a surgeon encounter last before reaching brain tissue? Answer: the pia mater — dura and arachnoid are crossed first; the pia lies directly on the brain.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The arachnoid is the thin MIDDLE layer; the pia mater is the INNERMOST and touches the brain. Anchor it by contact: pia is the one in physical contact with the neural tissue.

The Forebrain — Cerebrum, Thalamus, Hypothalamus and Limbic System
• DEFINITION — The forebrain consists of the cerebrum, the thalamus and the hypothalamus. It is the largest, most developed region in humans — the seat of thought, memory, voluntary action and the regulation of internal states.
• DEFINITION — The cerebrum is divided lengthwise into two cerebral hemispheres, connected by a tract of nerve fibres, the corpus callosum. A layer of cells called the cerebral cortex covers it — the folded grey matter where much of the processing happens.
• DEFINITION — Within the cortex lie motor areas (commanding movement), sensory areas (receiving sensation), and large association areas — neither clearly sensory nor motor — responsible for complex functions such as intersensory association, memory and communication.
• DEFINITION — The thalamus, around which the cerebrum wraps, is a major coordinating centre for sensory and motor signalling — the relay station for traffic between body and cortex.
• DEFINITION — The hypothalamus lies at the base of the diencephalon, below the thalamus. It contains centres controlling body temperature and the urge for eating and drinking, and it secretes hypothalamic hormones that govern the pituitary — the bridge between the neural and endocrine systems.
• DEFINITION — The limbic system is formed by the inner parts of the cerebral hemispheres with deep structures such as the amygdala and hippocampus. With the hypothalamus it regulates emotional reactions (excitement, pleasure, rage, fear) and motivation.
✔ KEY POINT — The forebrain job map: cerebrum thinks, decides, remembers; thalamus relays; hypothalamus regulates temperature, hunger, thirst and hormones; limbic system feels and motivates. Four owners, four verb families — match the stem’s verb to its owner.
• EXAMPLE — The smell of lunch reaches you: the thalamus routes the signal, the limbic system tags it with pleasure and memory, the hypothalamus converts it into hunger, and the cerebrum decides to walk to the kitchen — four forebrain structures in one ordinary moment.
• WORKED PATTERN — Run the job map: voluntary action, memory or language means cerebrum; relay of signals means thalamus; temperature, hunger, thirst or hormones means hypothalamus; emotion or motivation means limbic system. Match the verb in the stem to its owner.
• YOUR TURN — After an injury, a patient’s temperature regulation fails and appetite becomes erratic, while memory and voluntary movement are normal. Name the structure. Answer: the hypothalamus — temperature and appetite are its signature centres; the intact higher functions clear the cerebrum.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The corpus callosum connects the two CEREBRAL hemispheres — not the cerebellar ones, and it is a fibre tract, not a brain region. Pair it with cerebrum in every matching question.

Midbrain and Hindbrain — Relays, Balance and Survival Centres
• DEFINITION — The midbrain is located between the thalamus-hypothalamus of the forebrain and the pons of the hindbrain. Its dorsal portion consists mainly of four round lobes, the corpora quadrigemina — relay and reflex centres for visual and auditory inputs.
• DEFINITION — The hindbrain comprises the pons, the cerebellum and the medulla oblongata. The pons consists of fibre tracts interconnecting different parts of the brain — a bridge between regions.
• DEFINITION — The cerebellum, the second-largest part of the brain, has a very convoluted surface providing additional space for many more neurons. It coordinates posture, balance and the precision of voluntary movement — the fine-tuner, not the commander.
• DEFINITION — The medulla oblongata is the lowest part of the brain, continuing into the spinal cord. It contains centres controlling respiration, cardiovascular reflexes and gastric secretions.
✔ KEY POINT — Brain stem equals midbrain plus hindbrain: the stalk connecting cerebrum to cord, carrying all traffic between them and housing the survival centres. Forebrain thinks; the stem conducts and keeps alive.
• WHY the survival centres sit so low — breathing and heartbeat cannot wait for deliberation, so their centres sit in the oldest, most protected core, close to the cord, running automatically. The higher centres can advise them; they cannot replace them.
• EXAMPLE — Riding a bicycle without thinking is the cerebellum at work: the cerebrum learned the skill consciously years ago, but balance corrections now run automatically below awareness — which is why you cannot explain to a learner exactly what you do.
• WORKED PATTERN — Answer hindbrain questions by scale of function: connecting regions means pons; coordinating movement and balance means cerebellum; keeping you alive minute to minute (breathing, circulation, gastric secretion) means medulla.
• YOUR TURN — A patient can decide to move an arm and the arm moves, but the movements are clumsy, overshooting the target. Which hindbrain part is suspect? Answer: the cerebellum — intention and strength survive (cerebrum and pathways intact); precision is what failed.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The pons belongs to the HINDBRAIN, not the midbrain. Equally, the corpora quadrigemina are MIDBRAIN. Division questions are lost on these two placements more than on any others.

The Spinal Cord and Reflex Action — The Fast Lane
• DEFINITION — The spinal cord extends from the medulla oblongata through the vertebral column. It conducts impulses between the brain and the body in both directions and serves as the centre for spinal reflexes — processing simple responses locally, without waiting for the brain.
• DEFINITION — A reflex action is an immediate, involuntary response to a stimulus, routed through the spinal cord rather than the brain. Withdrawal from a hot or sharp object is the model example: fast, automatic, and protective.
• WHY reflexes bypass the brain — survival arithmetic. A signal to the brain and back travels far and crosses decision machinery; a cord-level loop is short and pre-wired. In the half-second the detour would cost, tissue damage multiplies — the shortcut is the whole point.
• EXAMPLE — The knee-jerk reflex is the textbook circuit: a tap stretches receptors in the thigh muscle, a sensory neuron reports to the cord, a motor neuron fires, and the same muscle contracts — leg extends before you have decided anything.
✔ KEY POINT — Reflex equals fast, involuntary and spinal-cord-controlled: the response is completed before conscious perception of the stimulus. If the brain must decide first, it is a reaction, not a reflex.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify any response with three checks: involuntary? immediate? stimulated by a specific trigger? Three yeses mean reflex arc. A thoughtful, delayed, voluntary act — however fast — is not a reflex, and exam stems exploit exactly that confusion.
• YOUR TURN — Stepping on a sharp stone, your foot withdraws instantly, and pain is felt just after. Explain the order. Answer: the withdrawal is completed by the spinal reflex arc; pain is perceived only when signals additionally ascend to the brain — the short loop finishes before the long report arrives.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Reflexes are controlled by the spinal cord, not “by the brain quickly”. If an option routes a spinal reflex through conscious thought, it has described a voluntary reaction, not a reflex — eliminate it.

The Reflex Arc — Five Links from Receptor to Effector
• DEFINITION — A reflex arc is the fixed pathway of a reflex: an afferent (sensory) neuron receives the signal from a sensory organ and transmits it via a dorsal nerve root into the CNS (spinal cord); an interneuron relays it to an efferent (motor) neuron, whose signal reaches the effector organ.
✔ KEY POINT — The five links in their only correct order: receptor, sensory (afferent) neuron, interneuron in the spinal cord, motor (efferent) neuron, effector. Dorsal root in, ventral root out — the arc’s traffic law.
• DEFINITION — Trace the arc on the hot-pan event. Heat receptors in the skin fire; the sensory neuron carries the impulse in through the dorsal root; an interneuron passes it to a motor neuron; the motor fibre leaves via the ventral root; arm muscles contract and the hand withdraws.
• WHY the interneuron matters — it is the cord’s switchboard: it receives the incoming report, selects the right motor neuron, and can also pass information upward to the brain. Without it, input and output could not be matched flexibly inside the cord.
• EXAMPLE — The same five-link design runs the knee jerk, the withdrawal from a pin, and the blink when dust approaches the eye. Learn one arc and you have learned them all — only the named parts change.
• WORKED PATTERN — Arc questions present events out of order and ask for the sequence. The receptor always opens and the effector always closes; between them, traffic flows sensory to interneuron to motor. Any sequence putting the effector mid-chain is wrong at a glance.
• YOUR TURN — The sensory fibre enters the cord through the dorsal root, and the motor fibre leaves through the ventral root. A lesion cuts the ventral root only. What fails? Answer: the motor exit — the stimulus is still sensed, but the effector never receives its command, so no response occurs.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Afferent and efferent are the most swapped pair in the chapter: afferent carries impulses TOWARDS the CNS, efferent AWAY from it. Sensory equals afferent, motor equals efferent — two names, one direction each.

The Human Eye — Parts of the Camera That Sees
• DEFINITION — The eye is the sense organ of vision: roughly spherical organs set in skull sockets called orbits. Its wall has three layers — the external sclera, the middle choroid, and the internal retina — each with a distinct job: shape, nourishment, sensing.
• DEFINITION — The sclera is the tough external layer of dense connective tissue — the white of the eye. At the front it becomes the transparent cornea, the window through which light enters. Protection at the back, admission at the front.
• DEFINITION — The choroid is the middle layer: vascular and bluish. It is thin over the posterior two-thirds of the eyeball but thickens in front to form the ciliary body, which continues forward as the pigmented, opaque iris — the visible coloured part, whose central opening is the pupil.
• DEFINITION — The crystalline lens is a transparent, biconvex structure held in place by ligaments attached to the ciliary body, behind the pupil. It focuses light onto the retina.
• DEFINITION — Two fluids fill the eye: between cornea and lens lies the aqueous humour; between lens and retina, the thick vitreous humour. Both are transparent — anything opaque in the light path would blind the eye from inside.
✔ KEY POINT — Light’s route through the eye, in order: cornea, aqueous humour, pupil (the iris’s opening), lens, vitreous humour, retina. Anything inserted into this sequence at the wrong place breaks the answer instantly.
• EXAMPLE — Your eye colour is the iris’s pigment seen through the transparent cornea; in a dark room the pupil widens as the iris opens the aperture — the eye’s own camera diaphragm, run by choroid tissue.
• WORKED PATTERN — Eye questions ask one of two things: which structure a ray passes next (order along the light path), or which structure a named part belongs to (layer of the wall). Walk the path or name the layer — never guess from familiarity.
• YOUR TURN — Which layer of the eyeball wall is vascular, and which of its front parts controls the pupil’s size? Answer: the choroid — its anterior continuation, the iris, adjusts the pupil; its ciliary body holds the lens.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The cornea is not a fourth layer and not part of the choroid: it is the transparent front of the SCLERA. And the iris, though visible and coloured, is choroid-derived — layer questions exploit both confusions.

The Retina — Rods, Cones and the Blind Spot
• DEFINITION — The retina contains three layers of neural cells, from inside to outside: ganglion cells, then bipolar cells, then photoreceptor cells. Note the oddity: light crosses the first two layers before reaching the sensors at the back.
• DEFINITION — Rods are photoreceptors containing rhodopsin (visual purple), a derivative of vitamin A. They function in dim light (scotopic vision) and contain only this one pigment, so they cannot distinguish colours.
• DEFINITION — Cones serve daylight, or photopic, vision and colour vision. They are of three types, with photopigments tuned to red, green and blue light; sensations of different colours arise from combinations in various proportions. Equal stimulation of all three gives white.
• DEFINITION — The optic nerves leave the eye and the retinal blood vessels enter it at a point slightly above the posterior pole of the eyeball. Photoreceptor cells are absent there, so no image is formed at that spot — the blind spot.
• DEFINITION — At the posterior pole, lateral to the blind spot, lies a yellowish pigmented spot, the macula lutea, with a central pit, the fovea — where only cones are densely packed, the seat of sharpest vision.
✔ KEY POINT — The retina’s division of labour: rods for dim light with rhodopsin and no colour; cones for daylight and colour, densest at the fovea. Blind spot has neither cell; fovea has cones only. Light in, impulses out through the optic nerve.
• EXAMPLE — Walk from bright sunlight into a dark cinema: for minutes you see almost nothing, then shapes emerge — rods taking over as cones fall silent. Colours never return until the lights rise: chemistry, not effort, decides what the dark allows you to see.
• WORKED PATTERN — Receptor questions give a lighting condition and ask for the cell: dim means rods and rhodopsin; bright or colour means cones and their three types. Location questions pair blind spot (no receptors) against fovea (cones only, sharpest vision).
• YOUR TURN — A child with chronic vitamin A deficiency struggles to see in dim light but manages in daylight. Explain. Answer: rhodopsin is derived from vitamin A; deficiency starves the rods first, so night blindness appears while cone-based daylight vision initially survives.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The blind spot lacks BOTH rods and cones; the fovea contains ONLY cones; the rest of the retina mixes both. Options giving the fovea rods, or the blind spot a few photoreceptors, fail this exact map.

Image Formation — How the Eye Builds a Picture
• DEFINITION — Visible light reflected from an object enters the eye through the cornea, passes the pupil and lens, and the lens focuses an image on the retina. The image formed there is inverted.
• DEFINITION — Light induces dissociation of the retinal pigments of the photoreceptors, creating changes in membrane permeability. Potential differences arise in the photoreceptor cells, producing a signal: action potentials in the ganglion cells through the bipolar cells.
• DEFINITION — The action potentials travel through the optic nerves to the visual area of the cerebral cortex, where the impulses are analysed and the image formed on the retina is recognised.
✔ KEY POINT — The visual pathway, link by link: photoreceptors (transduce light), bipolar cells (relay), ganglion cells (generate action potentials), optic nerve (transmit), visual cortex (recognise). The image on the retina is inverted; perception is not.
• WHY the world does not look upside down — “seeing” is the cortex interpreting a pattern of impulses, not a photograph being viewed. The brain learned in infancy which retinal pattern means “up”; consistency, not the image’s orientation, is what perception needs.
• EXAMPLE — A baby and a scholar receive the same inverted pattern on the retina; only the scholar reads the page. The difference is entirely cortical training — evidence that vision finishes in the brain, not in the eye.
• WORKED PATTERN — Use the relay logic: the signal changes form at each step — light energy, pigment chemistry, membrane potential, ganglion-cell impulses, cortical recognition. A question asking “where does X happen” is really asking which form the signal has there.
• YOUR TURN — A patient’s eyes, lenses and retinas test normal, yet the patient cannot recognise faces. Where is the fault most likely? Answer: the visual area of the cerebral cortex — the image is formed and transmitted correctly, but recognition is a cortical act.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The retinal image is INVERTED — “the lens forms an erect image on the retina” is false; erectness exists only in perception, after cortical processing. The trap also runs backwards: impulses, not images, travel through the optic nerve.

The Human Ear — Outer, Middle and Inner
• DEFINITION — The ear performs two sensory jobs: hearing, and maintenance of body balance. Anatomically it divides into outer ear (collection), middle ear (transmission) and inner ear (conversion) — one organ, two senses.
• DEFINITION — The outer ear consists of the pinna, the flap that collects sound waves, and the external auditory meatus (ear canal), which conducts them to the tympanic membrane (eardrum).
• DEFINITION — The middle ear contains three ossicles in a chain: malleus (hammer), incus (anvil) and stapes (stirrup). The malleus is attached to the tympanic membrane; the stapes is attached to the oval window of the cochlea. The chain transmits — and effectively amplifies — the eardrum’s vibrations.
• DEFINITION — The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear cavity with the pharynx and helps equalise the pressures on either side of the eardrum, keeping the membrane free to vibrate. A blocked tube means a bowed, stiff drum.
• DEFINITION — The inner ear, the labyrinth, lies deep in the temporal bone: a bony labyrinth inside which sits the membranous labyrinth, surrounded by perilymph and filled with endolymph. It houses the cochlea and vestibular apparatus.
✔ KEY POINT — The ear’s one-line map: pinna collects, canal conducts, eardrum vibrates, ossicles transmit (malleus, incus, stapes), oval window drives the cochlea, organ of Corti transduces, auditory nerve reports, cortex hears.
• EXAMPLE — Descending in an aeroplane, outside pressure rises faster than middle-ear pressure and the eardrum bows painfully inward — until a swallow opens the Eustachian tube, air enters, pressures equalise, and the drum snaps back. One tube, felt by every flyer.
• WORKED PATTERN — Ear questions are path questions in costume: recite the chain pinna, canal, eardrum, malleus, incus, stapes, oval window, cochlea, auditory nerve. Any option breaking the chain’s order, or skipping the ossicles, is wrong regardless of its other truths.
• YOUR TURN — Wax completely blocks one ear canal. What remains intact, and what fails? Answer: the ossicles, cochlea and nerve are untouched, but airborne sound cannot reach the eardrum, so hearing on that side is muffled. The lesion is in conduction, not in the sensor.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The stapes attaches to the OVAL window of the cochlea — never the round window, which belongs to the scala tympani and only relieves pressure. Swapping the windows is a standard one-mark trap.

The Cochlea and Organ of Corti — Turning Sound into Signals
• DEFINITION — Two membranes divide the cochlea along its length: Reissner’s membrane above and the basilar membrane below — creating scala vestibuli above, scala media in the middle (endolymph), and scala tympani below (perilymph).
• DEFINITION — At the base of the cochlea, the scala vestibuli ends at the oval window; the scala tympani terminates at the round window, opening to the middle ear. Pressure entering at one window is relieved at the other.
• DEFINITION — The organ of Corti is the true hearing apparatus: a structure on the basilar membrane containing hair cells that act as auditory receptors, their bases in close contact with afferent nerve fibres.
• DEFINITION — Above the hair cells lies a thin, elastic tectorial membrane. When the basilar membrane ripples, the hair cells bend against it; this mechanical bending is the event that converts vibration into neural signal.
✔ KEY POINT — The conversion chain: stapes pushes the oval window, perilymph carries the pressure, the basilar membrane ripples, hair cells of the organ of Corti bend against the tectorial membrane, impulses arise in afferent fibres. Transduction lives in the bending.
• WHY hair cells, not the membranes, are the receptors — membranes and fluids only move; a receptor must translate movement into impulses. Hair cells alone touch afferent fibres and change their firing when bent — translation happens exactly there.
• EXAMPLE — A tuning fork and a whispered secret differ in loudness, yet both end as hair cells bending on the organ of Corti. The entire outer and middle ear exist to deliver those two very different vibrations faithfully to the same small strip of tissue.
• WORKED PATTERN — Triangulate on three memberships: Reissner’s membrane bounds the scala media above, basilar below; endolymph fills the scala media, perilymph the other two; the organ of Corti sits on the basilar membrane under the tectorial membrane.
• YOUR TURN — A drug selectively destroys cochlear hair cells while leaving membranes, fluids and nerve intact. Predict the deficit. Answer: profound deafness despite a mechanically perfect ear — vibrations arrive, but no cell converts them into impulses.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The organ of Corti rests on the BASILAR membrane (not Reissner’s), and the tectorial membrane lies above the hair cells. Also, the scala media holds ENDOlymph while the scalae vestibuli and tympani hold PERIlymph — swapping the two fluids is a yearly plant.

Balance — Semicircular Canals and the Vestibular Apparatus
• DEFINITION — The vestibular apparatus is the balance organ of the inner ear, lying above the cochlea. It consists of three semicircular canals and the otolith organs — the saccule and utricle.
• DEFINITION — The three semicircular canals lie in different planes, so rotation in any direction moves fluid in at least one of them. Each is filled with endolymph; its swollen base, the ampulla, contains a projecting ridge, the crista ampullaris, bearing hair cells.
• DEFINITION — The otolith organs work at rest as well: the saccule and utricle contain a projecting ridge called a macula. Crista and macula are the vestibular receptors — cristae for rotation, maculae for position.
✔ KEY POINT — Balance has two receptor systems with a clean split: crista ampullaris in each semicircular canal’s ampulla senses rotation; macula in the saccule and utricle senses position and linear movement. Both belong to the vestibular apparatus, above the cochlea.
• EXAMPLE — Spin and stop suddenly: the world seems to keep rotating. The endolymph in your semicircular canals, set moving by the spin, keeps flowing briefly after you stop, bending the cristae and reporting a rotation that is no longer happening — the mismatch is the dizziness.
• WORKED PATTERN — Balance questions ask which receptor, not which organ: turning or rotating means crista ampullaris in a semicircular canal; tilting, posture or straight-line acceleration means macula in saccule or utricle. Both live in the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear.
• YOUR TURN — Standing on one leg with eyes closed, which vestibular receptors report your slight head tilts? Answer: the maculae of the saccule and utricle — the head is not rotating, so cristae are quiet; position sense is the otolith organs’ job.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The vestibular apparatus belongs to the INNER ear — options placing balance receptors in the middle ear (with the ossicles) confuse transmission with sensing. The middle ear conducts; only the inner ear senses.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Organisation: CNS (brain + spinal cord) processes; PNS connects. Afferent fibres carry impulses to the CNS, efferent away from it. Somatic serves skeletal muscle; autonomic serves involuntary organs and smooth muscle; sympathetic mobilises, parasympathetic restores.
✔ KEY POINT — Neuron: cell body (nucleus + Nissl granules) receives and maintains; dendrites (Nissl granules) bring impulses in; axon (no Nissl granules) carries them away to synaptic knobs holding vesicles of neurotransmitter.
✔ KEY POINT — Myelin: sheath built by Schwann cells in the PNS and by oligodendrocytes in the CNS; gaps are nodes of Ranvier, where regeneration happens. Unmyelinated fibres are Schwann-wrapped but unsheathed.
✔ KEY POINT — Resting potential: about −70 mV, inside negative. The resting membrane is highly permeable to K+, nearly impermeable to Na+, and impermeable to the trapped negative proteins of the axoplasm.
✔ KEY POINT — The sodium-potassium pump maintains the gradients using ATP: 3 Na+ transported out for every 2 K+ transported in.
✔ KEY POINT — Action potential: a threshold stimulus makes the membrane freely permeable to Na+; Na+ influx depolarises it (inside positive), then K+ efflux repolarises it. The spike is brief and regenerated patch by patch.
✔ KEY POINT — Saltatory conduction: in myelinated fibres the action potential is regenerated only at nodes of Ranvier and leaps node to node — faster and more economical than point-by-point continuous conduction in bare fibres.
✔ KEY POINT — Synapses: electrical (membranes nearly touching, current flows directly, faster) versus chemical (fluid-filled cleft, neurotransmitter, slower but controllable). Chemical chain: impulse arrives, vesicles fuse, transmitter crosses, binds receptors, channels open, new potential arises.
✔ KEY POINT — Protection: brain inside the cranium, wrapped in meninges — dura mater (outer), arachnoid (thin middle), pia mater (inner, touching brain). Brain stem = midbrain + hindbrain.
✔ KEY POINT — Forebrain map: cerebrum (grey cortex — motor, sensory and association areas for memory and communication; corpus callosum links hemispheres), thalamus (sensory-motor relay), hypothalamus (temperature, hunger, thirst, hormones), limbic system (amygdala, hippocampus — emotion and motivation).
✔ KEY POINT — Midbrain and hindbrain: corpora quadrigemina are four dorsal lobes of the midbrain. Hindbrain: pons (fibre tracts interconnecting brain parts), cerebellum (posture, balance, precision), medulla (respiration, cardiovascular reflexes, gastric secretions).
✔ KEY POINT — Reflex arc, five links in order: receptor, sensory (afferent) neuron, interneuron in the spinal cord, motor (efferent) neuron, effector. Entry by the dorsal root, exit by the ventral root; the brain is informed afterwards.
✔ KEY POINT — Eye wall: sclera outside (transparent cornea in front), choroid in the middle (vessels; ciliary body and iris with pupil in front), retina inside. Path of light: cornea, aqueous humour, pupil, lens, vitreous humour, retina — where an inverted image is focused.
✔ KEY POINT — Retina: layers from inside are ganglion cells, bipolar cells, photoreceptors. Rods (rhodopsin, dim light, no colour); cones (three types — red, green, blue; daylight and colour). Blind spot: optic disc, no photoreceptors. Fovea: cones only, sharpest vision.
✔ KEY POINT — Ear: pinna and canal deliver vibrations to the eardrum; ossicles malleus, incus, stapes pass them to the oval window; perilymph ripples the basilar membrane; hair cells of the organ of Corti transduce; the auditory nerve reports to the cortex. Eustachian tube equalises pressure.
✔ KEY POINT — Balance: in the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear, the crista ampullaris of each semicircular canal senses rotation, and the macula of the saccule and utricle senses position and linear movement.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Writing the pump ratio as 3 K+ out for 2 Na+ in. Why it is wrong: that reversed pump would drag positive charge inwards and fight the resting potential. Correct principle: the pump exports 3 Na+ and imports 2 K+ per ATP, a net export of one positive charge per cycle.
• Mistake 2: Crediting K+ influx with depolarisation. Why it is wrong: K+ is far more concentrated inside, so its gradient drives it outward, and depolarisation needs positive charge entering. Correct principle: Na+ influx depolarises; K+ efflux repolarises.
• Mistake 3: Placing Nissl granules in the axon. Why it is wrong: granules are the cell body’s protein machinery; the axon is a transmission cable maintained from the soma. Correct principle: Nissl granules occur in the cell body and dendrites, never in the axon.
• Mistake 4: Calling the electrical synapse the slow one. Why it is wrong: direct current flow has no chemical middleman to wait for. Correct principle: electrical transmission is faster; chemical transmission is slower but can be gated, graded and inhibited.
• Mistake 5: Releasing neurotransmitter from the post-synaptic side. Why it is wrong: the post-synaptic membrane holds receptors, not vesicles. Correct principle: vesicles and release belong to the pre-synaptic knob; binding and channel opening belong to the post-synaptic membrane.
• Mistake 6: Saying the impulse travels through the myelin sheath. Why it is wrong: the sheath is inert insulation; no ion exchange happens beneath it. Correct principle: the impulse is regenerated at the nodes of Ranvier and leaps between them — saltatory conduction in the axon, under the insulation.
• Mistake 7: Making the brain the controller of the spinal reflex. Why it is wrong: the defining feature of the spinal reflex arc is that it completes in the cord; the brain is informed afterwards. Correct principle: receptor, sensory neuron, interneuron, motor neuron, effector — all within the cord’s loop.
• Mistake 8: Treating the cornea as a separate layer and the iris as part of the sclera. Why it is wrong: the wall has exactly three layers. Correct principle: cornea is the sclera’s transparent front; iris and ciliary body belong to the choroid layer.
• Mistake 9: Reversing the ossicle chain or attaching the stapes to the round window. Why it is wrong: vibration enters the cochlea at the oval window; the round window only relieves pressure. Correct principle: malleus (on the eardrum), incus, stapes (on the oval window) — in that order, always.
• Mistake 10: Placing the organ of Corti on Reissner’s membrane. Why it is wrong: Reissner’s membrane is the roof of the scala media; the organ of Corti stands on its floor. Correct principle: organ of Corti on the BASILAR membrane, hair cells bending against the tectorial membrane above them.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A patch of axonal membrane runs its sodium-potassium pump steadily and exports 900 Na+ ions. (a) How many pump cycles ran? (b) How many K+ ions were imported in the same time? (c) What is the net charge movement across the membrane, and why does it matter?
• Given: Pump stoichiometry — each cycle transports 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in, consuming one ATP. Total Na+ expelled: 900.
• Principle: Cycles are counted by the fixed ratio; divide the Na+ total by 3, then multiply the cycles by 2 for K+.
• Steps: (a) 900 divided by 3 equals 300 cycles, so 300 ATP were used. (b) 300 cycles times 2 equals 600 K+ imported. (c) Each cycle exports one net positive charge — 300 net positive charges left the cell.
• Answer: (a) 300 cycles. (b) 600 K+ ions in. (c) A net export of 300 positive charges — one net positive charge per cycle.
• Reasoning: The arithmetic shows why the pump is electrogenic: one extra positive charge exported per cycle directly reinforces the inside-negative resting potential.
• Mistake to avoid: Using the reversed ratio (3 K+ out, 2 Na+ in) — that would import net positive charge and fight the resting potential instead of building it.
• Example 2 — Problem: On one axon, patch B (in the middle) has just depolarised. Patch A, behind it, is repolarising; patch C, ahead of it, is still at rest. Using local currents, predict where the action potential appears next, and explain why it does not reappear at A immediately.
• Given: Three consecutive patches: A restoring (K+ leaving, polarity returning), B just fired (inside positive), C resting (inside negative, excitable).
• Principle: Currents flow in circuits between patches of opposite charge: inside the axon from depolarised towards resting membrane; outside, the reverse. The resting patch ahead is thereby depolarised; the patch behind is repolarising and cannot yet fire again.
• Steps: Inside the axoplasm, positive charge flows from B towards C; outside, current flows from C back towards B. The inside flow depolarises C to threshold, its Na+ channels open, and C fires. A is mid-repolarisation, K+ streaming out — so B’s backward current cannot re-trigger it at once.
• Answer: The action potential appears next at C. The impulse advances from B to C — and keeps advancing away from the just-fired territory.
• Reasoning: One-way travel needs no traffic police: the territory ahead is excitable, the territory behind temporarily unresponsive while it restores its polarity. Direction follows from timing.
• Mistake to avoid: Drawing the inside current from the resting patch towards the fired patch — inside current flows from the depolarised site towards the resting site, and that flow depolarises the next patch.
• Example 3 — Problem: Two axons are each 100 mm long. Axon M is myelinated with nodes every 1 mm; axon U is unmyelinated and regenerates its action potential at every successive membrane patch. (a) Compare their regeneration counts. (b) Hence compare their conduction time and ATP cost.
• Given: Length 100 mm for both; nodes every 1 mm in M; U regenerates at every successive membrane patch.
• Principle: Regeneration happens only where ion exchange is possible: at nodes in a myelinated fibre, at every patch in an unmyelinated one. Speed and cost both scale with the number of regenerations.
• Steps: (a) M regenerates about 100 divided by 1 — roughly 100 times, at nodes. U regenerates continuously, at effectively thousands of successive patches. (b) A hundred slow steps against thousands: M’s impulse finishes far sooner, and with far fewer ions crossing, U’s pump spends much more ATP per impulse.
• Answer: (a) About 100 regenerations in M versus continuous regeneration at thousands of points in U. (b) M is much faster and spends far less ATP per impulse; U is slower and costlier on both counts.
• Reasoning: The commercial case for myelin as accounting: insulation converts an expensive point-by-point relay into a cheap series of leaps, shrinking the time sheet and the ATP bill together.
• Mistake to avoid: Saying M is faster because “the impulse travels faster inside the myelin” — the impulse is not inside the myelin; it is regenerated at the nodes, and the saving is in the number of regenerations.
• Example 4 — Problem: These events are out of order: (i) transmitter binds post-synaptic receptors; (ii) an action potential arrives at the terminal; (iii) vesicles fuse and release transmitter; (iv) ion channels open in the post-synaptic membrane; (v) transmitter diffuses across the cleft. Arrange them and name the synapse type.
• Given: Five events involving vesicles, a cleft and post-synaptic receptors.
• Principle: Cause must precede effect along the chain: arrival triggers release; release precedes diffusion; diffusion precedes binding; binding precedes channel opening.
• Steps: First (ii) — nothing starts until the impulse reaches the terminal. Then (iii) — vesicles cannot fuse before the signal arrives. Then (v) — the transmitter must cross the cleft before it can act. Then (i) — binding. Finally (iv) — the binding is what opens the post-synaptic channels.
• Answer: The order is (ii), (iii), (v), (i), (iv). This is a chemical synapse — the evidence is the vesicles, the fluid-filled cleft and receptor-mediated channel opening, none of which exists in an electrical synapse.
• Reasoning: Two landmarks lock the sequence: vesicle fusion cannot precede the impulse’s arrival, and post-synaptic channels cannot open before binding. Find those anchors first in any ordering question.
• Mistake to avoid: Placing diffusion after binding — a transmitter cannot bind a receptor it has not reached; it must cross the cleft first. Also avoid calling this electrical merely because an action potential is involved at the start.
• Example 5 — Problem: A student grips a metal vessel that is far hotter than expected. The hand jerks away at once; the feeling of pain arrives a moment later. (a) Name the five links of the pathway producing the jerk, in order. (b) Explain why the pain is perceived after the withdrawal.
• Given: Stimulus — excessive heat at the fingertips. Response 1 — immediate withdrawal. Response 2 — conscious pain, slightly delayed.
• Principle: A reflex arc runs receptor to sensory neuron to spinal cord interneuron to motor neuron to effector. Conscious perception requires the longer ascending path to the brain, so it completes later.
• Steps: Heat receptors in the fingertip fire. The sensory neuron carries impulses into the cord through the dorsal root. The signal passes, directly or via an interneuron, to a motor neuron, whose fibre leaves by the ventral root to the arm muscles, which contract — the hand is withdrawn. In parallel, signals ascend to the brain, where pain is perceived slightly later.
• Answer: Receptor (skin heat receptors), sensory neuron, interneuron in the spinal cord, motor neuron, effector (arm muscles). The withdrawal is spinal and immediate; the pain is cerebral and necessarily second.
• Reasoning: Both systems are honestly at work — the cord optimising for speed of rescue, the brain for understanding. The favourite exam twist (which came first?) is answered by path length.
• Mistake to avoid: Routing the reflex “up to the brain and back” — that detour destroys the reflex’s only advantage. Also, the effector here is muscle, not a gland; choose the effector the response actually uses.
• Example 6 — Problem: (a) In a dim room you can see your friend’s face but cannot tell the colour of their shirt. (b) Reading, you see sharply only the few words you fixate. (c) A small object vanishes when its image falls on one particular retinal spot. Explain all three from retinal anatomy.
• Given: Three phenomena — colour loss in dim light, a narrow zone of sharpness, a vanishing spot.
• Principle: Rods serve dim light without colour; cones serve colour and detail and are concentrated maximally at the fovea; the blind spot has no photoreceptors at all.
• Steps: (a) In dim light only rods are effectively stimulated, and rods carry no colour information — shapes remain, colours vanish. (b) Sharp vision requires the fovea, the cone-only pit of the macula lutea; only fixated words fall on it, the rest of the page on cone-poor peripheral retina, and blurs. (c) On the optic disc, photoreceptors are absent, so no signal arises.
• Answer: (a) Rods alone in dim light — scotopic vision is colourless. (b) The fovea’s dense cones give maximal acuity only at the fixation point. (c) The image landed on the blind spot, which cannot respond.
• Reasoning: One anatomical map explains three unrelated tricks: cell type decides colour, cell density decides sharpness, cell absence decides the hole.
• Mistake to avoid: Attributing dim-light colour loss to the cones “resting” — the point is which system is ACTIVE: rods, which never encode colour. And the fovea contains cones only, not a mixture of both cell types.
• Example 7 — Problem: Trace a pressure wave from a teacher’s voice to the moment the brain registers sound, naming every structure in order. Then explain why a passenger’s ears ache during an aeroplane’s descent and why swallowing relieves it.
• Given: Airborne sound; a listener with normal ears; descent raises external air pressure quickly.
• Principle: Sound pathway — pinna, canal, eardrum, ossicles, oval window, cochlear fluids, basilar membrane, hair cells, auditory nerve, cortex. The descent symptom belongs to pressure equalisation, not the pathway.
• Steps: Pinna collects the waves; the meatus conducts them to the tympanic membrane, which vibrates. Malleus passes the vibration to incus, then stapes, whose footplate drives the oval window. Vibrations ripple the basilar membrane and bend the hair cells of the organ of Corti; impulses reach the auditory cortex via the auditory nerve. In descent, swallowing opens the Eustachian tube and the pressure difference — the pain — disappears.
• Answer: Pinna to auditory meatus to tympanic membrane to malleus to incus to stapes to oval window to cochlear perilymph to hair cells of the organ of Corti to auditory nerve to auditory cortex. The descent pain is a pressure problem (a bowed eardrum), fixed only by the Eustachian tube.
• Reasoning: The halves test the ear’s two systems separately — the conducting-and-converting chain for hearing, and the plumbing keeping its first membrane free to vibrate.
• Mistake to avoid: Ending the pathway at the cochlea or the eardrum — hearing is completed only when impulses are interpreted in the auditory cortex. Stopping at transduction is one link short.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. One pump cycle transports 3 Na+ out of the cell and 2 K+ into the cell, powered by ATP. It maintains the gradients (high K+ inside, high Na+ outside) and exports one net positive charge per cycle, helping keep the inside negative. Misconception watch: the pump does not create the potential single-handedly — K+ leak builds most of it.
• A2. First, the resting membrane is comparatively highly permeable to K+, so K+ diffuses out, leaving unmatched negative charge behind. Second, it is nearly impermeable to Na+, so outside Na+ cannot cancel the separation. Third, negatively charged proteins are trapped inside. Misconception watch: negativity is what remains when K+ leaves.
• A3. Schwann cells form myelin in the PNS; oligodendrocytes in the CNS. The gaps are the nodes of Ranvier. They speed conduction because regeneration is needed only at these exposed nodes — the impulse leaps between them (saltatory conduction) instead of being rebuilt at every patch. Misconception watch: Schwann cells also wrap unmyelinated fibres.
• A4. The sequence is (ii) threshold stimulus, (iii) Na+ rushes in, (v) the site becomes depolarised, (i) K+ diffuses out, (iv) resting potential restored. Misconception watch: (i) cannot precede (iii) — K+ efflux is the reset, not the trigger, and it starts only after the Na+ influx has reversed the polarity.
• A5. First, synaptic vesicles exist only in the pre-synaptic knob, so only the pre-synaptic side can send. Second, the specific receptors exist only on the post-synaptic membrane, so only that side can receive. Misconception watch: the block is structural, not “one-way fluid”.
• A6. (a) Hypothalamus — temperature, eating and drinking urges. (b) Thalamus — relay for sensory and motor signalling. (c) Limbic system — amygdala, hippocampus and partners: emotion and motivation. (d) Cerebrum — association areas (memory, communication) and motor areas (voluntary commands). Misconception watch: thalamus routes, hypothalamus regulates.
• A7. Rods contain rhodopsin (a vitamin A derivative), work in dim light, and give no colour sensation. Cones are of three types tuned to red, green and blue, work in daylight, and give colour vision. Vitamin A deficiency starves the rods first: night blindness appears while daylight vision initially remains usable. Misconception watch: it is a rod pigment failure, not a lens defect.
• A8. Pinna to external auditory meatus to tympanic membrane to malleus to incus to stapes to oval window to perilymph to basilar membrane to hair cells of the organ of Corti to afferent fibres and the auditory nerve to the auditory cortex. Impulses are generated at the hair cells. Misconception watch: eardrum and ossicles only transmit vibration.

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: The neuron is built for distance and direction — dendrites and cell body (with Nissl granules) receive and maintain, the axon transmits, the knob hands over. At rest the membrane holds about −70 mV, built by selective permeability and the 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in pump; a threshold stimulus discharges it as Na+ influx, K+ efflux resets it, and local currents march the impulse along — leaping node to node where myelin insulates it.
• Summary: At the synapse the signal changes currency: chemical synapses release transmitter (acetylcholine the model) from pre-synaptic vesicles onto post-synaptic receptors, generating excitatory or inhibitory potentials; electrical synapses pass current directly and faster. One-way traffic follows from one-sided anatomy.

• Summary: The eye and ear translate the physical world into the same electrical language. The eye focuses an inverted image on a retina of rods (dim light, no colour) and cones (colour, foveal sharpness), avoiding the blind spot where its nerve exits. The ear funnels vibration through the ossicles into the cochlea, where hair cells of the organ of Corti transduce it.
