Introduction — The Smallest Workers Run the Biggest Factories
• DEFINITION — Microbes, or microorganisms, are organisms that cannot be seen with the unaided eye, only under a microscope. They are major components of earth's biological systems, and this chapter is the balance sheet of their usefulness: foods they make, factories that run on them, sewage they clean, fuel they generate, crops they protect and feed.
• WHY they are inescapable — microbes are found practically everywhere: in soil, water and air, inside our bodies and inside other animals and plants. They are present even where no other life form could possibly exist — deep inside geysers and thermal vents where temperatures may be as high as 100 degrees C, deep in the soil, under thick layers of snow, and in highly acidic environments.
• DEFINITION — Microbes are a diverse group: protozoa, bacteria, fungi and microscopic plants, along with viruses, viroids and prions. Viroids and viruses are infectious particles simpler than bacteria, and prions are proteinaceous infectious agents — proteins that infect without any nucleic acid.
• EXAMPLE — The simplest sample for seeing microbes is a spoonful of curd from your kitchen: under a microscope it teems with lactic acid bacteria — millions in a single drop — proof that the most familiar microbe laboratory is your own home.
• WORKED PATTERN — Read the chapter as five arenas: (1) the home — curd, dough, cheese, beverages; (2) the factory — antibiotics, acids, enzymes, statins; (3) the city — sewage; (4) the farm's shield — biocontrol; (5) the farm's feed — biofertilisers, with biogas as the energy thread. Every question is secretly asking which arena you are in.
✔ KEY POINT — Microbes are everywhere and diverse (bacteria, fungi, protozoa, viruses, viroids, prions) — and mostly not enemies: harnessed, they produce food, medicines, fuel and fertiliser while cleaning our waste.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The mindset "microbes are harmful germs" is what this chapter exists to break. Options treating every microbe as a pathogen, or claiming microbes live only in dirty places, are wrong: they occupy every habitat on earth, including healthy bodies.
• YOUR TURN — Which arena does Swiss cheese belong to, and who is its worker? Answer: the home arena — Propionibacterium sharmanii, whose carbon dioxide makes the holes.

Microbes Are Everywhere — A Diversity Census
• DEFINITION — A colony is the visible mass that grows when microbes multiply on a nutritive medium. Bacteria and fungi can be grown on nutritive media in the laboratory to form colonies visible to the naked eye, and such pure cultures are extremely useful in studying microbes — each colony is millions of descendants of a few starting cells, all doing identical chemistry.
• The census itself: bacteria are the smallest cellular microbes and the most numerous; fungi range from single-celled yeasts to moulds on stale bread; protozoa are microscopic animal-like organisms of water and soil; microscopic plant-like forms complete the cellular list. Alongside stand the non-cellular agents: viruses, inert outside a host cell, and the simpler viroids and prions.
• WHY "grown on media" divides the census — a bacterium or fungus on nutrient agar feeds, divides and builds a colony you can photograph. A virus on the same plate does nothing: it has no metabolism to feed and multiplies only inside a living host cell, so it fails the culture test though counted among microbes.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sort any infectious agent in two moves: (1) Cellular? Bacteria, fungi, protozoa — yes. (2) Sub-cellular? Then choose: virus (nucleic acid in a protein coat), viroid (a smaller infectious particle), or prion (proteinaceous infectious agent, no nucleic acid at all). The prion is the only census entry with no genetic material — a favourite one-line question.
• EXAMPLE — The mould patches spreading on stale bread are colonies: millions of fungal cells grown from single landed spores, visible with no microscope at all.
• YOUR TURN — Pond water plated on nutrient agar yields bacterial and fungal colonies but never viral colonies. Is the pond virus-free? Answer: no — the test is blind to viruses, which are obligate intracellular agents and need living host cells on the plate to multiply.
✔ KEY POINT — Bacteria and fungi form visible colonies on nutritive media and can be cultured and studied; viruses, viroids and prions cannot, and prions are unique as proteinaceous infectious agents with no nucleic acid.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not smuggle prions into "microorganisms with DNA", and do not call a single bacterium visible: only its colony — millions of cells — can be seen with the naked eye.

Curd — The LAB Story in Your Kitchen
• DEFINITION — LAB stands for lactic acid bacteria, the group typified by Lactobacillus. LAB grow in milk and convert it into curd: they produce acids that coagulate and partially digest the milk proteins, chiefly casein, turning liquid milk into set curd.
• The process, step by step: fresh milk is warmed and a little curd is added as the inoculum or starter. That spoonful is not a flavouring — it contains millions of LAB. At suitable temperatures they multiply, ferment the milk sugar lactose into lactic acid, and the rising acidity coagulates and partially digests the milk proteins until the mass sets as curd.
• The nutritional upgrade: LAB improve curd's nutritional quality by increasing its vitamin B12 content. Curd is enriched milk, not merely preserved milk — and "vitamin B12 rises" is a frequently asked one-liner.
• The bodyguard role: in our stomach, LAB play a very beneficial role in checking disease-causing microbes. Their acid and sheer occupancy make the gut harder for pathogens to colonise — curd is traditional advice during stomach upsets for this reason.
• WHY the inoculum must be alive and the milk only warm — curd setting is population growth, not test-tube chemistry. Boiling-hot milk kills the starter's LAB; refrigerated milk keeps them too sluggish to multiply. They need warmth and time to reach numbers at which their acid output sets the milk.
• EXAMPLE — Dosa batter and the curd pot obey the same law: both are bacterial fermentations whose speed rises with warmth — batter ferments overnight in Chennai but may need a day and a half in a cold hill-station house.
• WORKED PATTERN — Three facts, one microbe, one chain: LAB ferment lactose to lactic acid; the acid coagulates and partially digests casein; vitamin B12 rises. "Which vitamin?" — B12. "What do LAB produce?" — lactic acid. "What does the acid do?" — coagulates the proteins. Answer the chain in any order.
• YOUR TURN — A cook adds a spoon of curd to milk straight off the boil and hours later finds only thin, sour milk. What went wrong? Answer: the near-boiling milk killed the LAB in the inoculum, so too few bacteria survived to acidify and set the milk. Add the starter to warm milk, not hot.
✔ KEY POINT — Lactobacillus and other LAB convert milk to curd: lactic acid coagulates and partially digests casein, a spoonful of curd is a living inoculum of millions of LAB, vitamin B12 rises, and LAB in the stomach check disease-causing microbes.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The vitamin is B12, not B2 or C, and the organism is a bacterium (Lactobacillus), not a yeast or fungus. Yeast belongs to bread and beer; Lactobacillus belongs to curd.

Dough, Toddy and Cheese — Fermentation on the Plate
• Dough that rises is gas made visible. The dough used for dosa and idli is fermented by bacteria, and its puffed-up appearance is due to the production of carbon dioxide gas. Bread obeys the same physics: its dough is fermented using baker's yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, whose carbon dioxide is trapped in the dough and lifts it.
• Toddy is the chapter's regional entry: a traditional drink of some parts of southern India, made by fermenting sap tapped from palm trees — no distillery, just sap fermenting with its natural microbes.
• DEFINITION — Cheese is one of the oldest food items in which microbes were used, and it is a family, not a single food: each variety's texture, flavour and taste — its specificity — comes from the microbes used to make it. Change the microbe, change the cheese.
• WHY Swiss cheese has holes — the large holes are due to a large amount of carbon dioxide produced by a bacterium named Propionibacterium sharmanii. The gas pockets inside the ripening cheese are the famous "eyes", big because this bacterium is a heavy carbon dioxide producer.
• The Roquefort signature: Roquefort cheese is ripened by growing a specific fungus on it, giving it its particular flavour. Hold the division of labour examiners love: in Swiss cheese a bacterium makes the gas and holes; in Roquefort a fungus ripens and flavours.
• EXAMPLE — Two wheels of cheese leave the same dairy; one full of holes, one veined and sharp: the first met Propionibacterium sharmanii, the second a Roquefort fungus. Same milk — the microbe wrote the difference.
• WORKED PATTERN — The household matching drill, answerable in five seconds each: curd — Lactobacillus (LAB); dosa and idli batter — bacteria (gas puffs it); bread — baker's yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae; Swiss cheese holes — Propionibacterium sharmanii (carbon dioxide); Roquefort flavour — a specific fungus; toddy — fermented palm sap. Six pairings cover the whole household arena.
• YOUR TURN — A baker and a cheesemaker both credit "the gas" for their product's texture. Name each gas-maker. Answer: in the bread, Saccharomyces cerevisiae fermenting the dough; in the Swiss cheese, Propionibacterium sharmanii ripening the cheese. Same gas, carbon dioxide; different microbes, different stages.
✔ KEY POINT — Dosa and idli dough is puffed by bacterial carbon dioxide, bread dough by baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), toddy is fermented palm sap, Swiss cheese holes come from carbon dioxide released by Propionibacterium sharmanii, and Roquefort is ripened by a specific fungus.
★ HIGHLIGHT — NCERT spells the Swiss-cheese bacterium Propionibacterium sharmanii — with "sharmanii", exactly as printed in the textbook. Examiners copy NCERT's spelling into their options, so learn the name in NCERT's own letters.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The holes in Swiss cheese are made by a bacterium producing carbon dioxide — not by a fungus, and not by methane or oxygen. And the Roquefort worker is a fungus, not a bacterium: the two cheeses are a matched pair of opposite traps.

Fermented Beverages — Brewer's Yeast and the Distillation Line
• DEFINITION — Saccharomyces cerevisiae, commonly called brewer's yeast, is used for fermenting malted cereals and fruit juices to produce ethanol. The same species, under its other trade name of baker's yeast, raises your bread — one organism, two industries, and the fermentation is the anaerobic one you met in the respiration chapter.
• The beverage list splits into two families. Wine and beer are produced without distillation — the fermented liquid is itself the drink. Whisky, brandy and rum are produced by distillation of the fermented broth — fermented by the same yeast first, then distilled.
• WHY the split exists — fermentation cannot concentrate alcohol indefinitely, because ethanol is toxic to the yeast that makes it (recall yeast poisoning itself near 13 per cent alcohol). Wine and beer live under that ceiling; stronger drinks exist only because distillation concentrates the ethanol after fermentation ends.
• EXAMPLE — Follow a grape and a grain. Crushed grapes ferment into wine and stop there — wine is never distilled. Malted grain ferments into a beer-like broth; leave it and you have beer, but distil the broth and the same start becomes whisky. Brandy is distilled fermented fruit juice, rum a distilled sugarcane product — distillation changes the family.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify any beverage with one question: was the fermented broth distilled? No — wine or beer. Yes — whisky, brandy or rum. An option calling wine a distilled drink, or beer a product of distillation, is wrong whatever else it says.
• YOUR TURN — A label reads "fermented fruit juice, bottled without distillation". Wine or whisky? Answer: wine — whisky requires distillation of the fermented broth.
✔ KEY POINT — Saccharomyces cerevisiae (brewer's yeast) ferments malted cereals and fruit juices to ethanol: wine and beer without distillation; whisky, brandy and rum by distillation of the fermented broth.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Baker's yeast and brewer's yeast are the same species, Saccharomyces cerevisiae. Options assigning them to different species, or assigning ethanol production to Lactobacillus, are wrong: Lactobacillus makes lactic acid, never beverage ethanol.

Antibiotics — Fleming, a Mould and a War
• DEFINITION — Antibiotics are chemical substances produced by some microbes which can kill or retard the growth of other, disease-causing microbes. They are microbial weapons borrowed by medicine: one microbe's chemical defence becomes our drug.
• The discovery: Alexander Fleming, working on Staphylococci bacteria, observed a mould growing in one of his unwashed culture plates, around which the Staphylococci could not grow. The mould produced a chemical causing this effect; he named it penicillin, after the mould Penicillium notatum. Penicillin was the first antibiotic discovered.
• WHY discovery was not deployment — the full potential of penicillin was established much later by Ernst Chain and Howard Florey, who turned an observation into a producible drug. Penicillin was then extensively used to treat wounded American soldiers in World War II — the first war in which infected wounds stopped being the automatic killer they had always been.
• The honour ledger: Fleming, Chain and Florey were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1945. Learn the three roles — Fleming discovered, Chain and Florey established the full potential — because options routinely swap them.
• The impact list: antibiotics have greatly improved our capacity to treat deadly diseases such as plague, whooping cough, diphtheria and leprosy — NCERT's evidence quartet, a standard recall question.
• EXAMPLE — The unwashed plate is medicine's most famous accident: a Penicillium spore drifted onto a Staphylococcus plate, the mould grew, and a clear bacteria-free halo told the story — something diffusing from the mould stopped the bacteria. The halo was the data; penicillin the conclusion.
• WORKED PATTERN — Walk the timeline and every penicillin question settles: mould Penicillium notatum observed by Fleming on a Staphylococci plate; chemical named penicillin; full potential established by Chain and Florey; mass use on wounded American soldiers in World War II; Nobel Prize 1945 to all three. Five beats, in that order.
• YOUR TURN — A quiz claims penicillin was discovered by Chain and Florey and first used in World War I. Correct both errors. Answer: Fleming discovered it (Chain and Florey established its full potential later), and its extensive battlefield use was in World War II, not World War I.
✔ KEY POINT — Penicillin, the first antibiotic, was discovered by Fleming from the mould Penicillium notatum on a Staphylococci plate; Chain and Florey established its full potential; it treated wounded American soldiers in World War II; Nobel Prize 1945 to all three.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Penicillin comes from a fungus (a mould), not a bacterium — the chapter's most swapped kingdom. Antibiotics act on bacteria; penicillin was found precisely because bacteria (Staphylococci) could not grow near the mould.

Organic Acids and Ethanol — The Factory Floor
• Microbes work in industry on a large scale, and the first factory products to learn are the organic acids — four pairings NCERT lists together and examiners ask as direct matches. Treat this section as a table you must be able to reproduce from memory.
• The four pairings: citric acid from Aspergillus niger, a fungus. Acetic acid from Acetobacter aceti, a bacterium — the acid of vinegar, made by oxidising ethanol. Butyric acid from Clostridium butylicum, a bacterium — the rancid-butter acid. Lactic acid from Lactobacillus, a bacterium — the curd genus, here at industrial scale.
• DEFINITION — An industrial organic acid here is a carbon-based acid a microbe releases while metabolising its feedstock; this chapter's four are citric, acetic, butyric and lactic acid.
• EXAMPLE — Vinegar is a two-microbe relay: yeast ferments juice to ethanol, then Acetobacter aceti oxidises the ethanol to acetic acid.
• The pattern inside the table: only one producer is a fungus — Aspergillus niger; the other three are bacteria. In NCERT's order (citric, acetic, butyric, lactic) the producers run Aspergillus, Acetobacter, Clostridium, Lactobacillus — a sequence worth fixing, because scrambled match questions are built from it.
• Ethanol completes the factory floor: the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae, already met as brewer's yeast, is also used for the commercial production of ethanol, fermenting malted cereals and fruit juices. Industry and the beverage trade run on the same microbe; scale and purpose differ, the fermentation does not.
• WHY industry uses microbes for such simple molecules — a fermenter of the right microbe converts cheap feedstock into one desired product continuously, at ordinary temperatures, without harsh reagents. The microbe is a self-replicating catalyst that works for sugar.
• WORKED PATTERN — The acid drill, answerable in any scrambled order: citric — Aspergillus niger (fungus); acetic — Acetobacter aceti; butyric — Clostridium butylicum; lactic — Lactobacillus. The most common crossing is swapping the products of Acetobacter aceti and Clostridium butylicum, whose names give no hint unless you hear the echoes: Acetobacter-acetic, butylicum-butyric.
• YOUR TURN — A question pairs Clostridium butylicum with acetic acid and Acetobacter aceti with butyric acid. Verdict? Answer: both wrong — the pairs are swapped. Clostridium butylicum produces butyric acid and Acetobacter aceti produces acetic acid; the name echoes are the reliable anchors.
✔ KEY POINT — The four industrial organic acids: citric — Aspergillus niger (fungus); acetic — Acetobacter aceti; butyric — Clostridium butylicum; lactic — Lactobacillus. Saccharomyces cerevisiae also produces ethanol commercially.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Aspergillus niger is a fungus producing citric acid; do not let it drift into the bacteria list, and do not assign citric acid to Lactobacillus merely because Lactobacillus is famous for an acid — its acid is lactic.

Enzymes and Bioactive Molecules — Clot Busters to Statins
• Enzymes are the second factory product line. Lipases are used in detergent formulations and help remove oily stains from the laundry — they digest grease that water and soap loosen poorly. The detergent packet's "enzyme action" is this NCERT line in marketing dress.
• Pectinases and proteases work in the juice trade: bottled fruit juices are clarified using them, which is why bottled juices look clearer than home-made juices — a comparison NCERT makes explicitly and examiners quote directly.
• DEFINITION — Streptokinase is an enzyme produced by the bacterium Streptococcus, and modified by genetic engineering, used as a clot buster: it removes clots from the blood vessels of patients who have undergone myocardial infarction — a heart attack.
• DEFINITION — Cyclosporin A is a bioactive molecule used as an immunosuppressive agent in organ-transplant patients: it dampens immunity so the body does not reject the graft. It is produced by the fungus Trichoderma polysporum. Keep the genus in mind — Trichoderma returns as a biocontrol agent, a different species doing a different job.
• DEFINITION — Statins are blood-cholesterol lowering agents produced by the yeast Monascus purpureus. They act by competitively inhibiting the enzyme responsible for synthesis of cholesterol: the statin competes for the enzyme's active site, the enzyme is blocked, and cholesterol production falls.
• EXAMPLE — The transplant ward runs on a fungus: Trichoderma polysporum's cyclosporin A is what lets a donated kidney survive the patient's own immune system.
• YOUR TURN — A detergent advertises that it "digests grease biologically". Which enzyme class is it selling? Answer: lipases, the grease-digesting enzymes of this section.
• WHY competitive inhibition is the detail that matters — the mechanism is the question. Statins neither destroy cholesterol in the blood nor block its absorption from food; they sit on the synthesis enzyme and compete with its real substrate. Any option giving statins a different mechanism contradicts the textbook.
• WORKED PATTERN — The pharma drill: clot buster — streptokinase — Streptococcus (bacterium, engineered); immunosuppressant — cyclosporin A — Trichoderma polysporum (fungus); cholesterol — statins — Monascus purpureus (yeast), competitive inhibition; detergent enzyme — lipases; juice clarifiers — pectinases plus proteases. Five products, five producers.
✔ KEY POINT — Lipases go into detergents for oily stains; pectinases and proteases clarify bottled juices; streptokinase from Streptococcus is the engineered clot buster; cyclosporin A from Trichoderma polysporum suppresses transplant immunity; statins from Monascus purpureus lower cholesterol by competitive inhibition.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The two Trichoderma entries must not merge: Trichoderma polysporum makes cyclosporin A (medicine), while free-living Trichoderma species police plant roots (biocontrol). And streptokinase comes from Streptococcus — not Staphylococcus, which belongs to the penicillin story as the victim, not the producer.

Sewage — The Problem Every City Must Solve
• DEFINITION — Sewage is the municipal waste-water carrying large amounts of organic matter and microbes, many of them pathogenic. Cities generate it in huge quantities, and it cannot be discharged into rivers and streams without treatment — the pathogens and the organic load are the two hazards.
• Discharging sewage untreated into rivers is essentially a crime against the receiving water: the Ministry of Environment and Forests initiated the Ganga Action Plan and the Yamuna Action Plan to save these rivers from pollution, proposing many sewage treatment plants so only treated sewage may be discharged.
• The treatment map: sewage is treated in sewage treatment plants (STPs) in two stages — primary treatment, basically physical, and secondary treatment, biological. Everything in the next two sections details one of those two words: physical first, biological second.
• EXAMPLE — One litre of sewage carries debris, dissolved organic matter and pathogens; the STP strips the first, feeds the second to microbes, and destroys the third.
• WHY the physical stage must come first — raw sewage carries bottles, rags, grit and sand that no microbe can eat and that would clog or grind the machinery downstream. Removing the solids physically protects the biological workforce that handles the dissolved food.
• WORKED PATTERN — Two hazards, two answers: treatment destroys most pathogenic microbes, and the organic matter is largely consumed by microbes in the secondary stage — which is why secondary treatment is called biological treatment. An option describing sewage treatment as purely chemical contradicts the NCERT scheme.
• YOUR TURN — A proposal suggests skipping treatment plants and diluting sewage in the river instead, since "rivers clean themselves". Why does NCERT's scheme reject this? Answer: sewage carries pathogens and a large organic load whose decomposition would consume the river's oxygen; the Ganga and Yamuna Action Plans therefore require STPs and treated discharge only.
✔ KEY POINT — Sewage is municipal waste-water rich in organic matter and often pathogenic microbes; STPs treat it in a physical primary stage and a biological secondary stage, and the Ganga and Yamuna Action Plans expand STP capacity so only treated sewage reaches rivers.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Secondary treatment is the biological one; primary is physical. Options calling primary treatment "biological" invert the chapter's central scheme. And untreated sewage may not legally be discharged into rivers — that is the whole reason STPs exist.

Primary Treatment — Filtration and Sedimentation
• DEFINITION — Primary treatment is the physical removal of large and small particles from sewage through filtration and sedimentation. It involves steps taken basically for the physical removal of particles — no microbes are deliberately at work here; gravity and sieves do the job.
• Step one, filtration: the sewage is passed through filtration and floating debris is removed by sequential filtration — a series of sieves: whatever floats or is large enough to be caught leaves here.
• Step two, sedimentation: the grit — soil and small pebbles — is then removed by sedimentation. What settles out is collected: all the solids that settle form the primary sludge, and the liquid that leaves the stage is called the effluent.
• EXAMPLE — After an hour in the settling tank: a grey blanket of solids below (primary sludge, pumped away), clearer liquid above (effluent, flowing on). The tank only sorted by weight — nothing in it ate anything.
• WHY a second stage is needed — the effluent from the primary settling tank is taken for secondary treatment. Debris and sedimentable solids are gone, but the dissolved and fine organic matter — the bulk of the pollution load — flows on untouched, because no sieve catches a dissolved molecule.
• WORKED PATTERN — The sequence pattern for stage questions: sequential filtration (debris out) first, sedimentation (grit settles) second, giving primary sludge below and effluent above. The arrow always runs debris, grit, effluent.
• YOUR TURN — An inspector finds that primary sludge is being called "activated sludge" in a plant report. Correct him. Answer: the name is wrong — primary sludge is the solid settled in physical primary treatment; activated sludge is the settled microbial floc mass formed only later, in secondary treatment.
✔ KEY POINT — Primary treatment is purely physical: sequential filtration removes floating debris, sedimentation removes grit; settled solids are primary sludge, the liquid leaving is the effluent.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — No BOD-chomping flocs exist yet at this stage, and primary treatment does not significantly clean dissolved organic matter. The BOD drop belongs to secondary treatment.

Secondary Treatment — Flocs and the BOD Yardstick
• DEFINITION — The primary effluent is passed into large aeration tanks, where it is constantly agitated and air is pumped into it. This allows the vigorous growth of useful aerobic microbes into flocs: masses of bacteria associated with fungal filaments, forming mesh-like structures — bacteria do the eating, fungal filaments the scaffolding.
• As they grow, the microbes consume the major part of the organic matter in the effluent. Eating the dissolved organics is the actual cleaning: what flocs consume no longer demands oxygen from the river.
• DEFINITION — BOD, biochemical oxygen demand, is the amount of oxygen that would be consumed if all the organic matter in one litre of water were oxidised by bacteria. It measures organic matter indirectly: the more present, the more oxygen bacteria need.
• The interpretation, stated by NCERT without ambiguity: the greater the BOD of waste water, the more is its polluting potential. BOD is therefore the yardstick of dirtiness — high BOD means dirty, oxygen-hungry water. Treatment is considered complete only when this potential has been broken down.
• The success criterion: the effluent is treated until the BOD is reduced. The flocs lower BOD significantly, because consuming organic matter removes what creates the demand. Once BOD has fallen enough, the effluent passes into a settling tank.
• The settling and the split: in the settling tank the bacterial flocs sediment — this sediment is activated sludge. A small part is pumped back into the aeration tank as the inoculum, the starter of the next batch, exactly as a spoon of curd starts the next pot. The remaining major part goes into large tanks called anaerobic sludge digesters.
• WHY return a part of the sludge — a settled floc mass already contains the full aerobic workforce in high numbers. Returning some seeds the fresh effluent with a running population instead of waiting for microbes to rebuild from scratch, keeping treatment fast and continuous.
• EXAMPLE — Two one-litre bottles: treated river water and raw sewage. Let bacteria oxidise each fully: the sewage litre consumes far more oxygen — far higher BOD — because it carries far more organic matter. That difference is what BOD reports.
• WORKED PATTERN — The BOD comparison pattern: sample A BOD higher than sample B, therefore sample A has more organic matter and greater polluting potential, and sample B is cleaner. Never reverse it: BOD is demand created by dirt, not oxygen stored by cleanliness. Untreated sewage shows the highest BOD in any such table; properly treated effluent the lowest.
• YOUR TURN — Town A's river sample shows twice the BOD of town B's. Which is more polluted? Answer: town A's — higher BOD means more organic matter and greater polluting potential.
✔ KEY POINT — In aeration tanks, aerobic microbes grow as flocs (bacteria with fungal filaments), consume most organic matter and reduce BOD — the oxygen bacteria need to oxidise all organic matter in one litre of water; higher BOD means greater polluting potential. Settled flocs are activated sludge: a small part returns as inoculum, the major part goes to anaerobic sludge digesters.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Activated sludge is not primary sludge: primary sludge settles during physical treatment and contains no working flocs. Also, flocs form in the aeration tank of secondary treatment, never in the settling tank of primary treatment, and BOD rises with pollution — never with purity.

Biogas — Methanogens Turn Waste into Fuel
• In the anaerobic sludge digesters, anaerobic bacteria digest the bacteria and fungi in the sludge, producing a mixture of gases — methane, hydrogen sulphide and carbon dioxide. These gases form biogas, which is inflammable and can be used as a source of energy.
• EXAMPLE — A village evening: dung slurry enters the tank, methanogens digest it, methane-rich gas lifts the cover and flows to stoves, and the spent slurry leaves for the fields. Energy out one pipe, fertiliser out the other.
• DEFINITION — Methanogens are the anaerobic bacteria that produce large amounts of methane along with carbon dioxide and hydrogen during anaerobic digestion. One prominent example is Methanobacterium, and NCERT gives it two homes: the anaerobic sludge of the treatment plant, and the rumen — the first stomach chamber — of cattle.
• WHY dung is the feedstock — the rumen connection: cattle eat cellulose-rich fodder, and rumen methanogens help break down cellulose, playing an important role in cattle nutrition. The dung carries the same workforce: cattle excreta, commonly called gobar, is rich in these bacteria — why dung is the classic feedstock and the plant a gobar-gas plant.
• The plant, part by part: a concrete tank, 10 to 15 feet deep, where bio-wastes are collected and a slurry of dung is fed. A floating cover over the slurry keeps rising as gas is produced. One outlet pipe supplies biogas to nearby houses; another removes the spent slurry, used as fertiliser.
• The developers: biogas technology was developed in India mainly due to the efforts of the Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) and the Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC) — two names asked as a pair.
• WORKED PATTERN — The biogas pattern examiners reuse: gas — predominantly methane, with carbon dioxide and hydrogen (hydrogen sulphide in the digester account); producers — methanogens such as Methanobacterium; conditions — strictly anaerobic; feedstock — dung and sludge; by-product — spent slurry fertiliser. Five slots, five fixed answers.
• YOUR TURN — A biogas tank is accidentally left open to the air for a week and gas production collapses. Why? Answer: methanogens are anaerobes; oxygen exposure stops their activity, so methane production falls. The tank must stay sealed — that is what the floating cover is for.
✔ KEY POINT — Biogas is mostly methane (with carbon dioxide and hydrogen, plus hydrogen sulphide in sludge digesters), produced by methanogens such as Methanobacterium growing anaerobically — in anaerobic sludge and the cattle rumen. The gobar-gas plant: a 10 to 15 feet deep concrete tank, floating cover, gas outlet to houses, spent-slurry outlet for fertiliser; technology by IARI and KVIC.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Methanogens never appear in the aeration tank: aerobic flocs and anaerobic methanogens belong to opposite halves of the plant. Also, the major part of activated sludge goes to the digesters; only a small part returns as inoculum — options reversing the fractions are wrong.

Biocontrol — Why Chemical War Is Losing
• The problem that opens this arena: chemical insecticides and pesticides are toxic and extremely harmful to humans and animals alike, and they pollute the environment — soil, ground water, fruits, vegetables and crops. Weedicides pollute the soil too. The chemical strategy works, but it bills the whole ecosystem.
• DEFINITION — Biological control of pests and diseases relies on natural predation rather than introduced chemicals. Unlike the chemical approach, which kills useful and harmful life forms indiscriminately, biocontrol keeps pests at manageable levels using the web of organisms that already checks them.
• WHY balance, not eradication — the philosophy, worth quoting in spirit: the organic farmer builds a system where pest control is a living, self-sustaining process — increased biodiversity is the key to the system's health, because a diverse field supports the predators and parasites that hold each pest in check. Eradication is not even attempted; balance is.
• A satellite NCERT example sits honestly in the outline: useful insects such as ladybirds and dragonflies are to be encouraged — the ladybird gets rid of aphids, and dragonflies get rid of mosquitoes. They are the awareness-level illustration of the philosophy before the engineered agents begin.
• EXAMPLE — A ladybird patrolling an aphid colony is biocontrol in its simplest form: a predator eating the pest, nothing sprayed and nothing else killed.
• YOUR TURN — Why does the organic farmer not aim for zero aphids? Answer: his ladybirds need prey to survive on; biocontrol targets manageable levels, not eradication.
• WORKED PATTERN — Contrast pattern for every biocontrol question: chemicals — broad-spectrum, kill useful and harmful forms indiscriminately, pollute; biocontrol — targeted natural predation, pests at manageable levels, no residue. If an option praises a biocontrol agent for total eradication, be suspicious — the chapter's word is control.
✔ KEY POINT — Biocontrol uses natural predation instead of chemicals, keeping pests at manageable levels rather than eradicating them, while chemical pesticides kill indiscriminately and pollute soil, water and food. Ladybirds (aphids) and dragonflies (mosquitoes) are the everyday illustrations.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Biocontrol is not "no control": pests remain, at levels the crop can tolerate. Conversely, a chemical that kills everything is the anti-model of this section, however effective it looks in the short term.

Bt and the Cry Proteins — A Toxin with an Address
• DEFINITION — Bacillus thuringiensis, abbreviated Bt, is a bacterium used as a biological pesticide: its spores are available in sachets and are used as a spray on vulnerable plants, such as brassicas and fruit trees. The bacteria are not killing on contact — they carry a protein weapon that must be eaten and activated.
• The kill mechanism, in order: the spores are sprayed on plants and eaten by insect larvae. In the larval gut the toxin is released and the larvae are killed — because the bacterial protein is an inactive protoxin converted into the active form by the alkaline pH of the gut. The activated toxin creates pores in gut cells, causing them to swell and lyse, and the larva dies.
• WHY alkaline activation is a safety system — the toxin travels inert through the spray tank, the leaf and any non-target mouth, arming itself only in the alkaline caterpillar gut. A neutral or acidic gut never converts it. Selectivity is built into the chemistry, not into careful spraying.
• The engineered step: specific Bt toxin genes were isolated from Bacillus thuringiensis and incorporated into several crop plants, such as cotton. Bt cotton makes its own protoxin in its tissues, so the plant needs no spraying — a microbe's gene, rather than the microbe, deployed.
• The gene map, exact from NCERT: the proteins encoded by the genes cryIAc and cryIIAb control cotton bollworms, while the protein encoded by cryIAb controls the corn borer. Two genes for the bollworms, one for the borer — the most-tested mapping in the entire chapter.
• EXAMPLE — A bollworm chews Bt cotton, swallows protoxin, and within its alkaline gut the toxin activates, pores gut cells, they lyse, and the larva dies. A visiting bee eats no leaf tissue and has no such gut chemistry: unharmed. Same plant, opposite outcomes — the address matched only one gut.
• YOUR TURN — Bt spray drifts onto a plot where only beetles feed. Expect beetle deaths? Answer: no — the protoxin arms itself only in the alkaline gut of susceptible larvae that eat it.
• WORKED PATTERN — The Bt chain, in the only order that scores: spores sprayed and eaten; protoxin released in the gut; alkaline pH activates it; toxin pores gut cells; cells swell and lyse; larva dies. Overlay the gene map: bollworms — cryIAc, cryIIAb; corn borer — cryIAb. Any option putting cryIAb with bollworms is the planted error.
✔ KEY POINT — Bacillus thuringiensis spores sprayed on plants (brassicas, fruit trees) are eaten by larvae; the inactive protoxin converts to active toxin in the alkaline gut, creates pores, and gut cells swell and lyse. In Bt cotton, cryIAc and cryIIAb control cotton bollworms and cryIAb controls the corn borer.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The protoxin is activated by alkaline pH, never by acidic pH — the single most-reversed fact in this chapter. And the pairing cryIAb–bollworm is wrong: cryIAb is the corn borer's gene; the bollworms belong to cryIAc and cryIIAb.

Trichoderma and Baculoviruses — The Gentle Killers
• DEFINITION — Trichoderma species are free-living fungi very common in root ecosystems. They are effective biocontrol agents of several plant pathogens: living freely around roots, they suppress the pathogens attacking the plant from the soil. No gene transfer, no chemistry — the guardian lives where the attack comes from.
• DEFINITION — Baculoviruses are pathogens that attack insects and other arthropods. The majority used as biological control agents are in the genus Nucleopolyhedrovirus. They are excellent candidates for species-specific, narrow-spectrum insecticidal applications — one pest group, nothing else.
• The safety ledger, complete from NCERT: baculoviruses have no negative impacts on plants, mammals, birds, fish or even non-target insects. That last clause is the point — the pest's own virus kills the pest while every bystander is untouched.
• WHY species-specificity wins in conservation settings — these viruses belong in integrated pest management (IPM): they are especially desirable when an ecologically sensitive area is being treated and when beneficial insects are being conserved. A broad-spectrum chemical would kill the very insects the programme exists to protect.
• EXAMPLE — In an IPM orchard the pest's own Nucleopolyhedrovirus is sprayed: caterpillars die while bees, birds and pond fish remain untouched.
• YOUR TURN — A grower wants a root-zone guard against soil pathogens without chemicals. Answer: Trichoderma — free-living root fungi that suppress plant pathogens where roots live.
• WORKED PATTERN — Complete the biocontrol roster with roles: Bt — bacterial protoxin against specific larvae; Trichoderma — free-living root fungi against plant pathogens; baculoviruses (Nucleopolyhedrovirus) — species-specific viral killers of insect pests; ladybirds — aphids; dragonflies — mosquitoes. Five agents, five niches: match agent to niche and the section is solved.
✔ KEY POINT — Trichoderma are free-living root fungi controlling several plant pathogens; baculoviruses (Nucleopolyhedrovirus) attack insects and other arthropods species-specifically, harm no plants, mammals, birds, fish or non-target insects, and are ideal for IPM in ecologically sensitive areas.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not confuse the chapter's two Trichoderma roles: Trichoderma polysporum the cyclosporin producer (factory) and free-living Trichoderma species the root guardians (farm). And baculoviruses are prized for being narrow-spectrum — an option calling them broad-spectrum inverts their entire value.

Biofertilisers — Feeding the Soil, Not Just the Plant
• DEFINITION — Biofertilisers are organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of the soil. The main sources are bacteria, fungi and cyanobacteria. Where chemical fertilisers are manufactured and poured onto fields, biofertilisers are alive: they fix, absorb and deliver nutrients from within the soil itself.
• The headline symbiosis: leguminous plants bear root nodules formed by the symbiotic association of Rhizobium with the root. These bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen into organic forms the plant uses as nutrients. The plant cannot touch atmospheric nitrogen directly — Rhizobium is the bridge.
• The wider family, in outline: another genus, Frankia, also produces nitrogen-fixing nodules on the roots of non-leguminous plants such as Alnus — NCERT's reminder that the nodule habit is not a legume monopoly.
• EXAMPLE — A paddy plot with a thriving mat of Anabaena and Nostoc in its floodwater gains fixed nitrogen and organic matter all season: its fertility rises while it is being cropped.
• The independents: other bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen while free-living in the soil — Azospirillum and Azotobacter — enriching soil nitrogen without any host plant or nodule. Where Rhizobium is a tenant farmer paying rent in nitrogen, these are self-employed fixers.
• The blue-green workforce: cyanobacteria are autotrophic microbes widely distributed in aquatic and terrestrial environments, and many can fix atmospheric nitrogen — Anabaena, Nostoc and Oscillatoria. In paddy fields they serve as important biofertilisers and add organic matter to the soil — a double service no chemical bag performs.
• DEFINITION — Mycorrhiza is the symbiotic association between fungi and the roots of higher plants. Many members of the genus Glomus form mycorrhiza; the fungal symbiont absorbs phosphorus from the soil and passes it to the plant. Such plants also gain resistance to root-borne pathogens, tolerance to salinity and drought, and an overall increase in growth and development.
• WHY phosphorus is the mycorrhizal currency — plants take nitrogen through several microbial routes, but phosphorus sits in soil in forms roots absorb poorly, and none exists in air to be fixed. The fungal partner's vast hyphal network absorbs phosphorus the roots cannot reach, trading it for the plant's sugars. Nitrogen questions point to fixers; phosphorus questions point to Glomus.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sort biofertilisers by currency and lifestyle: nitrogen symbiotic — Rhizobium nodules (Frankia in non-legumes like Alnus); nitrogen free-living — Azospirillum, Azotobacter; nitrogen aquatic — cyanobacteria in paddy fields; phosphorus — Glomus mycorrhiza, with pathogen resistance and stress tolerance as bonuses. Two axes classify every entry.
• YOUR TURN — A mango orchard on phosphorus-poor soil also suffers root pathogens and summer drought stress. Which single biofertiliser partner addresses all three problems? Answer: Glomus mycorrhiza — it absorbs phosphorus and passes it to the plant, and mycorrhizal plants show resistance to root-borne pathogens and tolerance to salinity and drought.
✔ KEY POINT — Biofertilisers enrich soil nutrients: Rhizobium fixes nitrogen in legume root nodules (Frankia in non-legumes like Alnus); Azospirillum and Azotobacter fix it free-living; cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc, Oscillatoria) fertilise paddy fields; Glomus mycorrhiza absorbs phosphorus and adds pathogen resistance plus salinity and drought tolerance.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Mycorrhiza does not fix nitrogen — Glomus delivers phosphorus; nitrogen fixation belongs to Rhizobium, Azospirillum, Azotobacter and the cyanobacteria. And Azotobacter and Azospirillum form no nodules: they fix nitrogen free-living, while nodules belong to Rhizobium (and Frankia).

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Microbes are diverse — protozoa, bacteria, fungi, microscopic plants, viruses, viroids and prions (proteinaceous infectious agents) — and are found everywhere, from thermal vents at 100 degrees C to acidic habitats; bacteria and fungi grow on nutritive media into visible colonies.
✔ KEY POINT — LAB (Lactobacillus and other lactic acid bacteria) grow in milk and convert it to curd: their acids coagulate and partially digest the milk proteins (casein); a little curd acts as inoculum carrying millions of LAB; the vitamin B12 content of curd rises; LAB in our stomach check disease-causing microbes.
✔ KEY POINT — Household gases: dosa and idli dough is fermented by bacteria and puffed by carbon dioxide; bread dough by baker's yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae; toddy is fermented palm sap, a traditional southern Indian drink.
✔ KEY POINT — Cheese specificity comes from its microbes: the large holes in Swiss cheese are due to much carbon dioxide from Propionibacterium sharmanii, while Roquefort cheese is ripened by growing a specific fungus on it.
✔ KEY POINT — Saccharomyces cerevisiae (brewer's yeast) ferments malted cereals and fruit juices to ethanol: wine and beer without distillation; whisky, brandy and rum by distillation of the fermented broth.
✔ KEY POINT — Penicillin, the first antibiotic, was discovered by Alexander Fleming from the mould Penicillium notatum on a Staphylococci plate; Chain and Florey established its full potential; it treated wounded American soldiers in World War II; Nobel Prize 1945 to Fleming, Chain and Florey; antibiotics made plague, whooping cough, diphtheria and leprosy treatable.
✔ KEY POINT — Industrial organic acids: citric acid from Aspergillus niger (a fungus), acetic acid from Acetobacter aceti, butyric acid from Clostridium butylicum, lactic acid from Lactobacillus (the last three bacteria); Saccharomyces cerevisiae is also used for commercial ethanol production.
✔ KEY POINT — Enzymes and bioactive molecules: lipases in detergents remove oily stains; pectinases and proteases clarify bottled juices; streptokinase from Streptococcus (engineered) is a clot buster for heart-attack patients; cyclosporin A from Trichoderma polysporum is the transplant immunosuppressant; statins from Monascus purpureus lower cholesterol by competitively inhibiting the synthesis enzyme.
✔ KEY POINT — Sewage treatment: primary is physical — sequential filtration (floating debris) and sedimentation (grit) give primary sludge and effluent; secondary is biological — aeration grows microbes into flocs (bacteria with fungal filaments) that consume organic matter and reduce BOD; BOD is the oxygen bacteria need to oxidise all organic matter in one litre of water, greater BOD meaning greater polluting potential; settled flocs are activated sludge — a small part recycled as inoculum, the major part sent to anaerobic digesters; the Ganga and Yamuna Action Plans build STP capacity.
✔ KEY POINT — Biogas is predominantly methane, produced with carbon dioxide and hydrogen by methanogens (e.g., Methanobacterium) growing anaerobically in anaerobic sludge and the cattle rumen; the gobar-gas plant is a 10 to 15 feet deep concrete tank with a floating cover, a gas outlet to houses and a spent-slurry outlet used as fertiliser; IARI and KVIC developed the technology in India.
✔ KEY POINT — Biocontrol keeps pests at manageable levels by natural predation: ladybirds get rid of aphids, dragonflies get rid of mosquitoes; Bacillus thuringiensis spores sprayed on brassicas and fruit trees kill larvae whose alkaline gut converts the protoxin into toxin that pores and lyses gut cells; cryIAc and cryIIAb control cotton bollworms, cryIAb the corn borer; Trichoderma are free-living root fungi against plant pathogens; baculoviruses (Nucleopolyhedrovirus) attack insects and other arthropods species-specifically, harming no plants, mammals, birds, fish or non-target insects — ideal for IPM.
✔ KEY POINT — Biofertilisers (bacteria, fungi, cyanobacteria) enrich soil nutrients: Rhizobium fixes atmospheric nitrogen in legume root nodules; Azospirillum and Azotobacter fix it free-living; cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc, Oscillatoria) fertilise paddy fields and add organic matter; Glomus mycorrhiza absorbs phosphorus for the plant and adds resistance to root-borne pathogens plus tolerance to salinity and drought.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Saying the vitamin that increases in curd is vitamin C, D or B2. Why it is wrong: NCERT names exactly one vitamin for this conversion. Correct principle: LAB increase the vitamin B12 content of curd — B12 is the only vitamin answer here.
• Mistake 2: Crediting the holes in Swiss cheese to a fungus, or to oxygen or methane. Why it is wrong: the holes are gas pockets blown by a bacterium's heavy carbon dioxide output. Correct principle: Propionibacterium sharmanii (a bacterium) makes the carbon dioxide; the fungus belongs to Roquefort, where it ripens the cheese.
• Mistake 3: Calling wine or beer distilled products. Why it is wrong: wine and beer are bottled fermentations; only whisky, brandy and rum pass through distillation. Correct principle: ask "was the broth distilled?" — no for wine and beer, yes for spirits.
• Mistake 4: Giving penicillin's discovery to Chain and Florey, or its source as a bacterium. Why it is wrong: Fleming discovered it from the mould Penicillium notatum on a Staphylococci plate; Chain and Florey established its full potential afterwards. Correct principle: discoverer Fleming, developers Chain and Florey, source a fungus, Nobel 1945 to all three.
• Mistake 5: Swapping the organic-acid producers — Acetobacter aceti with butyric acid, Clostridium butylicum with acetic acid, or citric acid to a bacterium. Why it is wrong: the pairings are fixed and the middle two are the swapped pair. Correct principle: citric — Aspergillus niger (fungus); acetic — Acetobacter aceti; butyric — Clostridium butylicum; lactic — Lactobacillus.
• Mistake 6: Confusing primary sludge with activated sludge, or placing flocs in primary treatment. Why it is wrong: primary treatment is physical, producing primary sludge; flocs grow only in the aeration tank and settle as activated sludge. Correct principle: primary = filtration and sedimentation; secondary = flocs, BOD reduction, activated sludge.
• Mistake 7: Reading BOD backwards — claiming high BOD means clean water. Why it is wrong: BOD counts the oxygen bacteria need to oxidise a litre's organic matter, so it rises with the load. Correct principle: greater BOD means greater polluting potential; treatment succeeds by bringing BOD down.
• Mistake 8: Placing methanogens in the aeration tank or calling biogas mostly hydrogen. Why it is wrong: methanogens are strict anaerobes working in anaerobic digesters and the cattle rumen, and their gas is predominantly methane. Correct principle: aeration tank = aerobic flocs; digesters and rumen = anaerobic methanogens; biogas = mostly methane.
• Mistake 9: Reversing the cry gene map or the Bt activation chemistry. Why it is wrong: cryIAb controls the corn borer, not the bollworms, and the protoxin is activated by the alkaline gut, never by acid. Correct principle: cryIAc and cryIIAb — cotton bollworms; cryIAb — corn borer; activation — alkaline gut, pores, lysis.
• Mistake 10: Giving nitrogen to mycorrhiza or nodules to Azotobacter. Why it is wrong: Glomus mycorrhiza supplies phosphorus; nitrogen fixation belongs to Rhizobium (legume nodules), free-living Azospirillum and Azotobacter (no nodules), and cyanobacteria. Correct principle: phosphorus — mycorrhiza; nitrogen — Rhizobium, Azospirillum, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: Match (a) Aspergillus niger, (b) Acetobacter aceti, (c) Clostridium butylicum, (d) Lactobacillus against (i) acetic, (ii) butyric, (iii) citric, (iv) lactic acid.
• Given: The four industrial organic-acid pairings of NCERT, producers in NCERT's own order.
• Principle: Anchor the name-echoes first — Acetobacter-acetic, butylicum-butyric — then the remainder: Aspergillus niger-citric, Lactobacillus-lactic.
• Steps: (b) takes (i); (c) takes (ii); (a) takes (iii); (d) takes (iv).
• Answer: a-iii, b-i, c-ii, d-iv.
• Reasoning: Only one producer is a fungus (Aspergillus niger), welded to citric acid; with the echoes and the fungus placed, Lactobacillus has only lactic acid left.
• Mistake to avoid: The designed trap is b-ii and c-i — Acetobacter with butyric, Clostridium with acetic — the swap this example disarms.
• Example 2 — Problem: "Our whisky and the winery's wine are made the same way — both start with yeast fermenting plant material." Evaluate.
• Given: Both begin with Saccharomyces cerevisiae fermenting cereals or fruit juices to ethanol; the processes then diverge.
• Principle: Beverage families are defined by what follows fermentation — bottling versus distillation of the fermented broth.
• Steps: Wine: fruit juice fermented, bottled undistilled. Whisky: cereal broth fermented, then distilled to concentrate ethanol. Same first step, different second.
• Answer: Only half right — fermentation is shared, but whisky is produced by distillation of the fermented broth while wine is produced without distillation.
• Reasoning: Fermentation caps alcohol near the level yeast can survive; distillation is the extra step beyond it. Options exploit this overlap of step one.
• Mistake to avoid: Do not let the shared yeast blur the classification — the NCERT line is drawn at distillation, not at raw material or microbe.
• Example 3 — Problem: Sample P (raw sewage) has the highest BOD, R (primary effluent) lower, Q (after secondary treatment) lowest. Explain the fall, in stage order.
• Given: BOD indirectly measures organic matter — the oxygen bacteria would consume oxidising all of it in one litre of water.
• Principle: Primary treatment removes solids physically but leaves most dissolved organic matter; secondary microbes consume the organic matter itself, so BOD falls most sharply there.
• Steps: P carries the full load, so BOD is maximal. Filtration and sedimentation remove debris and grit — BOD drops modestly (R). In aeration tanks, flocs consume the major part of the organic matter — BOD falls steeply (Q).
• Answer: BOD falls a little across primary treatment and a lot across secondary treatment, because flocs digest the organic matter whose abundance BOD measures.
• Reasoning: The BOD graph is a biography of the microbes: the physical stage removes only what settles or strains; the biological stage removes the dissolved load.
• Mistake to avoid: Crediting primary treatment with the big BOD drop — filtration and sedimentation cannot touch dissolved organic matter; only flocs can.
• Example 4 — Problem: A heart-attack patient, a transplant patient and a high-cholesterol patient each receive a microbial product. Name each product, producer and job.
• Given: The chapter's three bioactive molecules: streptokinase, cyclosporin A and statins.
• Principle: Match by clinical job — dissolving a clot, suppressing immunity, lowering cholesterol synthesis.
• Steps: Heart attack: streptokinase, from Streptococcus (engineered) — clot buster. Transplant: cyclosporin A, from Trichoderma polysporum — immunosuppressant. Cholesterol: statins, from Monascus purpureus — competitive inhibition of the synthesis enzyme.
• Answer: Streptokinase — Streptococcus — clot buster. Cyclosporin A — Trichoderma polysporum — immunosuppressant. Statins — Monascus purpureus — cholesterol lowering by competitive inhibition.
• Reasoning: The producers span the census — bacterium, fungus, yeast — so one question tests three kingdoms and three mechanisms.
• Mistake to avoid: Do not swap the patients' products; and statins inhibit cholesterol synthesis — they do not dissolve cholesterol already in the blood.
• Example 5 — Problem: A village gobar-gas plant: the floating cover rides high, the stove burns with gas, and fields receiving the spent slurry need less fertiliser. Explain all three observations with one microbial group.
• Given: A concrete tank 10 to 15 feet deep fed with dung slurry; dung is rich in methanogens from the cattle rumen.
• Principle: Methanogens grow anaerobically on cellulosic material, producing much methane with carbon dioxide and hydrogen; the gas is biogas, the residue fertiliser.
• Steps: Methanogens digest the slurry; released gas lifts the floating cover (one) and flows through the outlet pipe to the stove (two). The spent slurry, nutrients intact, fertilises the fields (three).
• Answer: Methanogens (e.g., Methanobacterium): anaerobic digestion makes methane-rich biogas for the stove and leaves nutrient-rich spent slurry for the fields.
• Reasoning: One anaerobic workforce, two products — gas above, fertiliser below — and the cover's rise meters the invisible labour. Technology credit: IARI and KVIC.
• Mistake to avoid: Do not credit aerobic flocs or yeast; gobar gas is anaerobic methanogen work, its main gas methane.
• Example 6 — Problem: Bt cotton stays nearly free of bollworms while corn borers thrive in a neighbouring maize plot. A student concludes the cotton's cry genes are "not working properly". Evaluate.
• Given: The cry map: cryIAc and cryIIAb — cotton bollworms; cryIAb — corn borer. The maize is a separate, non-Bt crop.
• Principle: Each cry protein matches specific pests, and Bt protection lives only inside the engineered plant — it does not radiate to neighbours.
• Steps: The cotton carries bollworm-active genes and is protected, as designed. The maize has no cry genes, so borers feed unopposed; their success says nothing about the cotton. Maize would need cryIAb built in.
• Answer: Wrong — the cotton's genes work (bollworms controlled); borers thrive because the maize is unprotected and borer control requires cryIAb.
• Reasoning: Gene-to-pest specificity is the point: cryIAc and cryIIAb are not interchangeable with cryIAb; protection is plant-by-plant, not field-by-field.
• Mistake to avoid: Do not assign cryIAb to bollworms or expect Bt cotton to defend a neighbouring crop — both confuse the address system making Bt selective.
• Example 7 — Problem: Design the microbe team for (a) more nitrogen in a legume field without chemical fertiliser, (b) better phosphorus uptake and drought tolerance in an orchard, (c) nitrogen enrichment of a flooded paddy. Justify each choice.
• Given: The roster: Rhizobium (legume nodules), Azospirillum and Azotobacter (free-living), cyanobacteria (paddy), Glomus mycorrhiza (phosphorus).
• Principle: Match nutrient and habitat: legumes host nodule symbionts; flooded fields suit photosynthetic cyanobacteria; phosphorus and stress tolerance are the mycorrhizal brief.
• Steps: (a) Rhizobium — legume root nodules fixing atmospheric nitrogen into organic forms the plant uses. (b) Glomus mycorrhiza — absorbs soil phosphorus, adding resistance to root-borne pathogens and tolerance to salinity and drought. (c) Cyanobacteria (Anabaena, Nostoc, Oscillatoria) — autotrophic nitrogen fixers of paddy water, also adding organic matter.
• Answer: Rhizobium for the legume field, Glomus mycorrhiza for the orchard, nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria for the paddy.
• Reasoning: Every choice is a habitat match: nodules need legume roots, cyanobacteria the flooded sunlit field, and the orchard's problem — phosphorus and stress — is the roster's one fungal job.
• Mistake to avoid: Do not send mycorrhiza to fix nitrogen or Rhizobium to a paddy — currencies and habitats decide the team.

• Q5. Which microbe produces the clot buster used after a heart attack, and which microbe produces the immunosuppressant used in organ transplants?
• Q6. A caterpillar eats leaves sprayed with Bt but a second insect species feeding nearby is unharmed. Outline the two-step reason the toxin kills selectively, naming the gut condition required.
• Q7. Name the fungal genus that forms mycorrhiza, the nutrient it supplies to the plant, and two additional benefits the plant receives.
• Q8. Wine, beer, whisky, brandy, rum: sort the five into "produced without distillation" and "produced by distillation of the fermented broth", and name the yeast responsible for the fermentation in all five.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. Lactobacillus (the LAB); the vitamin is B12, whose content increases as milk becomes curd. Misconception watch: LAB's acid is lactic acid and they coagulate and partially digest casein — but the vitamin in the standard question is always B12, never B2.
• A2. Propionibacterium sharmanii; the gas is carbon dioxide, produced in large amounts during ripening. Misconception watch: the worker is a bacterium, not the Roquefort fungus, and the gas is carbon dioxide — not methane and not oxygen.
• A3. Sequential filtration (floating debris) first, then removal of grit by sedimentation — together, primary treatment; then growth of flocs in aeration tanks; finally the settling of those flocs as activated sludge. Misconception watch: activated sludge ends the biological stage, not the physical one — primary treatment's solid is primary sludge.
• A4. The higher-BOD sample carries more organic matter and is the more polluting one: BOD is the oxygen bacteria would consume oxidising all the organic matter in one litre, so it rises with the load. Misconception watch: high BOD never means clean water — it means oxygen-demanding water.
• A5. Streptokinase, the clot buster, is produced by the bacterium Streptococcus (modified by genetic engineering); cyclosporin A, the immunosuppressant, by the fungus Trichoderma polysporum. Misconception watch: Staphylococcus is the bacterium Fleming's mould stopped — it produces nothing here; the clot buster's producer is Streptococcus.
• A6. The Bt protein is swallowed as an inactive protoxin; only in the caterpillar's alkaline gut is it converted to the active toxin, which pores gut cells so they swell and lyse. A species lacking that alkaline gut — or never eating sprayed leaves — is untouched. Misconception watch: the toxin is not active in the sachet; alkaline activation inside the victim is the basis of selectivity.
• A7. Glomus. The nutrient is phosphorus, absorbed from soil by the fungal symbiont and passed to the plant. Other benefits (any two): resistance to root-borne pathogens, tolerance to salinity and drought, overall increase in growth. Misconception watch: mycorrhiza fixes no nitrogen — that belongs to Rhizobium, Azospirillum, Azotobacter and cyanobacteria.
• A8. Without distillation: wine and beer. By distillation of the fermented broth: whisky, brandy and rum. The yeast in all five is Saccharomyces cerevisiae — brewer's yeast, the same species as baker's yeast. Misconception watch: distillation is a physical step after fermentation, not a second fermentation, and involves no microbe.
