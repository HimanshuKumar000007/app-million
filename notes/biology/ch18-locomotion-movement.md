Introduction — Why Movement Is Life's Signature
• A sunflower tracking the sun, a leucocyte squeezing through a capillary wall, you flicking to the next line — movement is happening at every scale of your body right now. NCERT opens with a simple claim: movement is a significant feature of living beings, locomotion its most visible form. This chapter follows movement from a crawling white blood cell to the 206-bone frame that lets you sprint; almost every fact is a counting or matching trap.
• DEFINITION — Movement is any change in position of the body or a body part, from cytoplasm streaming inside one cell to the swing of a whole limb. Locomotion is movement that shifts the entire organism from place to place — walking, running, swimming, flying.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — it is built from lists papers recycle endlessly: four movement types, three muscle types, two filaments, one contraction cycle, 206 bones split 80 plus 126, five synovial joint types and six disorders. Every list is short, fixed and paired with a classic example — the shape of one-mark and match-the-following questions.
• EXAMPLE — Your body moves even when you sit still: cilia in your trachea sweep dust upwards, leucocytes crawl amoeba-style through tissues hunting microbes, your heart muscle contracts, and gut smooth muscle pushes breakfast along. Stillness is an illusion of small movements cancelling out.
• WORKED PATTERN — The master key for the whole chapter is a two-question drill for any structure: which system does it belong to (muscular, skeletal or neural), and what exactly does it move or allow to move? Ask it of the sarcomere, the atlas, the femur and the synovial cavity, and most options in a question eliminate themselves.
• YOUR TURN — A sperm swims towards the ovum using a tail-like structure. Is this locomotion, movement, or both? Answer: both — the whole cell changes place (locomotion), achieved by flagellar movement of its tail.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The whole chapter in one breath: cells move in four ways (amoeboid, ciliary, flagellar, muscular); muscles are three types built from two filaments (actin and myosin) that slide, not shorten; the skeleton is 206 bones joined by three families of joints; and the disorders are what you get when each moving part fails in its own way.

Four Ways Living Things Move — Amoeboid, Ciliary, Flagellar, Muscular
• DEFINITION — Amoeboid movement is crawling by pseudopodia, temporary feet formed by the streaming of protoplasm into one direction, exactly as in Amoeba. Cytoskeletal elements, especially microfilaments, are involved in producing it. In your body, macrophages and leucocytes of the blood move this way, squeezing between cells to reach sites of infection.
• DEFINITION — Ciliary movement is the coordinated beating of cilia, the short hair-like outgrowths on cells lining most of our internal tubular organs. The beating is synchronised, like oars on a boat, so material is swept steadily in one direction across the surface.
• DEFINITION — Flagellar movement is propulsion by a long whip-like flagellum. Its NCERT cast is precise: swimming of spermatozoa, maintenance of water current in the canal system of sponges, and locomotion of protozoans such as Euglena.
• DEFINITION — Muscular movement is movement produced by the contraction of muscle tissue — limbs, jaws, tongue, eyelids and the entire act of locomotion. NCERT stresses that locomotion is not a one-system job: it needs the perfectly coordinated activity of the muscular, skeletal and neural systems together.
• EXAMPLE — The trachea is the favourite ciliary example: its ciliated epithelium sweeps inhaled dust up and out, an escalator you never notice. The same design moves the ovum along the female reproductive tract — no muscle involved, yet a whole cell travels.
• WHY four designs exist — each solves a different physics problem. Pseudopodia suit a soft cell crawling over surfaces. Cilia move fluids and particles across a fixed surface. A flagellum propels a whole cell through liquid. Muscle moves levers — bones — and only muscle generates the large, controlled forces a land animal needs.
• WORKED PATTERN — Movement-type questions are pure matching: leucocyte or macrophage means amoeboid; trachea or ovum transport means ciliary; sperm, sponge canals or Euglena means flagellar; limb, jaw or tongue means muscular. Learn each type with its NCERT examples welded to it, and never let an option swap examples between types.
• YOUR TURN — A question says: movement helps maintain the water current in the canal system of sponges. Which type, and what structure beats or whips? Answer: flagellar movement — flagella of the canal-lining cells keep the current flowing.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Ciliary and flagellar are not interchangeable labels for the same examples. The ovum travels by cilia, the sperm swims by a flagellum — papers delight in swapping exactly this pair. Anchor it: the small cell with the long tail whips (sperm, flagellum); the large cell is carried by the crowd (ovum, cilia).

The Muscle Family — Skeletal, Smooth and Cardiac
• DEFINITION — Muscle is a specialised tissue of mesodermal origin, and no minor tissue: about 40 to 50 percent of an adult's body weight is muscle. All muscles share four properties — excitability (respond to signals), contractility (shorten), extensibility (can be stretched) and elasticity (return to resting shape).
• DEFINITION — Skeletal muscles are closely associated with the skeletal system. Under the microscope they show stripes, so they are called striated muscles, and because the nervous system controls them at will, they are voluntary muscles. Their jobs are locomotion and changes of body posture.
• DEFINITION — Visceral or smooth muscles are located in the inner walls of hollow visceral organs — the alimentary canal, the reproductive tract and similar tubes. They show no striations, look smooth, and are involuntary: you cannot will your gut to squeeze. Their work is quiet transport of materials inside the body.
• DEFINITION — Cardiac muscles build the heart. Cardiac muscle cells assemble in a branching pattern to form the tissue; the heart is striated in appearance like skeletal muscle but is involuntary like smooth muscle — a genuine hybrid, and a favourite trap for exactly that reason.
• EXAMPLE — One meal uses all three types in sequence: skeletal muscle moves jaw and tongue as you chew by choice, smooth muscle pushes the food through the alimentary canal without your permission, and cardiac muscle keeps blood moving to digest it all — three tissues, one dinner.
• WHY location decides the name — NCERT classifies muscles based on location, not looks alone. Striated describes appearance; skeletal describes address. Options mix the two axes deliberately, so keep them separate: where is it, does it stripe, is it voluntary — three independent questions, three independent answers.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any muscle question run the triple check. Heart wall: cardiac, striated, involuntary. Limb: skeletal, striated, voluntary. Gut wall: smooth, non-striated, involuntary. An option pairing the heart with non-striated, or the gut with voluntary, is wrong however polished it sounds.
• YOUR TURN — The muscles that move your eyelids as you blink on purpose: which type, and which two labels apply? Answer: skeletal muscle — striated and voluntary.
✔ KEY POINT — Muscle anchors: mesodermal origin, 40 to 50 percent of adult body weight, four properties (excitability, contractility, extensibility, elasticity), and the triple identity of each type — skeletal (striated, voluntary), smooth (non-striated, involuntary, visceral walls), cardiac (branched, striated, involuntary).

Inside a Skeletal Muscle — Bundles, Fibres and Fibrils
• DEFINITION — An organised skeletal muscle is made of muscle bundles, also called fascicles, held together by a common collagenous connective tissue layer called the fascia. Zoom in once: each bundle contains a number of muscle fibres. Zoom in again: each fibre holds many parallelly arranged filaments called myofilaments or myofibrils.
• DEFINITION — Each muscle fibre is lined by its own plasma membrane, the sarcolemma, which encloses the cytoplasm of the muscle, the sarcoplasm. A muscle fibre is a syncytium — the sarcoplasm contains many nuclei in one shared cytoplasm, instead of the usual one nucleus per cell.
• DEFINITION — The endoplasmic reticulum of the muscle fibre has a special name and a special job: it is the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and it is the store house of calcium ions. Remember this address — the entire contraction story of the next sections is triggered by calcium leaving and returning to this store.
• WHY the bundle design — a muscle must pull as one coordinated rope, not loose threads. Fascia wraps bundles so force adds up along one line, while the fibre is the cellular unit: one membrane, one shared cytoplasm, many nuclei servicing a very long cell, hundreds of myofibrils pulling side by side.
• EXAMPLE — Think of a rope being built. The whole rope is the muscle; each thick strand is a fascicle in its fascia wrapping; each strand is woven from fibres; each fibre is a bundle of fine threads — the myofibrils — themselves made of the two filaments you will meet next, actin and myosin.
• WORKED PATTERN — Hierarchy questions come as ordering tasks: muscle, fascicle (bundle), fibre, myofibril, filament. The two membrane traps live at fixed levels: fascia wraps the bundle, sarcolemma wraps the fibre. An option putting sarcolemma around the bundle or fascia around one fibre is out.
• YOUR TURN — A cell from a skeletal muscle is found to contain many nuclei. Is it abnormal? Answer: no — a skeletal muscle fibre is a syncytium and normally carries many nuclei in its sarcoplasm.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Students swap the two wrappings and the two plasm names under pressure. Lock the pairs: fascia-bundle, sarcolemma-fibre, sarcoplasm-cytoplasm of the fibre, sarcoplasmic reticulum-calcium store. Four pairs, zero interchangeability.

The Sarcomere — Map of Bands, Zones and Lines
• DEFINITION — Each myofibril has alternate dark and light bands, and the pattern comes from just two proteins arranged in parallel: actin and myosin. The light band contains actin and is called the I band, I for isotropic. The dark band contains myosin and is called the A band, A for anisotropic.
• DEFINITION — In the centre of each I band runs an elastic fibre called the Z line, which bisects the band, and the thin filaments are firmly attached to this Z line. In the A band, the thick filaments are held together in the middle by a thin fibrous membrane called the M line.
• DEFINITION — The sarcomere is the portion of a myofibril between two successive Z lines, and NCERT names it the functional unit of contraction. Everything in the contraction mechanism is described as changes inside this one repeating unit.
• DEFINITION — The H zone is visible only in the resting muscle: at rest, the edges of the thin filaments on either side partially overlap the free ends of the thick filaments, leaving the central part of the thick filaments uncovered by actin. That bare central part is the H zone — myosin alone, no actin.
• EXAMPLE — Read one sarcomere like a map: Z line, half an I band (actin only), the A band whose outer parts mix actin and myosin, the central H zone (myosin only) crossed by the M line, then the mirror image back to the next Z line. Walk this map aloud and band questions become easy.
• WHY two bands alternate — the stripes are not decoration; they are a shadow of the machinery. Where only thin filaments sit, light passes easily and the band looks light. Where thick filaments sit, it looks dark. The microscope pattern is the contraction machine photographed at rest.
• WORKED PATTERN — Band identity drill: I band means actin, isotropic, light. A band means myosin, anisotropic, dark, its length equalling the thick filaments. Z line means boundary and actin anchor. M line means middle of the A band. H zone means the actin-free centre of the A band, present only at rest. Five terms, five one-line identities — papers only ever test these.
• YOUR TURN — A micrograph shows a region containing only thick filaments, located at the centre of an A band. Name the region and the line through its middle. Answer: the H zone, crossed by the M line.
✔ KEY POINT — Sarcomere equals Z line to Z line. The I band is bisected by the Z line, the A band is centred on the M line, and the H zone is the part of the A band that actin has not reached at rest.

Actin — The Thin Filament and Its Masked Sites
• DEFINITION — Each actin filament is made of two F actins, filamentous strands helically wound around each other. Each F actin is itself a polymer built from many monomeric G actins — G for globular — linked like beads on a string, then twisted into a double helix.
• DEFINITION — Two filaments of another protein, tropomyosin, run close to the F actins along their length. A third protein, troponin, a complex of subunits, sits distributed at regular intervals on the tropomyosin, like guards posted at fixed distances along a fence.
• DEFINITION — In the resting state, one subunit of troponin masks the active binding sites for myosin on the actin filaments. This single fact runs the whole chapter: at rest the binding sites are covered, so myosin cannot attach; contraction begins exactly when the mask is lifted.
• WHY the mask exists — a muscle that grabbed its own filaments without a signal would lock in permanent contraction. The troponin-tropomyosin system is the safety catch on the trigger: it keeps the actin sites hidden until a specific chemical key, the calcium ion, moves the guard aside.
• EXAMPLE — Picture door handles (active sites) along a corridor (the actin). Tropomyosin is the cover strip over the handles; troponin the clip holding the strip at regular intervals. Nothing grips the handles until the clips shift — and calcium is the hand that shifts them.
• WORKED PATTERN — Protein role drill, actin side: G actin is the monomer bead; F actin is the polymer strand; two F actins make one thin filament; tropomyosin is the covering strand; troponin is the spaced-out complex masking the myosin-binding sites at rest. Questions hand you a job description and ask for the protein — these five descriptions are the entire cast.
• YOUR TURN — If troponin failed to mask the active sites in a resting muscle, what would go wrong? Answer: myosin heads could bind actin without any signal, so the muscle would tend to contract uncontrollably instead of waiting for a command.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Troponin sits on tropomyosin, not on myosin, and it masks sites on actin — three attachments students routinely reverse. Also keep the letters straight: G actin is globular and single; F actin is filamentous and polymerised. Papers swap G and F definitions as a one-mark gift to the careless.

Myosin — The Thick Filament and Its Swinging Heads
• DEFINITION — Each myosin filament is also a polymerised protein: many monomeric units called meromyosins join to build one thick filament. Each meromyosin has two parts — a globular head with a short arm, and a tail.
• DEFINITION — The head-plus-arm part is called the heavy meromyosin, HMM, and the tail is the light meromyosin, LMM. The HMM projects outwards from the surface of the myosin filament at regular intervals and angles, forming what is called a cross arm.
• DEFINITION — The globular head is an active ATPase enzyme: it carries binding sites for ATP and active sites for actin. Read that twice — the head is simultaneously the motor, the fuel-burning enzyme and the gripping hand of the whole system.
• WHY heads point both ways — in each half of the A band, myosin heads orient towards the nearer Z line. When all heads pull at once, actin from both sides is dragged towards the centre, so the Z lines are pulled inwards, never pushed apart — head geometry decides the direction of pull.
• EXAMPLE — A tug-of-war where every player faces the centre: both sides haul their ropes towards the middle. The ropes are actin, the players are myosin heads, and the middle of the A band is where every effort converges.
• WORKED PATTERN — Myosin questions test three welded facts: meromyosin builds the filament (monomer to polymer, parallel to G actin building F actin); HMM is head plus short arm, LMM is tail; the head is the ATPase with both the ATP site and the actin site. An option making the tail the ATPase, or putting the actin site on LMM, is wrong.
• YOUR TURN — A drug blocks the ATPase activity of myosin heads but leaves everything else untouched. Predict the effect. Answer: contraction weakens or fails — bridges cannot be powered and reset, since the head needs ATP both for the power-stroke cycle and to detach after each pull.
✔ KEY POINT — Meromyosin equals HMM (globular head plus short arm — the cross arm, the ATPase) plus LMM (the tail). The heads repeat along the thick filament at regular intervals and angles, pointing towards the nearest Z line in each A-band half.

The Signal Arrives — Motor Unit and Motor-End Plate
• DEFINITION — Contraction starts in the central nervous system: a neural signal travels down a motor neuron to the muscle it commands. A motor neuron together with all the muscle fibres it connects to is called a motor unit — the smallest team the brain can switch on at once.
• DEFINITION — The junction between a motor neuron and the sarcolemma of a muscle fibre is the neuromuscular junction, also called the motor-end plate. It is a synapse built onto muscle, and it converts an electrical signal in a nerve into an electrical signal in a fibre.
• DEFINITION — When the neural signal reaches the junction, it releases a neurotransmitter — acetylcholine — into the gap. Acetylcholine generates an action potential in the sarcolemma, and that action potential spreads through the whole muscle fibre, diving into its interior.
• DEFINITION — The arriving action potential triggers the release of calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum into the sarcoplasm. Note the chain of custody: brain signal, motor neuron, acetylcholine, sarcolemma action potential, calcium flood — five links, and the next section picks up exactly where the calcium lands.
• EXAMPLE — The chain is a relay of messengers, each speaking a different language: electricity down the neuron, chemistry across the junction (acetylcholine), electricity across the sarcolemma, chemistry inside (calcium). Every disorder here works by jamming exactly one translation.
• WHY a chemical step at the junction — a nerve ending is tiny compared with the huge fibre it commands. The neurotransmitter step lets one small ending flood a wide membrane with signal at once, and gives the body a control point: enzymes clear acetylcholine quickly, ending the command so the muscle can stop.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sequence questions are free marks if the chain is memorised as one sentence: CNS signal to motor neuron to motor-end plate, acetylcholine released, action potential in sarcolemma, calcium released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. An option releasing calcium before the action potential, or acetylcholine after calcium, breaks the chain.
• YOUR TURN — Which single structure is both the end of the neural pathway and the start of the muscle's own electrical signal, and what chemical does it release? Answer: the motor-end plate (neuromuscular junction); it releases acetylcholine.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Myasthenia gravis, coming later in this chapter, attacks precisely this junction — autoimmune damage here makes muscles fatigue and weaken even though the muscle fibres themselves are healthy. Remember the junction now and the disorder will explain itself later.

Sliding Filaments — The Cross-Bridge Cycle Step by Step
• DEFINITION — The sliding filament theory states that contraction is caused by the sliding of the thin filaments over the thick filaments. The filaments themselves do not shorten, fold or melt — they glide past each other, so the overlap increases and the sarcomere shortens.
• DEFINITION — Step one, unmasking: the calcium ions released into the sarcoplasm bind to a subunit of troponin on the actin filaments. This shifts the troponin-tropomyosin cover aside, and the active binding sites for myosin stand exposed on actin.
• DEFINITION — Step two, cross-bridge formation: using the energy from ATP hydrolysis, the myosin head binds to an exposed active site on actin, forming a cross bridge. The ATPase in the head splits ATP into ADP and inorganic phosphate, and the released energy cocks and arms the head.
• DEFINITION — Step three, the power stroke: the attached head pulls the actin filament towards the centre of the A band. Because the actin is anchored to the Z line, the Z line is pulled inwards too, and the sarcomere shortens — this visible shortening is the contraction.
• DEFINITION — Step four, detachment and reset: the myosin releases the ADP and phosphate; a new ATP molecule binds to the head, and that binding breaks the cross bridge. The head then hydrolyses the fresh ATP and grips the next site further along the actin, so the cycle repeats like a hand climbing a rope.
• WHY the cycle must repeat — one power stroke moves actin only a tiny distance, so heads work in relays: while some pull, others let go and re-grip further along. Millions of heads cycling many times per second shorten the whole muscle, while calcium keeps sites exposed and ATP keeps arriving.
• EXAMPLE — Think of hauling a boat rope hand over hand. Each hand grips (cross bridge), pulls (power stroke), lets go only when the next grip is ready (ATP binding breaks the bridge), and reaches forward again (ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head). Remove the energy and the hands freeze onto the rope — why muscles stiffen after death, when ATP runs out.
• WORKED PATTERN — The exam's favourite four events must stay in order: calcium binds troponin; sites unmask; head binds using ATP-hydrolysis energy and pulls; new ATP binds and the bridge breaks. The two ATP events are the trick — ATP powers and cocks the head before the pull, and fresh ATP is needed to let go after it. An option breaking the bridge without new ATP, or pulling before unmasking, is wrong.
• YOUR TURN — A muscle is given plenty of calcium but no ATP at all. Will it contract once and lock, contract repeatedly, or never contract? Answer: heads may attach to exposed sites, but without ATP they cannot power strokes or detach — the muscle locks stiff instead of cycling, the logic behind rigor mortis.
✔ KEY POINT — The cycle in one line: calcium unmasks, ATP hydrolysis arms the head, the head grips and pulls actin to the centre, new ATP breaks the grip — repeat until calcium goes home.

What Shortens and What Does Not — Band Changes in Contraction
• DEFINITION — During shortening of the sarcomere, the I bands get reduced in width, whereas the A bands retain their length. This is NCERT's exact wording and the single most tested sentence of the chapter: learn it word for word, because options attack every word of it.
• DEFINITION — The H zone also shrinks during contraction and can disappear in a strongly contracted sarcomere, because actin sliding in from both sides covers more and more of the thick filament's central region. The zone that was actin-free at rest gets invaded by actin.
• DEFINITION — The Z lines move closer together, since they ride inwards with the actin anchored to them. The sarcomere — Z line to Z line — therefore shortens, while neither the actin nor the myosin filament itself changes length at all.
• EXAMPLE — Slide two combs into each other: the teeth interpenetrate further, the total span shrinks, but no tooth shortens. Bands name regions of overlap, so as overlap grows, the actin-only region (I band) and myosin-only region (H zone) shrink, while the A band stays fixed by the thick filaments' length.
• WHY the A band cannot change — it is defined by where the thick filaments are, and thick filaments neither shorten nor slide (they are the track, not the train). The I band must shrink as the sarcomere shortens, because it is the gap between the Z line and the A band's fixed edge.
• WORKED PATTERN — Contraction-change table, the safest lines in this chapter: sarcomere shortens; Z lines come closer; I band reduces; H zone reduces or vanishes; A band unchanged; actin and myosin lengths unchanged. Any statement outside this table — especially A band shortens, or filaments contract — is automatically false.
• YOUR TURN — In a resting sarcomere the I band is wide and the H zone is clear. After a strong contraction, a student claims the A band has narrowed. Grade the claim. Answer: wrong — the A band retains the length of the thick filaments; only the I band and H zone reduced.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The phrase filaments contract is biologically wrong even though muscles contract. Muscles shorten because filaments slide; the filaments' own lengths are constant. Papers also reverse the pair into I band retains, A band reduces — the mirror image of the truth, planted for students who memorised shapes instead of the sentence.

Relaxation, Fatigue and the Calcium Reset
• DEFINITION — Contraction continues only while calcium floods the sarcoplasm. When the signal stops, calcium ions are pumped back into the sarcoplasmic cisternae — the reticulum reclaims its store — and with calcium gone, troponin lets the cover fall back over the active sites on actin.
• DEFINITION — Masking restored, the cross bridges can no longer form, and the Z lines return to their original position: the fibre relaxes. Relaxation is therefore not passive collapse but an active reset — pumps spend energy to haul calcium home, which is why relaxation also fails when energy runs out.
• DEFINITION — Repeated activation of a muscle can lead to fatigue: anaerobic breakdown of glycogen in the muscle accumulates lactic acid, and the muscle's performance falls. NCERT names lactic acid accumulation from anaerobic glycogen breakdown as the cause — another exact-wording fact worth storing.
• EXAMPLE — Hold a heavy bag at arm's length. At first the calcium stays out, bridges cycle and the bag stays up. As stimulation repeats, lactic acid builds, the burn arrives, force fades and the bag sinks — fatigue in one honest minute of your own muscle.
• WHY calcium is the perfect switch — it is stored one membrane away from the filaments, floods the sarcoplasm in an instant, binds troponin specifically, and is pumped home just as fast. A signal switching thousands of cross bridges fully on and off within fractions of a second needs exactly this local, reversible messenger.
• WORKED PATTERN — End-of-contraction sequence: signal stops, acetylcholine cleared at the junction, calcium pumped back into the reticulum, troponin masks the sites again, Z lines return to resting spacing. What directly ends contraction is the removal of calcium from the sarcoplasm — not the arrival of any relaxing chemical.
• YOUR TURN — A toxin keeps the sarcoplasmic reticulum's calcium channels permanently open. What state does the muscle end up in? Answer: prolonged contraction — calcium never leaves the sarcoplasm and cross bridges keep cycling as long as ATP lasts.
✔ KEY POINT — Three stored sentences close the contraction story: calcium out means contract, calcium home means relax; sites re-mask when calcium is pumped back; fatigue is lactic acid from anaerobic glycogen breakdown during repeated activation.

Red Fibres and White Fibres — Two Engine Designs
• DEFINITION — The red muscle fibres owe their colour to a high content of myoglobin, the red oxygen-storing pigment, packed inside them. They also contain plenty of mitochondria, which use the large amounts of oxygen stored in the fibres for ATP production — red fibres are aerobic muscles, built for sustained work.
• DEFINITION — The white fibres are the opposite design: the amount of myoglobin in them is very low, so they appear pale or whitish, and they possess very few mitochondria. For energy they depend on anaerobic processes — fast fuel, borrowed against a lactic acid debt.
• EXAMPLE — The marathoner versus the sprinter, in tissue form. Posture muscles and long-distance runners' muscles run the red, aerobic design: steady oxygen, steady ATP, no early fatigue. The explosive muscles of a sprint start or sudden jump run whiter: enormous power for seconds, paid for with rapid fatigue.
• WHY colour is chemistry, not decoration — myoglobin is both the paint and the warehouse: it colours the fibre red precisely because it holds oxygen in reserve. Where the warehouse is big and mitochondria many, oxygen-based ATP production dominates; where both are small, the fibre burns fuel without oxygen and accepts the debt.
• WORKED PATTERN — Fibre questions are two-column matching with short NCERT entries: red — high myoglobin, plenty of mitochondria, aerobic, fatigue-resistant; white — low myoglobin, few mitochondria, anaerobic, fast fatigue. The classic trap swaps exactly one entry between columns (red fibres with few mitochondria), so check every cell, not just the first.
• YOUR TURN — A muscle must contract continuously for an hour without rest. Which fibre design should dominate it, and which two contents will be high? Answer: red fibres — high myoglobin and plenty of mitochondria, feeding aerobic ATP production.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not confuse myoglobin with haemoglobin outside their addresses: haemoglobin is the oxygen carrier of blood, myoglobin the oxygen store inside muscle fibres. And note the logic's direction — red fibres are not red because they work aerobically; they work aerobically because they store oxygen in myoglobin and own many mitochondria. Cause first, colour second.

The Skeleton at a Glance — 206 Bones in Two Divisions
• DEFINITION — The skeletal system consists of a framework of bones and a few cartilages. In an adult human it has exactly 206 bones, and NCERT divides the framework into two clear divisions: the axial skeleton and the appendicular skeleton.
• DEFINITION — The axial skeleton runs along the main axis of the body and comprises 80 bones: skull, vertebral column, sternum and ribs — the protective core housing the brain, spinal cord and thoracic organs, and carrying the body's weight line.
• DEFINITION — The appendicular skeleton comprises 126 bones: the bones of the limbs and the girdles that attach the limbs to the axis. Check the audit that examiners love: 80 plus 126 equals 206 — if your sub-counts ever fail to reach these totals, a part has been miscounted.
• WHY a frame of separate bones — a single solid shell could protect but never bend, grow or be repaired piece by piece. Separate bones joined at joints give a structure rigid where it must shield (skull, rib cage) and mobile where it must move (limbs), cartilage cushioning the meeting points.
• EXAMPLE — Weigh the design like an engineer: about two-fifths of your bones (80 of 206) form the central axis that mostly protects and supports, while three-fifths (126) belong to the limbs and girdles that mostly move you. Most of the bone budget is spent on locomotion.
• WORKED PATTERN — Every counting question is arithmetic with fixed NCERT addends: skull 29, column 26, ribs 24, sternum 1 (sum 80); limbs 30 each, four limbs 120, girdles 4 plus 2 (sum 126). Memorise the addends, not just the totals, because options corrupt one addend at a time — 27 vertebrae, 28 skull bones, 8 tarsals — and dare you to add without noticing.
• YOUR TURN — A chart claims the appendicular skeleton has 120 bones. What did the chart forget? Answer: the girdles — 120 counts only the four limbs (30 each); adding the pectoral girdle (4) and pelvic girdle (2) gives the true 126.
✔ KEY POINT — The three totals that must never wobble: 206 bones overall, 80 axial, 126 appendicular. Everything else in the skeleton sections is the breakdown of these three numbers.

The Axial Skeleton — Skull, Spine, Ribs and Sternum
• DEFINITION — The skull is composed of two sets of bones, cranial and facial, totalling 22 bones: 8 cranial bones form the hard protective cranium for the brain, and 14 facial bones form the front part of the skull. Add the ear ossicles and the hyoid (below) and the skull region counts 29 bones in NCERT bookkeeping.
• DEFINITION — Each middle ear contains three tiny bones — malleus, incus and stapes, the ear ossicles, six in all — and a single U-shaped bone, the hyoid, is present at the base of the buccal cavity. Both sets are included in the total count of skull bones: 22 plus 6 plus 1 equals 29.
• DEFINITION — The skull articulates with the vertebral column through two occipital condyles — the dicondylic skull. The first vertebra is the atlas, and it articulates with these condyles, forming the joint that lets the head sit and nod on the spine.
• DEFINITION — The vertebral column is dorsally placed and formed by 26 serially arranged units called vertebrae: cervical 7, thoracic 12, lumbar 5, sacral 1 (fused) and coccygeal 1 (fused). It protects the spinal cord, supports the head and serves as the point of attachment for the ribs and back musculature.
• DEFINITION — The sternum is a flat bone on the ventral midline of the thorax. The ribs are 12 pairs of thin flat bones, each connected dorsally to the vertebral column and, for most pairs, ventrally to the sternum; each rib has two dorsal articulation surfaces, so ribs are called bicephalic. Thoracic vertebrae, ribs and sternum together form the rib cage.
• DEFINITION — The first seven pairs of ribs are true ribs (vertebrosternal): dorsally attached to the thoracic vertebrae, ventrally connected to the sternum by hyaline cartilage. The 8th, 9th and 10th pairs are false ribs (vertebrochondral): they join the seventh rib by hyaline cartilage instead of reaching the sternum. The last two pairs, 11th and 12th, are floating ribs (vertebral ribs), unconnected ventrally.
• EXAMPLE — Verify the axial total yourself: 29 plus 26 is 55, plus 24 is 79, plus 1 is 80. The axial skeleton checks out exactly — and any option set totalling 79 or 81 has corrupted exactly one addend.
• WHY ribs come in three classes — the rib cage must be a box that can still breathe. True ribs anchor firmly to the sternum for protection; false ribs borrow the seventh rib's cartilage, keeping the cage flexible lower down; floating ribs protect the back organs while leaving the abdomen free to expand.
• WORKED PATTERN — Rib questions need two facts per pair: the name and the ventral connection. Pairs 1 to 7: true, own cartilage to sternum. Pairs 8 to 10: false, join the seventh rib's cartilage. Pairs 11 to 12: floating, no ventral connection. And one dorsal fact for all: bicephalic, two articulation surfaces on the vertebral end.
• YOUR TURN — A rib joins the cartilage of the series above rather than the sternum, and its number is 9. Classify it. Answer: a false rib (vertebrochondral) — pairs 8, 9 and 10 join the seventh rib with hyaline cartilage instead of reaching the sternum.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Three skull numbers circulate and papers rotate them: 22 skull bones proper (8 cranial plus 14 facial), 29 for the full skull region (22 plus 6 ear ossicles plus 1 hyoid), 80 for the whole axial skeleton. Know which question is being asked before answering. Also: our skull is dicondylic — two occipital condyles — a word examiners love to swap with monocondylic.

The Appendicular Skeleton — Limbs and Girdles
• DEFINITION — The bones of each limb number exactly 30. Fore limb: humerus, radius and ulna, carpals (wrist) 8, metacarpals (palm) 5, phalanges (digits) 14. Hind limb: femur, tibia and fibula, tarsals (ankle) 7, metatarsals 5 and phalanges 14 — plus the patella, the cup-shaped bone covering the knee ventrally, the knee cap.
• DEFINITION — The femur, the thigh bone, is the longest bone of the body. Learn the limb bones in walking order and the counts attach themselves: one long bone above the joint (humerus or femur), two below it (radius-ulna or tibia-fibula), a cluster of small wrist or ankle bones, five palm or sole bones, fourteen in the digits.
• DEFINITION — The pectoral girdle has, on each side, a clavicle and a scapula — four bones in all. The scapula is a large triangular flat bone in the dorsal thorax between the second and seventh ribs; its dorsal ridge, the spine, expands into the acromion, with which the clavicle articulates. Below it lies the glenoid cavity, receiving the humerus head to form the shoulder joint. The clavicle is a long slender bone with two curvatures — the collar bone.
• DEFINITION — The pelvic girdle consists of two coxal bones. Each coxal bone is formed by the fusion of three bones — ilium, ischium and pubis — and at their fusion point sits a cavity, the acetabulum, to which the thigh bone articulates. The two halves meet ventrally to form the pubic symphysis, containing fibrous cartilage.
• EXAMPLE — The shoulder and hip are a designed pair: a girdle cavity (glenoid cavity in the scapula, acetabulum in the coxal bone) receives the ball of the limb's first bone (humerus or femur) to make a ball and socket joint. Same blueprint, two sizes — the hip's socket is deeper, trading mobility for weight-bearing stability.
• WHY girdles exist — limbs cannot bolt directly onto the spine; they need adapter plates that spread limb forces onto the axis and give muscles broad attachment surfaces. The pectoral girdle stays light and mobile for a free-swinging arm; the pelvic girdle is massive and fused for transferring the upper body's weight to the legs.
• WORKED PATTERN — Girdle drill, fixed answers: triangular dorsal pectoral bone — scapula; its cavity for the humerus — glenoid cavity; the slender double-curved bone — clavicle; pelvic socket for the femur — acetabulum; the three bones fused in each coxal bone — ilium, ischium, pubis. Girdle matching questions are assembled from these pieces.
• YOUR TURN — A bone set is listed: ilium, ischium, pubis, and a deep cup. Which single bone do these form, and which limb bone enters the cup? Answer: one coxal bone of the pelvic girdle; the femur's head articulates with its acetabulum.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Carpals are 8 but tarsals are 7 — the wrist-ankle swap is the most reliable one-mark trap in the skeleton. Second favourite: making the patella part of the axial skeleton or forgetting it in the hind limb's 30. Third: placing the glenoid cavity in the pelvic girdle — glenoid is shoulder (pectoral), acetabulum is hip (pelvic).

Joints — Where Bones Meet and Movement Happens
• DEFINITION — Joints are points of contact between bones, or between bones and cartilages, and they are essential for all types of movements involving the bony parts of the body. Locomotor movements are impossible without them: a skeleton of fused bones would be a statue, not an animal.
• DEFINITION — Joints are classified into three major structural forms, and the classification doubles as a movement scale. Fibrous joints allow no movement. Cartilaginous joints allow limited movement. Synovial joints allow considerable, free movement — the scale runs none, some, plenty.
• DEFINITION — In fibrous joints, the flat bones of the skull are fused end to end by dense fibrous connective tissue, forming sutures. The design goal is armour, not motion: the cranium must be a rigid box, so its joints sacrifice movement entirely.
• DEFINITION — In cartilaginous joints, the bones are joined together by cartilage, which permits limited movement — the joint between adjacent vertebrae is the standard example, giving the spine its slight give without letting it collapse.
• DEFINITION — Synovial joints are characterised by a fluid-filled synovial cavity between the articulating surfaces of the two bones. The fluid lubricates the meeting surfaces, and such joints allow considerable movement — NCERT adds that a number of synovial joints, detailed in the next section, help in locomotion and many other movements.
• EXAMPLE — Your spine demonstrates the whole scale in one column: cartilage-joined vertebrae bend a little each, and dozens of small bends add up to touching your toes; the skull above it, fibrous-joined, does not bend at all — while your synovial shoulder swings through the body's largest range.
• WHY classify by structure — because structure predicts movement exactly. What joins the bones decides how far they can travel: rigid fibrous tissue locks, compressible cartilage yields slightly, a lubricated fluid cavity lets surfaces glide. Given the joining material, the movement class is predictable sight unseen.
• WORKED PATTERN — Joint questions come in two shapes: name the family from the example (skull sutures — fibrous; between vertebrae — cartilaginous; limb joints — synovial), or name the movement from the family (fibrous — none; cartilaginous — limited; synovial — considerable). They are the same coin; answer in whichever direction the question flips it.
• YOUR TURN — Which joint family fits a structure that must flex slightly with every breath and step, but never dislocate? Answer: cartilaginous — cartilage gives limited, cushioned movement, as between adjacent vertebrae.
✔ KEY POINT — Three families, three movement grades, three welded examples: fibrous (none, skull sutures), cartilaginous (limited, between vertebrae), synovial (considerable, limb joints, its signature a fluid-filled cavity).

Synovial Joints — Five Designs for Five Jobs
• DEFINITION — The ball and socket joint pairs a rounded head with a cup-shaped cavity, the freest design of all: the NCERT example is the joint between the humerus and the pectoral girdle — the shoulder. A ball in a socket swings in every direction, which is why your arm out-manoeuvres your leg.
• DEFINITION — The hinge joint works like the hinge of a door, allowing movement in one plane only; the NCERT example is the knee joint. One plane sounds limiting, but for a joint that must bear the body's weight while folding and straightening thousands of times a day, restriction is safety.
• DEFINITION — The pivot joint lets one bone rotate around another; the NCERT example is the joint between the atlas and the axis, the first two vertebrae. This is the joint that turns your head when you shake it in disagreement.
• DEFINITION — The gliding joint forms where flat bone surfaces slide over one another; the NCERT example is the joint between the carpals of the wrist. Small slides at many small joints add up to the wrist's supple adjustments.
• DEFINITION — The saddle joint is the thumb's special design, found between the carpal and the metacarpal of the thumb. Its interlocking saddle surfaces give the thumb the movements behind gripping, holding a pen and this exam's handwriting.
• EXAMPLE — Mime the five in ten seconds: swing your arm in a circle (ball and socket, shoulder); bend and straighten your knee (hinge); shake your head no (pivot, atlas-axis); slide one flat palm over the other wrist's back (gliding, carpals); touch your thumb tip to each fingertip (saddle, thumb). Your own body is this section's revision chart.
• WHY five designs rather than one — each joint is a compromise between freedom and stability tuned to its job. The shoulder buys maximum range with a shallow socket; the knee buys stability with a one-plane hinge; the thumb buys grip with a saddle. Match design to example and movement, and any swap in an option shows at once.
• WORKED PATTERN — The matching grid to memorise cold: ball and socket — humerus and pectoral girdle; hinge — knee; pivot — atlas and axis; gliding — between carpals; saddle — carpal and metacarpal of thumb. Five rows, no extras. Favourite wrong options pair pivot with the wrist, hinge with the elbow (not the NCERT example here), and saddle with a toe.
• YOUR TURN — A joint allows a bone to rotate so the head can turn side to side. Name the type and the two bones involved. Answer: pivot joint, between the atlas (first vertebra) and the axis (second vertebra).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The elbow feels like the obvious hinge, but NCERT's named hinge example in this chapter is the knee joint — answer with the book's example when options offer both. And keep pivot (atlas-axis, rotation) separate from the atlanto-occipital articulation where the atlas meets the skull's two condyles; only the atlas-axis pair is the pivot.

When Movement Fails — Disorders of Muscle and Bone
• DEFINITION — Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disorder affecting the neuromuscular junction, leading to fatigue, weakening and paralysis of skeletal muscle. The muscles and bones are intact; the body attacks its own signal-receiving junction, so commands arrive weaker and weaker — the failure is at the motor-end plate you met earlier.
• DEFINITION — Muscular dystrophy is the progressive degeneration of skeletal muscle, mostly due to a genetic disorder. Unlike myasthenia, here the muscle tissue itself wastes away over time, inherited rather than acquired.
• DEFINITION — Tetany is rapid spasm — wild, uncontrolled contraction — in muscle, caused by low calcium ion levels in the body fluid. Connect it to the mechanism: calcium handling goes wrong extracellularly, and muscles fire into spasm instead of orderly contraction.
• DEFINITION — Arthritis is inflammation of joints. Its two common forms deserve separate identities: osteoarthritis is the wear-and-tear form, where the articular cartilage cushioning a joint degenerates with age and use, so bone grinds on bone; rheumatoid arthritis is autoimmune, the immune system attacking the synovial membrane, inflaming and deforming the joint.
• DEFINITION — Gout is inflammation of joints due to the accumulation of uric acid crystals. The chemistry is the diagnosis: excess uric acid precipitates as crystals inside joints, and the joint responds with painful inflammation.
• DEFINITION — Osteoporosis is an age-related disorder characterised by decreased bone mass and increased chances of fractures; decreased levels of estrogen are a common cause. The bone is not inflamed or infected — it is simply thinner, so ordinary loads begin to break it.
• EXAMPLE — Six patients, six clean diagnoses. Eyelids droop, grip fades by evening, autoimmune: myasthenia gravis. A child's muscles waste yearly, family history present: muscular dystrophy. Wild spasms, low blood calcium: tetany. An elderly knee grinding as cartilage wears: osteoarthritis. Autoimmune-inflamed small joints: rheumatoid arthritis. A big toe red-hot with uric acid crystals: gout. A wrist fractured in a minor fall, low estrogen: osteoporosis.
• WHY each disorder maps to one component — this section is the chapter read backwards. Junction fails: myasthenia. Muscle machinery inherited faulty: dystrophy. Calcium balance fails: tetany. Joint surfaces inflame or wear: arthritis and gout. Bone mass falls: osteoporosis. Diagnose by location of failure, not memorised pairings.
• WORKED PATTERN — Disorder drill: autoimmune plus junction equals myasthenia gravis; genetic plus degeneration equals muscular dystrophy; low calcium plus spasms equals tetany; joint inflammation equals arthritis (wear: osteoarthritis; autoimmune synovium: rheumatoid); uric acid crystals equals gout; low bone mass plus low estrogen equals osteoporosis. Six equations, six keywords.
• YOUR TURN — A patient's joints are inflamed and tests find crystals of uric acid inside them, while another patient of the same age shows thinning bones on a scan but no joint inflammation. Name both conditions. Answer: gout for the crystal-inflamed joints; osteoporosis for the thinned, fracture-prone bones.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Tetany and tetanus are different words and different conditions — tetany here is the low-calcium spasm disorder of this chapter. Also watch the arthritis pair: osteoarthritis is mechanical wear of cartilage, rheumatoid arthritis is autoimmune attack on the synovial membrane; options routinely exchange their causes.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Four movements: amoeboid (pseudopodia by streaming protoplasm — leucocytes, macrophages), ciliary (trachea sweeping dust, ovum transport), flagellar (sperm, sponge canal current, Euglena), muscular (limbs, jaws, tongue; locomotion coordinates muscular, skeletal and neural systems)
✔ KEY POINT — Muscle is mesodermal, 40-50 percent of adult body weight, with four properties — excitability, contractility, extensibility, elasticity. Skeletal: striated and voluntary. Smooth: non-striated and involuntary, in visceral walls. Cardiac: branched, striated, involuntary
✔ KEY POINT — Hierarchy: muscle, then fascicles in fascia, then fibres lined by sarcolemma, then myofibrils. The fibre is a syncytium with many nuclei; the sarcoplasmic reticulum stores calcium ions
✔ KEY POINT — Bands: I band is light, isotropic, actin only, bisected by the Z line. A band is dark, anisotropic, myosin, centred on the M line. H zone is the actin-free centre of the A band at rest. Sarcomere equals Z line to Z line, the functional unit of contraction
✔ KEY POINT — Actin filament: two F actins (polymers of G actin beads) wound helically, with tropomyosin strands alongside and troponin complexes at regular intervals; at rest a troponin subunit masks the myosin-binding sites on actin
✔ KEY POINT — Myosin filament: many meromyosins; each meromyosin equals HMM (globular head plus short arm — the cross arm, an ATPase with ATP and actin binding sites) plus LMM (tail)
✔ KEY POINT — Signal chain: CNS to motor neuron to motor-end plate (neuromuscular junction); acetylcholine released; action potential spreads over the sarcolemma; calcium pours from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Motor unit equals one motor neuron plus all its fibres
✔ KEY POINT — Contraction cycle: calcium binds troponin and unmasks the sites; the myosin head, powered by ATP hydrolysis, binds actin as a cross bridge and pulls actin towards the A-band centre; Z lines are pulled in; new ATP binding breaks the bridge; repeat while calcium stays
✔ KEY POINT — Band changes in contraction: I bands get reduced, A bands retain their length, H zone shrinks or disappears, Z lines come closer, and neither filament shortens — filaments slide, per the sliding filament theory
✔ KEY POINT — Relaxation and fatigue: calcium pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum re-masks the sites and the Z lines return; repeated activation accumulates lactic acid from anaerobic breakdown of glycogen, causing fatigue
✔ KEY POINT — Fibres: red fibres have plenty of myoglobin and mitochondria and work aerobically; white fibres have very little myoglobin, very few mitochondria and depend on anaerobic processes
✔ KEY POINT — Skeleton audit: 206 equals axial 80 (skull region 29 equals cranial 8 plus facial 14 plus ear ossicles 6 plus hyoid 1; column 26 equals 7, 12, 5, 1 fused, 1 fused; ribs 24; sternum 1) plus appendicular 126 (four limbs of 30, girdles 4 plus 2)
✔ KEY POINT — Ribs and limbs: true ribs 1-7 (vertebrosternal), false ribs 8-10 (vertebrochondral, join the seventh rib), floating ribs 11-12; ribs are bicephalic. Each limb has 30 bones; carpals 8, metacarpals 5, tarsals 7, metatarsals 5, phalanges 14; femur longest; patella covers the knee
✔ KEY POINT — Joints: fibrous (no movement, skull sutures), cartilaginous (limited, between vertebrae), synovial (free, fluid-filled cavity) — ball and socket shoulder, hinge knee, pivot atlas-axis, gliding carpals, saddle thumb. Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Saying the A band shortens during contraction. Why it is wrong: the A band is the length of the thick filaments, and thick filaments do not shorten — sliding filament theory says filaments glide, not shrink. Correct principle: I bands reduce, A bands retain their length, H zone shrinks, Z lines approach.
• Mistake 2: Claiming actin filaments contract or fold up. Why it is wrong: neither actin nor myosin changes length; only their overlap changes as actin slides towards the centre of the A band. Correct principle: the sarcomere shortens because filaments slide over each other, pulling the Z lines inward.
• Mistake 3: Putting the calcium store in the sarcolemma or the nucleus. Why it is wrong: the sarcolemma is the fibre's outer membrane; the store house of calcium ions is the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the fibre's specialised endoplasmic reticulum. Correct principle: the reticulum stores and releases the calcium that switches contraction on and off.
• Mistake 4: Making ATP necessary only for the pull, forgetting detachment. Why it is wrong: the head also needs fresh ATP to bind in order to break the cross bridge after the stroke; without it the bridge stays locked — the stiffness of rigor mortis. Correct principle: ATP hydrolysis arms and powers the head; ATP binding releases it.
• Mistake 5: Swapping the addresses of troponin and the sites it guards. Why it is wrong: troponin sits on tropomyosin along the actin filament and masks active sites on actin; nothing is masked on myosin. Correct principle: troponin and tropomyosin belong to the thin filament; the sites they cover are actin's sites for myosin heads.
• Mistake 6: Counting the skull as 22 when the question asks for the skull region total, or 29 when it asks only for skull bones. Why it is wrong: 22 counts cranial (8) plus facial (14) only; the NCERT skull-region total of 29 adds six ear ossicles and the hyoid. Correct principle: read which total is asked — 22 for the skull proper, 29 with ear ossicles and hyoid.
• Mistake 7: Writing 33 vertebrae or 31 vertebrae for the adult column in an NCERT answer. Why it is wrong: the column is formed by 26 units — cervical 7, thoracic 12, lumbar 5, sacral 1 fused, coccygeal 1 fused; the two fused blocks count as one unit each. Correct principle: 7 plus 12 plus 5 plus 1 plus 1 equals 26.
• Mistake 8: Giving carpals 7 and tarsals 8. Why it is wrong: the counts are carpals (wrist) 8 and tarsals (ankle) 7 — the pair sums the same, so the sum cannot rescue a swap; only the addresses differ. Correct principle: wrist eight, ankle seven; metacarpals and metatarsals are 5 each, phalanges 14 in every limb.
• Mistake 9: Naming the elbow as this chapter's hinge, or the wrist as the pivot. Why it is wrong: NCERT's listed hinge is the knee, the pivot is atlas-axis, and the wrist's carpal joints are gliding. Correct principle: use the chapter's own examples — hinge knee, pivot atlas-axis, gliding carpals, saddle thumb, ball and socket shoulder.
• Mistake 10: Mixing up the six disorders. Why it is wrong: each owns a unique trigger word — myasthenia gravis autoimmune at the junction, dystrophy genetic, tetany low calcium, gout uric acid crystals, osteoporosis low bone mass with low estrogen, arthritis joint inflammation (wear versus autoimmune). Correct principle: diagnose from the trigger word in the stem, not vague symptom similarity.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A sarcomere shows a clear H zone at rest. Predict the state of the H zone, the I band and the A band after full contraction, with reasons.
• Given: Resting sarcomere — thin filaments partially overlap the thick filaments; H zone is the actin-free centre of the A band; I band is the actin-only region near each Z line.
• Principle: Contraction is sliding: myosin heads pull actin towards the centre of the A band, the Z lines are drawn inward, and the bands change only because overlap changes.
• Steps: Actin slides inward, covering more of the thick filament centre — the H zone shrinks and may disappear. The gap between Z line and A band edge narrows — the I band is reduced. The thick filaments do not move or shorten — the A band keeps their length.
• Answer: H zone reduced or absent, I band reduced, A band unchanged.
• Reasoning: Every band answer follows from one picture: actin moving inwards over a stationary myosin track. Replay that picture and the table memorises itself.
• Mistake to avoid: Writing that the H zone widens because the muscle is working harder, or that the A band shortens with the sarcomere — the sarcomere shortens, the A band does not.
• Example 2 — Problem: Verify from its parts that the axial skeleton contains exactly 80 bones.
• Given: Skull region 29 bones; vertebral column 26; ribs 12 pairs; sternum 1.
• Principle: The axial skeleton is skull plus vertebral column plus ribs plus sternum, and the ribs must be counted as individual bones, not pairs.
• Steps: Convert ribs first: 12 pairs means 24 bones. Now add: 29 (skull) plus 26 (column) equals 55. Add 24 (ribs) equals 79. Add 1 (sternum) equals 80.
• Answer: 80 bones exactly — the audit closes.
• Reasoning: Two traps hide in the givens: pairs must be doubled, and the skull must be the 29 of the full region (with ear ossicles and hyoid), not the 22 of the skull bones alone. Handle both and the sum lands exactly on 80.
• Mistake to avoid: Adding 12 instead of 24 for the ribs, which produces the classic wrong total of 68, or using 22 for the skull, which produces 73.
• Example 3 — Problem: A student claims the appendicular skeleton has 124 bones: each limb 30, and the girdles 4. Find the error and give the correct total.
• Given: Four limbs; each limb 30 bones; two girdles — pectoral and pelvic.
• Principle: Appendicular total equals limb bones plus girdle bones; the pelvic girdle has two coxal bones and the pectoral girdle four (clavicle and scapula each side).
• Steps: Limbs: 4 times 30 equals 120. Girdles: pectoral 4 plus pelvic 2 equals 6, not 4 — the student used the pectoral count for both. Total: 120 plus 6 equals 126.
• Answer: The error is counting only 4 girdle bones; the correct appendicular total is 126, and the full skeleton is 80 plus 126 equals 206.
• Reasoning: In audit questions, rebuild girdle figures from named bones: clavicle 2 plus scapula 2 pectoral, coxal 2 pelvic.
• Mistake to avoid: Also counting the patella twice or omitting it — it is already inside the hind limb's 30 (femur 1, patella 1, tibia 1, fibula 1, tarsals 7, metatarsals 5, phalanges 14).
• Example 4 — Problem: Arrange these contraction events in the correct order and justify each step: (a) myosin head binds actin forming a cross bridge; (b) calcium ions bind troponin; (c) acetylcholine is released at the motor-end plate; (d) calcium ions are released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum; (e) the active sites on actin are exposed.
• Given: The events span two locations — the junction between nerve and muscle, and the filaments inside the sarcomere.
• Principle: The signal chain is fixed: junction chemistry first, then membrane electricity, then calcium, then unmasking, then binding.
• Steps: (c) Acetylcholine is released first, at the motor-end plate, generating the action potential. That triggers (d) calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Then (b) calcium binds troponin, causing (e) exposure of the active sites. Only now can (a) the head bind and form the cross bridge.
• Answer: c, then d, then b, then e, then a.
• Reasoning: Each event is the cause of the next; an order question is solved by asking of each pair, which one must exist before the other can happen. Binding (a) cannot precede exposure (e); exposure cannot precede calcium binding (b); calcium cannot bind before it is released (d); release cannot precede the junction signal (c).
• Mistake to avoid: Placing calcium release after troponin binding — troponin binds calcium that is already free in the sarcoplasm, so release must come first.
• Example 5 — Problem: Muscle P is deep red, rich in myoglobin, with abundant mitochondria. Muscle Q is pale, with little myoglobin and few mitochondria. Assign each its job — holding posture for hours, or powering a sprint start — and its energy route.
• Given: The fibre design table: red design equals high myoglobin, many mitochondria, aerobic; white design equals low myoglobin, few mitochondria, anaerobic.
• Principle: Match the design to the demand: sustained work needs oxygen-based ATP (aerobic) and fatigue resistance; brief maximal work can run on anaerobic bursts and accept fatigue.
• Steps: Muscle P carries the red design, producing ATP aerobically from stored oxygen — assign it posture duty over hours. Muscle Q carries the white design, built for anaerobic power — assign it the sprint start, expecting quick fatigue.
• Answer: P — posture, aerobic. Q — sprint start, anaerobic.
• Reasoning: The colour is clue and conclusion at once: myoglobin makes the fibre red and makes aerobic work possible. Examiners give histology and ask physiology; the two-column table converts one into the other.
• Mistake to avoid: Assigning the pale muscle to posture because sprinter muscles look strong — strength for seconds is not endurance; posture is an aerobic, fatigue-resistance job.
• Example 6 — Problem: Identify the joint type and partner bones for each movement: (i) turning the head to look behind; (ii) folding the leg at the knee; (iii) the thumb gripping a pen; (iv) the wrist's small sliding adjustments; (v) swinging the arm in a full circle.
• Given: The five synovial designs with their NCERT examples: ball and socket (humerus-pectoral girdle), hinge (knee), pivot (atlas-axis), gliding (carpals), saddle (carpal-metacarpal of thumb).
• Principle: Name the movement first in mechanical words — rotation, one-plane fold, grip, slide, all-direction swing — then read the design off the grid.
• Steps: (i) Head rotation: pivot, atlas-axis. (ii) One-plane fold: hinge, knee. (iii) Thumb grip: saddle, carpal with metacarpal of thumb. (iv) Sliding: gliding, between carpals. (v) All-direction swing: ball and socket, humerus with pectoral girdle.
• Answer: (i) pivot — atlas and axis; (ii) hinge — knee; (iii) saddle — thumb base; (iv) gliding — carpals; (v) ball and socket — shoulder.
• Reasoning: The grid has exactly five rows, so a five-part question is the grid dictated back. If one answer repeats a joint type, a movement has been misread — recheck its mechanics.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling head-turning a ball and socket movement because the head feels round — the rotation happens at the pivot between atlas and axis, below the skull.
• Example 7 — Problem: By evening a patient's eyelids droop, chewing tires her jaw and her grip weakens, yet scans show her muscles and bones are structurally normal, and her immune system is attacking the junction between nerve and muscle. Diagnose the disorder, name the structure attacked, and say whether rest fits the mechanism.
• Given: Fatigue and weakening of skeletal muscle, normal muscle structure, autoimmune cause located at the neuromuscular junction.
• Principle: Myasthenia gravis is the autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction, producing fatigue, weakening and paralysis of skeletal muscle while the fibres themselves are sound.
• Steps: Match the keywords: autoimmune plus neuromuscular junction plus fatigue-weakness is the exact NCERT signature of myasthenia gravis. The attacked structure is the motor-end plate. The fault is signal delivery, not calcium release inside the fibre, so rest — letting the weakened junction recover — fits the mechanism; the filaments are intact.
• Answer: Myasthenia gravis; the neuromuscular junction (motor-end plate) is attacked; rest fits the mechanism better, because the failure is in signal transmission, not in the filaments or their calcium supply.
• Reasoning: Disorder questions are keyword equations. Autoimmune plus junction is unique to myasthenia gravis — muscular dystrophy would need genetic degeneration of the muscle itself, which the normal scans exclude; tetany would need low calcium and spasms, the opposite symptom direction.
• Mistake to avoid: Choosing muscular dystrophy because the muscles are weak — in dystrophy the muscle tissue degenerates (scans would show it) and the cause is genetic, not an immune attack on the junction.

Practice Questions — Self-Test Set
• Q1. A leucocyte crawls through a capillary wall towards an infection, while in the trachea above it, particles are being swept upwards. Name the two movement types and the structures responsible in each case.
• Q2. Write the contraction chain in order, from a signal arriving at the motor-end plate to the formation of a cross bridge. Your chain must include acetylcholine, the action potential, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, calcium and troponin.
• Q3. In a contracted sarcomere, state what happens to the I band, the A band, the H zone and the distance between Z lines, and give the one-line reason that explains all four answers.
• Q4. Deconstruct the number 29 for the skull region into its four components, and the number 26 for the vertebral column into its five components.
• Q5. A muscle fibre keeps receiving signals but its sarcoplasmic reticulum cannot pump calcium back. Predict the state of the muscle and name the step of the contraction cycle that fails.
• Q6. An athlete's calf muscle is built for a 100 metre sprint: predict its colour, myoglobin content, mitochondrial density and energy route, and the trade-off this design accepts.
• Q7. Match each movement to its synovial joint type and example pair: rotating the head, folding the knee, gripping with the thumb, sliding wrist bones, swinging the arm.
• Q8. Two patients: one has wild muscle spasms and low calcium in the blood; the other, elderly, fractures a wrist in a minor fall and has low estrogen. Name both disorders and the system each belongs to.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. The leucocyte moves amoeboid-style, by pseudopodia formed by streaming protoplasm with microfilaments; the trachea uses ciliary movement, the coordinated beating of cilia on its lining epithelium. Misconception watch: both are non-muscular, but the mechanisms differ — crawling by protoplasmic streaming versus beating of cilia; swapping labels is the standard trap.
• A2. Acetylcholine at the motor-end plate, then action potential over the sarcolemma, then calcium released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, then calcium binds a troponin subunit and unmasks the active sites on actin, then the myosin head (powered by ATP hydrolysis) binds — a cross bridge. Misconception watch: calcium binds troponin only after release; any chain binding before releasing, or binding myosin before unmasking, is broken.
• A3. I band reduced, A band unchanged, H zone reduced or gone, Z lines closer together. The reason: actin slides inward over myosin, increasing overlap, while the filaments keep their lengths — so every region defined by non-overlap shrinks and the thick-filament region stays. Misconception watch: the A band is the length of the myosin filaments; it cannot change unless they do, and sliding filament theory says they do not.
• A4. Skull 29 equals cranial 8 plus facial 14 plus ear ossicles 6 (malleus, incus, stapes per ear) plus hyoid 1. Column 26 equals cervical 7 plus thoracic 12 plus lumbar 5 plus sacral 1 (fused) plus coccygeal 1 (fused). Misconception watch: the fused sacral and coccygeal blocks count once each — counting originally separate vertebrae inflates the column beyond 26.
• A5. The muscle stays contracted and cannot relax: calcium remains in the sarcoplasm, troponin keeps the actin sites unmasked and cross bridges keep cycling while ATP lasts. The failing step is the reset — pumping calcium back into the cisternae to re-mask the sites. Misconception watch: relaxation is an active, energy-using process driven by calcium removal, not the mere absence of a signal.
• A6. Pale or white fibres: very low myoglobin (hence the pale colour), very few mitochondria, energy from anaerobic processes. The trade-off: enormous short-burst power bought with rapid fatigue and lactic acid accumulation. Misconception watch: white does not mean weak — it means specialised for brief, powerful, anaerobic effort: the sprinter's design, not the marathoner's.
• A7. Rotating the head — pivot, atlas and axis. Folding the knee — hinge, knee joint. Gripping with the thumb — saddle, carpal and metacarpal of the thumb. Sliding wrist bones — gliding, carpals. Swinging the arm — ball and socket, humerus and pectoral girdle. Misconception watch: the elbow is the hinge students reach for first, but the chapter's named hinge is the knee; head rotation is atlas-axis pivot work.
• A8. The spasm patient has tetany — wild, rapid spasms from low calcium ions in the body fluid, a muscular-system disorder. The fracture patient has osteoporosis — age-related loss of bone mass, low estrogen a common cause, a skeletal-system disorder. Misconception watch: tetany is not tetanus, and osteoporosis is thinning of bone, not joint inflammation (arthritis) or crystal deposition (gout).

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: Movement comes in four NCERT designs — amoeboid crawling by pseudopodia (leucocytes, macrophages), ciliary sweeping (trachea, ovum transport), flagellar propulsion (sperm, sponge canals, Euglena) and muscular contraction for limbs, jaws, tongue and locomotion, always as a team of muscular, skeletal and neural systems.
• Summary: Muscle tissue is mesodermal, 40 to 50 percent of adult body weight, and comes as skeletal (striated, voluntary), smooth (non-striated, involuntary) and cardiac (branched, striated, involuntary). A skeletal muscle is bundles in fascia, fibres in sarcolemma, fibrils inside — the fibre a syncytium whose sarcoplasmic reticulum banks the calcium.
• Summary: The sarcomere, Z line to Z line, is the functional unit: I band (actin, bisected by Z line), A band (myosin, centred by M line), H zone (actin-free centre at rest). Actin carries masked sites guarded by troponin; myosin heads (HMM of meromyosin) are ATPase cross arms. Calcium unmasks, ATP powers the pull, new ATP breaks the bridge; the bands report the slide: I reduced, A retained, H shrunk, Z lines closer.
• Summary: The skeleton totals 206 — axial 80 (skull region 29, column 26, ribs 24 in three classes, sternum 1) and appendicular 126 (four limbs of 30 with carpals 8, tarsals 7; girdles 4 plus 2). Joints grade movement from none (fibrous) through limited (cartilaginous) to free (synovial, five designs); the six disorders are the failure modes of junction, muscle, calcium, joint and bone mass.
