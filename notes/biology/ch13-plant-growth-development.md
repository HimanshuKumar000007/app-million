Introduction — Growth Is a One-Way Street
• A cutting planted in moist soil becomes a shrub, a shrub becomes a tree, and the tree never shrinks back into a cutting. That one-way, irreversible quality is the signature of growth — and this chapter is about how a plant controls it: first with five hormone groups of its own, then by reading the environment — the length of the night, the memory of winter, and a seed's decision to sleep or wake.
• DEFINITION — Growth is an irreversible, permanent increase in the size of an organ, a part of an organ, or even an individual cell. It is among the most fundamental characteristics of living beings, and it is always accompanied by metabolic processes, anabolic and catabolic, that run at the expense of energy.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — the IAT and NEET-style papers recycle a small cast every year: the arithmetic-versus-geometric contrast, the leaf example for growth rates, dedifferentiation, the five PGRs and their discovery stories, 2,4-D, the night-break experiment, and the spring-versus-winter wheat logic of vernalisation. Pattern questions — drilled once, they pay out every season.
• EXAMPLE — Growth wears many costumes: a pollen tube lengthens by millimetres, a leaf expands by square centimetres, a root tip manufactures thousands of cells an hour. The chapter keeps returning to these yardsticks, so fix them early.
• WORKED PATTERN — Three questions for any process in this chapter: (1) Is it growth, differentiation, or a PGR-directed response? (2) If a hormone is involved, is it a promoter (auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin) or an inhibitor-side player (abscisic acid, ethylene)? (3) If the environment is involved, is the cue light duration, cold, or a dormancy block being released? Answer those and the chapter's tables fill themselves.
• YOUR TURN — A seedling's root grows longer every day but its diameter stays almost the same in the first weeks. Which kind of growth dominates — the one that elongates or the one that adds girth? Answer: elongation — primary growth from the root apical meristem; girth comes later, from lateral meristems.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The whole chapter in one breath: meristems build the plant by arithmetic and geometric increase, five chemical messengers steer the building, and three environmental calendars — night length, winter cold, and dormancy release — decide when the plant flowers and when its seeds wake.

Growth — What It Is and Where It Happens
• DEFINITION — Plant growth is indeterminate and open-ended because plants retain localised regions of meristems — self-perpetuating tissues of dividing cells — for their entire life. New cells are always being added to the plant body, so a plant can grow throughout its life in a way no animal can.
• DEFINITION — Primary growth is the elongation of the plant along its axis, carried out by the root apical meristem and the shoot apical meristem. Secondary growth is the increase in girth, carried out by lateral meristems — the vascular cambium and cork cambium — which appear later in life in dicotyledonous plants and gymnosperms.
• EXAMPLE — A maize root apical meristem can give rise to more than 17,500 new cells per hour — growth measured as cell production. At the other extreme, a single cell of a watermelon can increase in size by up to 350,000 times — growth measured as pure cell enlargement, with no division at all.
• DEFINITION — Growth is measurable, but the ruler changes with the organ: fresh weight or dry weight for a whole plant, length for a pollen tube, surface area for a leaf or dorsiventral organ, volume for a fruit, and cell number for a meristem culture. Choosing the parameter is part of reading any growth question.
• WHY water is the silent partner — cells enlarge mainly because water rushes into their vacuoles and stretches the wall under turgor. Water also provides the medium for the enzymatic activity growth depends on, so a water-stressed plant stops growing long before it runs out of raw materials.
• DEFINITION — A growing cell passes through three overlapping phases: the meristematic phase (rich protoplasm, large conspicuous nucleus, thin primary walls, abundant plasmodesmatal connections), the elongation phase (increased vacuolation, cell enlargement and new wall deposition), and the maturation phase (maximal size, wall thickenings and protoplasmic modifications for a final job).
• EXAMPLE — Oxygen is a growth requirement, not a background detail: it releases the metabolic energy needed for growth activities, which is why waterlogged roots, starved of oxygen, stop elongating. Nutrients — macro and micro essential elements — are equally essential, for building protoplasm and as a source of energy.
• WORKED PATTERN — When a question describes a structure, place it on the map before anything else: shoot or root tip region with dense protoplasm and big nuclei means meristematic zone; large vacuoles and stretching walls mean elongation zone; thick, modified walls mean maturation zone. The zone names the process, and the process usually is the answer.
• YOUR TURN — A cell has a huge central vacuole, a stretched thin wall and no division activity. Which phase is it in? Answer: the elongation phase — vacuolation and enlargement are its signatures.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Primary growth and secondary growth are sorted by the meristem, not by the organ's age: elongation of a root tip is always primary (apical meristem), while girth increase of an old stem is secondary (lateral meristems). Students who sort by "young versus old" walk into the swap.

Growth Rates — Arithmetic and Geometric
• DEFINITION — The growth rate is the increased growth per unit time. It can be arithmetic or geometric, and the difference between the two is simply a question of what the daughter cells do after mitosis.
• DEFINITION — In arithmetic growth, only one daughter cell continues to divide after mitosis while the other differentiates and matures. The increase is therefore constant, step by step. A root elongating at a constant rate is the standard example.
✔ KEY POINT — Arithmetic growth plots as a straight line — a linear curve. If the initial length is L0 and the constant rate is r, the length at time t is Lt = L0 + rt. The equation is the fingerprint: linear in an option means arithmetic growth.
• DEFINITION — In geometric growth, both daughter cells retain the capacity to divide, and so does every generation after them. The population of dividing cells doubles each round, and the increase itself keeps increasing.
• EXAMPLE — A zygote dividing into 2, then 4, then 8, then 16 cells is geometric growth in action — every product of division stays in the dividing pool. The early development of an embryo runs on this pattern before cells begin to specialise.
✔ KEY POINT — Geometric growth is exponential: the final size W1 = W0 multiplied by e raised to the power rt, where W0 is the initial size, r is the relative growth rate and t is the time of growth. The rate r is also called the efficiency index — a measure of the capacity of a plant to produce new plant material.
• WHY most real growth is neither pure line nor pure exponential forever — resources are limited. A culture or an organism starts slow, accelerates geometrically while resources last, and then levels off as food, space or light runs short. That flattening converts the exponential into the sigmoid curve of the next section — examiners test the junction between the two ideas.
• WORKED PATTERN — Identify the pattern from the data before reaching for names: equal increments per unit time (5, 10, 15, 20) mean arithmetic; equal multiplication factors per unit time (5, 10, 20, 40) mean geometric. If a question gives you a straight-line graph, it has already answered "arithmetic" — do not talk yourself into the sigmoid.
• YOUR TURN — A root is 4 cm long and elongates at a constant 0.5 cm per day. How long after 10 days? Answer: Lt = 4 + 0.5 x 10 = 9 cm — arithmetic growth, because the daily increment never changes.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — In arithmetic growth it is not true that "both cells stop dividing" — one daughter continues dividing and the other matures. And in geometric growth the lag phase still exists at the start; "geometric" describes the division behaviour, not an instant takeoff.

The Sigmoid Curve — Lag, Log and Stationary
• DEFINITION — If the growth of an organism or organ is plotted against time under natural conditions, the curve is typically sigmoid — an S-shaped curve. This happens because growth in a living system is influenced by the environment and by the limited supply of nutrients, space and light.
• DEFINITION — The sigmoid curve has three phases: the lag phase, where growth is slow while cells adapt and machinery is built; the log or exponential phase, where growth is fastest and geometric; and the stationary phase, where growth slows and levels off as resources are exhausted or maturity is reached.
✔ KEY POINT — The sigmoid is the geometric pattern wearing real-world clothes: lag plus log plus stationary. The parameter r in the exponential equation measures efficiency during the log phase — plants with a higher r build new material faster from the same starting mass.
• EXAMPLE — A bacterial culture, a growing leaf, and a child's height all trace the same S: a quiet start, a surge, and a plateau. In a sigmoid question, the steepest point of the curve is the log phase — the moment of maximum growth rate.
• WHY the stationary phase is not death — the curve flattens because net increase stops, not because metabolism stops. Cells still respire, maintain themselves and often shift into differentiation and reproduction; zero growth rate is a statement about size, not about life.
• WORKED PATTERN — Read a sigmoid question in this order: name the phase from the slope (flat-slow = lag, steep = log, flat-level = stationary), then connect it to resources (plenty = log; exhausted = stationary). Two-step reading converts a picture question into a one-line answer.
• YOUR TURN — A culture grows slowly for two days, explodes on days three to five, and stops increasing by day eight. Which phase covers days three to five? Answer: the log (exponential) phase — the steep middle of the S.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The sigmoid curve is NOT a third type of cell division alongside arithmetic and geometric. It is what geometric growth looks like over time when resources run out. Options that list "sigmoid growth" as a division pattern are bait.

Absolute versus Relative Growth Rate
• DEFINITION — Absolute growth rate is the measurement and comparison of total growth per unit time — the raw increase itself, in square centimetres, grams or cells, with no reference to how big the system already was.
• DEFINITION — Relative growth rate is the growth of a given system per unit time expressed on a common basis — per unit of the initial parameter. It answers not "how much was added?" but "how much was added for every unit already present?"
• EXAMPLE — Two leaves, A and B, of different sizes both increase by exactly 5 square centimetres in a day. Leaf A started at 5 square centimetres and reached 10; leaf B started at 50 and reached 55. Their absolute growth rates are identical — 5 square centimetres each.
✔ KEY POINT — The NCERT leaf verdict: leaf A grew from 5 to 10 square centimetres, a relative growth rate of 100 percent; leaf B grew from 50 to 55, a relative growth rate of only 10 percent. Same absolute growth, tenfold difference in relative growth — relative rate is the fair comparison between systems of different sizes.
• WHY relative rate falls as things get bigger — a large leaf adds new tissue only at its margins and through a limited meristematic fraction, while its whole existing mass counts in the denominator. Big systems therefore show small relative rates even when their absolute gains are impressive — this is also why a seedling out-grows a tree in percentage terms.
• WORKED PATTERN — Given two systems and their before-and-after sizes: compute the absolute increase (final minus initial) for each; if asked for relative growth rate, divide each increase by its own initial size and multiply by 100. The question word decides the arithmetic — "total" means absolute, "per unit initial" means relative.
• YOUR TURN — Leaf X grows from 20 to 26 square centimetres in a day; leaf Y grows from 4 to 8. Which has the higher absolute growth rate, and which the higher relative? Answer: X has the higher absolute rate (6 versus 4 square centimetres), but Y has the far higher relative rate (100 percent versus 30 percent).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The classic trap sets equal absolute increases and asks which leaf "grew more". If the options reward the bigger leaf, they are wrong unless the question said absolute. Read the last three words of the question before computing anything.

Differentiation, Dedifferentiation, Redifferentiation
• DEFINITION — Differentiation is the process by which cells derived from the root or shoot apical meristem, or from the cambium, mature to perform specific functions. Differentiating cells undergo major structural changes in their cell walls and protoplasm and normally lose the capacity to divide.
• EXAMPLE — A tracheary element of the xylem is differentiation in one cell: as it matures it loses its protoplasm entirely and develops a strong, elastic, lignocellulosic secondary wall — it must die and hollow itself out to become a good water pipe.
• DEFINITION — Dedifferentiation is the phenomenon by which living differentiated cells, which had lost the capacity to divide, regain that capacity under certain conditions and start dividing again.
✔ KEY POINT — The two NCERT anchor examples of dedifferentiation: the interfascicular cambium and the cork cambium are both formed from fully differentiated parenchyma cells. Whenever a question mentions a meristem arising from mature parenchyma, the process being tested is dedifferentiation.
• EXAMPLE — In tissue culture, a small piece of mature plant tissue — an explant of differentiated cells — is coaxed into dividing to form an undifferentiated mass of cells called callus. That is dedifferentiation performed in a dish, and plant breeders use it as the starting point for regenerating whole plants.
• DEFINITION — Redifferentiation completes the cycle: the cells produced by a dedifferentiated tissue divide and then once again lose the capacity to divide in order to perform specific functions. Secondary xylem, secondary phloem and cork cells are redifferentiated products of cambium.
• WHY plants can do this and animals mostly cannot — plant cells retain a remarkable developmental flexibility because the plant body plan is modular and meristem-driven. A parenchyma cell carries the whole genome and, given the right signals, can restart the division programme its ancestors switched off.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sort any example with one question — which direction did the capacity to divide move? Dividing cell becomes specialised non-dividing cell: differentiation. Specialised cell starts dividing again: dedifferentiation. Daughter of that revived division specialises again: redifferentiation. Direction of travel is the whole key.
• YOUR TURN — During secondary growth, parenchyma cells between the vascular bundles become a meristem and later produce secondary xylem. Name both processes in order. Answer: dedifferentiation first (parenchyma to interfascicular cambium), then redifferentiation (cambium products maturing into secondary xylem).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Cork cambium and interfascicular cambium are dedifferentiation, NOT fresh primary meristems — they arise in a mature organ from cells that had already specialised. Options calling them "primary meristems" confuse their origin; they are secondary (lateral) meristems born by dedifferentiation.

Development and Plasticity — One Genome, Many Shapes
• DEFINITION — Development is the sum of growth and differentiation — it includes all the changes that an organism goes through during its life cycle, from the germination of a seed to senescence. Where growth says "bigger" and differentiation says "specialised", development is the whole biography.
• DEFINITION — Plasticity is the capacity of plants to follow different pathways of development in response to the environment or to different phases of life, forming different kinds of structures along the way. A plant cannot walk away from its surroundings, so it remodels itself instead.
• EXAMPLE — Heterophylly — leaves of different shapes on the same genetic plant — is plasticity's showcase. In cotton, coriander and larkspur, the leaves of the juvenile plant differ in shape from those of the mature plant: same genome, different life phase, different leaf.
• EXAMPLE — The buttercup (Ranunculus) shows environmental heterophylly: leaves produced submerged in water are dissected and ribbon-like to flow with the current, while leaves produced in air are broad and entire for catching light. One plant, two leaf blueprints, chosen by address.
✔ KEY POINT — The two heterophylly triggers must not be swapped in the exam: cotton, coriander and larkspur differ by phase of life (juvenile versus mature); buttercup differs by environment (in water versus in air). Examiners cross the pairs deliberately.
• WHY plasticity is a survival strategy — a sessile organism experiences drought, shade, flooding and grazing in place. Rebuilding organs to match conditions — thinner leaves in water, tougher leaves in wind — converts a fixed body plan into a flexible one, which is why plasticity, not movement, is the plant kingdom's answer to change.
• WORKED PATTERN — When a question shows two structures from one plant and asks why they differ, run the shortlist: different life phase points to developmental plasticity (heterophylly of cotton type); different environment points to environmental plasticity (buttercup type); a structure built by a different meristem entirely is not plasticity at all — it is normal development.
• YOUR TURN — A larkspur seedling's leaves are slender and lobed, while the same plant at maturity bears broad, less-divided leaves. Which phenomenon and which trigger? Answer: heterophylly, triggered by the phase of life — juvenile versus mature.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Plasticity does not change the genome — the DNA is identical in both leaf forms. Options describing heterophylly as mutation or genetic variation are wrong; it is differential expression of the same genes, switched by phase or environment.

Plant Growth Regulators — Five Chemical Messengers
• DEFINITION — Plant growth regulators (PGRs), also loosely called plant hormones or phytohormones, are small, simple molecules of diverse chemical composition that one part of a plant uses to instruct distant parts — when to divide, when to elongate, when to flower, when to drop a leaf and when to sleep.
• DEFINITION — Know them by their chemistry, because chemistry questions are pure recall: auxins are indole compounds (indole-3-acetic acid is the type), cytokinins are adenine derivatives (kinetin is a modified purine), gibberellins are terpenes and all acidic, abscisic acid is a derivative of carotenoids, and ethylene is a gas.
✔ KEY POINT — The functional split NCERT uses: one group of PGRs promotes growth — auxins, gibberellins and cytokinins, players in cell division, cell enlargement, pattern formation, flowering, fruiting and seed formation. The second group plays roles in dormancy, abscission and responses to wounding and stresses of biotic and abiotic origin — abscisic acid is its clear member, and ethylene mostly belongs here too, though it also promotes some activities.
• EXAMPLE — Every group was discovered by accident: a bending coleoptile, a foolish rice seedling, a callus that refused to divide without coconut milk, an overripe orange in a godown, and three laboratories independently reporting the same inhibitor molecule.
• WHY plants use travelling chemicals at all — a plant has no nerves and no muscles, yet it must coordinate a root tip sitting in wet soil with a shoot tip reaching for light. Slow, stable, diffusible signals that move from cell to cell and through the vascular tissue are its messaging system — and different tissues answer the same signal differently, which is why a single hormone can hold many jobs in one plant.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any PGR question, first place the hormone in its group (promoter trio, or ABA/ethylene side), then ask what tissue and what stage. Promoter questions run on division, elongation, flowering and fruiting; inhibitor questions run on dormancy, abscission, stomatal closure and stress. The group placement alone eliminates two options in most questions.
• YOUR TURN — A warehouse of unripe bananas ripens suspiciously fast after one crate of ripe oranges is stored beside it. Which PGR group is at work, and which side of the promoter-inhibitor split does it sit on? Answer: ethylene, a gas released by the ripening oranges — it mostly belongs to the inhibitor-stress side, but fruit ripening is one of its promoting activities.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not file ethylene simply as "the ripening promoter" or ABA as "the abscission hormone". NCERT's own caution: ethylene could fit either group but is largely an inhibitor of growth activities, and ABA's headline jobs are dormancy, stomatal closure and stress tolerance — the names abscisin and dormin are history, not the full job list.

Auxins — The Shoot Tip's Growth Orders
• DEFINITION — Auxin (from the Greek auxein, to grow) was the first PGR discovered. Charles Darwin and his son Francis Darwin observed that coleoptiles of canary grass bend towards unilateral light; the tip of the coleoptile was found to be the site of a transmittable influence that causes the bending of the whole coleoptile.
• DEFINITION — F. W. Went isolated auxin from the tips of coleoptiles of oat (Avena) seedlings — the work behind the Avena curvature test, still the classic auxin bioassay. The natural auxin is indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), produced mainly by the growing apices of stems and roots and transported to the regions of its action.
• EXAMPLE — Synthetic auxins are farm tools: NAA (naphthalene acetic acid) and 2,4-D (2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid) are made in factories, sprayed on fields, and behave like IAA in the plant — which is exactly what makes one of them a weapon, as the weed story below shows.
✔ KEY POINT — Apical dominance: in most higher plants the growing apical bud inhibits the growth of the lateral (axillary) buds. Auxin flowing down from the tip enforces this. Remove the shoot tip (decapitation) and the lateral buds are released and grow out — a fact applied daily in tea plantations and in hedge-making, where repeated trimming keeps the plant bushy.
• EXAMPLE — Auxins initiate rooting in stem cuttings — dip the base of a cutting in auxin and roots emerge far more reliably. They also promote flowering, for example in pineapple, prevent fruit and leaf drop at early stages while promoting the abscission of older mature leaves and fruits, and induce parthenocarpy — seedless fruit set — as in tomato.
✔ KEY POINT — 2,4-D is widely used as a herbicide: it kills dicotyledonous weeds but does not affect mature monocotyledonous plants, so a cereal field or a lawn (monocots) can be sprayed to remove broad-leaved dicot weeds. Gardeners use it to prepare weed-free lawns. In the exam, "selective weed killer" and "2,4-D" are the same answer.
• WHY one hormone can both prevent and promote leaf drop — auxin's effect depends on the age and state of the target tissue, not on the molecule changing its mind. Young leaves and fruits are held; old, mature ones are shed. Dose and developmental stage write the outcome — a principle that recurs with every PGR in this chapter.
• WORKED PATTERN — Map the auxin application to its headline: bushy hedges and tea bushes mean apical dominance broken by decapitation; rooted cuttings mean root initiation; pineapple flowering means auxin promotion of flowering; seedless tomatoes mean parthenocarpy; weed-free wheat fields mean 2,4-D. Five applications, five fixed addresses.
• YOUR TURN — A gardener prunes the tips of a hedge every month and the hedge stays dense and bushy instead of growing tall and sparse. Which phenomenon is being exploited? Answer: apical dominance — removing the auxin source at the tip releases the lateral buds, which grow out and fill the hedge.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The auxin discovery chain has three names and students shuffle them: Darwin and son showed the tip senses light and sends an influence (canary grass); Went isolated the substance (oat coleoptile). Kurosawa belongs to gibberellins, Skoog to cytokinins, Cousins to ethylene — keep each scientist in his own section.

Gibberellins — The Foolish-Seedling Hormone
• DEFINITION — Gibberellins entered science through a disease. E. Kurosawa, in 1926, reported the bakanae — foolish seedling — disease of rice, caused by the fungal pathogen Gibberella fujikuroi: infected seedlings grew absurdly tall, pale and weak, and the fungus was found to be secreting the active substance, gibberellic acid.
• DEFINITION — More than 100 gibberellins have been reported from fungi and higher plants, denoted GA1, GA2, GA3 and so on. GA3 was one of the first to be discovered and remains the most intensively studied form. All gibberellins are acidic in nature — hence "gibberellic acid".
✔ KEY POINT — Gibberellins are the elongation specialists: they increase the length of grape stalks, elongate apples and improve their shape, delay senescence so that fruits can be left on the tree longer to extend the market period, and speed up the malting process in the brewing industry.
• EXAMPLE — Spraying sugarcane with gibberellins increases the length of the stem and lifts sugar yield by as much as 20 tonnes per acre. Spraying juvenile conifers with GA induces early maturity and therefore early seed production — a gift to tree breeders who otherwise wait years for cones.
• DEFINITION — Bolting is the sudden elongation of internodes just prior to flowering in rosette plants — beet, cabbage and many others that normally hug the ground in a flat rosette. Gibberellins induce bolting: the squat rosette shoots up a flowering stalk when GA arrives.
• WHY a fungus makes a plant hormone — Gibberella fujikuroi is not being generous: by forcing the rice plant into spindly, weakened growth, the fungus engineers a host that is easier to colonise and quicker to collapse. The plant's own GA pathway exists for legitimate stem elongation and seed germination; the fungus merely shouts into a channel the plant already listens to.
• WORKED PATTERN — Gibberellin questions wear four costumes: foolish seedlings or bakanae (discovery), bunches and stalks (grape stalk length, sugarcane yield), fruit shape and market life (apples, delayed senescence), and brewing or bolting (malting speed, rosette plants flowering). Any costume, same hormone.
• YOUR TURN — A cabbage grower wants his rosette cabbages to bolt and flower for seed production this season. Which PGR should be applied? Answer: gibberellin — it induces bolting, the pre-flowering internode elongation of rosette plants.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Delay of senescence" is claimed by both gibberellins and cytokinins, but in different theatres: GA delays fruit senescence so fruit stays longer on the tree; cytokinins delay leaf senescence by promoting nutrient mobilisation. When an option says "fruit left on tree", choose GA; "leaves stay green", choose cytokinin.

Cytokinins — Partners in Cell Division
• DEFINITION — Cytokinins are the cell-division hormones. F. Skoog and his co-workers observed that callus from the internodal segments of tobacco stems proliferated only when the nutrient medium contained auxin plus one of the following: extracts of vascular tissues, yeast extract, coconut milk or DNA.
• DEFINITION — Miller and co-workers crystallised the cytokinesis-promoting substance and named it kinetin — chemically a modified form of adenine, the purine N6-furfurylamino purine. Kinetin does not occur naturally in plants; the natural cytokinin zeatin was later isolated from corn kernels and coconut milk.
✔ KEY POINT — Natural cytokinins are synthesised in regions where rapid cell division occurs — root apices, developing shoot buds and young fruits. A cytokinin map of the plant is simply a map of its construction sites.
• EXAMPLE — Coconut milk's fame in tissue culture begins here: it was one of the additives that made tobacco callus divide, because it carries cytokinin activity. Modern micropropagation media still pair an auxin with a cytokinin, adjusting the ratio to order roots or shoots from a callus.
• DEFINITION — Cytokinin headline functions: they promote cell division (cytokinesis), help to produce new leaves and chloroplasts in leaves, drive lateral shoot growth and adventitious shoot formation, and help overcome apical dominance — the direct counterweight to auxin in the branching decision.
✔ KEY POINT — Cytokinins promote nutrient mobilisation, and nutrient mobilisation delays the senescence of leaves: a leaf kept supplied stays green and working. This is the leaf-side answer to the senescence trap — GA keeps fruit on trees, cytokinin keeps leaves alive.
• WHY two hormones fight over one bud — apical dominance is a negotiation, not a dictatorship. Auxin from the tip says "wait"; cytokinin, arriving largely from the roots, says "grow". The bud reads the balance. That is why decapitation (removing auxin) and cytokinin application produce the same bushy result by opposite moves.
• WORKED PATTERN — Division questions point to cytokinin when the stem includes: callus proliferation, coconut milk, kinetin or zeatin, lateral buds growing despite an intact apex, or leaves refusing to yellow. Any one of these is the cytokinin signature; combined with "modified adenine", the answer is certain.
• YOUR TURN — Detached leaves treated with cytokinin stay green days longer than untreated leaves, while the untreated ones yellow. Which cytokinin function explains it? Answer: nutrient mobilisation — the treated leaf keeps importing nutrients, so its senescence is delayed.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Kinetin is a modified adenine and is NOT a natural plant product — it was crystallised from herring DNA preparations in the laboratory; zeatin is the natural one. Options that call kinetin "isolated from coconut milk" have swapped the two: coconut milk is the source associated with zeatin and with Skoog's original callus observation.

Ethylene — The Gaseous Ripening Signal
• DEFINITION — Ethylene is a simple gaseous PGR — the only hormone in this chapter that travels as a gas, which is why it can act between fruits in a closed store as easily as within one plant. It is synthesised in large amounts by tissues undergoing senescence and by ripening fruits.
• DEFINITION — H. H. Cousins confirmed that a volatile substance released from ripened oranges hastened the ripening of stored, unripened bananas nearby — the first hard evidence that a gas could be a plant hormone, and the reason one rotten apple really does spoil the barrel.
✔ KEY POINT — The seedling effects of ethylene are a named trio, often asked together: it influences horizontal growth of seedlings, causes swelling of the axis, and produces apical hook formation in dicot seedlings. It also promotes senescence and abscission of plant organs, especially leaves and flowers.
• EXAMPLE — Ethylene breaks seed and bud dormancy: it initiates germination in peanut seeds and sprouting of potato tubers. In deep-water rice it does the opposite of dwarfing — it promotes rapid internode and petiole elongation so that the leaves and upper parts of the shoot stay above the rising water.
• DEFINITION — During fruit ripening, ethylene enhances the rate of respiration — the rise is called the respiratory climactic. It is also an effective flower and fruit thinner: it accelerates abscission in flowers and fruits, a property used for thinning cotton, cherry and walnut crops.
✔ KEY POINT — Ethephon is the most widely used ethylene source in agriculture: supplied in aqueous solution, it is readily absorbed and transported within the plant and releases ethylene slowly. It hastens fruit ripening in tomatoes and apples and accelerates abscission in flowers and fruits (as in cotton).
• EXAMPLE — Ethylene also initiates flowering and synchronises fruit-set in pineapple, and induces flowering in mango — two promoter-style jobs that sit oddly beside its inhibitor reputation, and are therefore favourite trap material. It additionally promotes root growth and root hair formation, increasing the absorption surface of the root.
• WHY a gas is the right design for ripening — ripening must be synchronised: a fruit ripening alone, seed by seed, attracts seed dispersers poorly. A volatile signal coordinates the whole cluster — and leaks to neighbours, which fruit sellers have exploited, knowingly or not, for centuries.
• WORKED PATTERN — Read the organ and the event: fruit plus ripening means ethylene (or its stand-in ethephon); seed or bud plus dormancy breaking means ethylene or GA (check whether the stem mentions a gas or a climactic rise — gas means ethylene); flooded rice elongating means ethylene; thinning of cotton or cherry means ethylene-driven abscission.
• YOUR TURN — A tomato grower wants a whole consignment to ripen uniformly just before market day. Name the compound to apply and the hormone it releases. Answer: ethephon — absorbed by the fruit and slowly releases ethylene, the ripening signal.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Ethylene breaks the dormancy of seeds and buds, while abscisic acid imposes dormancy — the two are opponents, and options love reversing them. Also keep the sites straight: ethephon is not itself the hormone; it is a transportable compound that releases ethylene inside the plant.

Abscisic Acid — The Stress Hormone
• DEFINITION — Abscisic acid (ABA) is the chapter's general growth inhibitor — a derivative of carotenoids that acts as the plant's brake pedal and emergency signal. It was reported in the mid-1960s by three independent groups under three names — inhibitor-B, abscisin II and dormin — before the three were found to be chemically identical.
• DEFINITION — ABA stimulates the closure of stomata and thereby increases the tolerance of plants to various kinds of stress. That is the basis of its everyday title: ABA is called the stress hormone, because it is the signal that makes a thirsty plant shut its pores and ride out the drought.
✔ KEY POINT — ABA inhibits seed germination and is a central player in seed development, maturation and dormancy: by inducing dormancy it helps seeds withstand desiccation and other unfavourable factors, holding them asleep until conditions improve.
• EXAMPLE — The seed story in one line: during maturation ABA rises, the embryo stops growing, reserves are packed, water is expelled, and the seed enters dormancy dry and patient. Later ABA falls and gibberellin rises — and the same seed germinates. The ABA-to-GA balance is the seed's sleep-wake switch.
• DEFINITION — In most situations ABA acts as an antagonist to the gibberellins: where GA says elongate, germinate, bolt, ABA says stop, close, wait. Many exam questions are simply this tug-of-war staged in different organs.
• WHY a hormone of stopping is indispensable — growth at the wrong moment is fatal. A seed germinating in a dry week, or stomata staying open through a drought afternoon, wastes the plant's one life. ABA exists because survival often means refusing to grow — development includes the wisdom to pause.
• WORKED PATTERN — ABA questions announce themselves with stress vocabulary: drought, stomatal closure, desiccation, dormancy, inhibited germination, "withstand unfavourable conditions". If the stem instead asks what breaks dormancy or closes the ABA chapter, the answer swings to GA or ethylene — dormancy is imposed by ABA and released against it.
• YOUR TURN — On a hot, dry afternoon a plant closes its stomata within minutes, long before its leaves wilt. Which PGR is the most likely messenger? Answer: abscisic acid — stomatal closure under stress is its signature action.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — ABA's historical names (abscisin, dormin) describe its discovery, not its limits — and its biggest modern headline is stomatal closure, not abscission: in NCERT's treatment, abscission of flowers and fruits is driven mainly by ethylene, while ABA's named roles are dormancy, germination inhibition and stress tolerance. Do not let the name "abscisin" mark the wrong answer.

Photoperiodism — Flowering Timed by the Night
• DEFINITION — Photoperiodism is the response of plants to the relative durations of light and dark — the photoperiod. Some plants require a periodic exposure to light to induce flowering, and it was Garner and Allard who first worked out that the duration of the light period sorts plants into flowering classes.
• DEFINITION — The three classes are defined against a critical duration (critical photoperiod): short-day plants (SDP) flower when the light period is less than the critical duration; long-day plants (LDP) flower when the light period exceeds the critical duration; and day-neutral plants (DNP) show no correlation between light duration and flowering.
• EXAMPLE — Classic addresses: soybean and chrysanthemum are short-day plants; spinach and lettuce are long-day plants; tomato and cucumber are day-neutral. When a question names one of these, it has quietly told you the class — the rest is reasoning about nights.
✔ KEY POINT — The decisive variable is the length of the dark period, not the day. A short-day plant is really a long-night plant: it flowers only when it receives a long, uninterrupted night. Interrupt that night with even a brief flash of light and the plant reads the night as broken — and refuses to flower.
• DEFINITION — The night-break experiment is the proof: give a short-day plant a long night but break it in the middle with a short light exposure, and flowering is prevented. For a long-day plant the same interruption of a long night is favourable — it effectively shortens the night, and the plant flowers as if the days were long.
• EXAMPLE — Commercial growers exploit this ruthlessly: chrysanthemum, a short-day plant, is kept vegetative under artificially lengthened days and then flowered on schedule by covering the crop to enforce long nights — which is why chrysanthemums can be sold in flower in any month of the year.
• DEFINITION — The photoperiodic stimulus is perceived by the leaves — mature leaves, not the shoot tip that will flower. It is hypothesised that a flowering hormone, florigen, is produced in the stimulated leaves and translocated to the shoot apices, where it causes floral initiation.
• WHY flowering must wait for a calendar — flowers and fruits are expensive, and frost or drought can destroy a whole reproductive season. A plant that measures night length owns a reliable calendar, since nights lengthen predictably with season — so it flowers when pollinators are active and seeds will have time to ripen.
• WORKED PATTERN — Attack every photoperiodism question with the night, not the day: restate the regime as a night length, ask whether the night is longer or shorter than the critical night, and ask whether it is interrupted. Uninterrupted long night: SDP flowers, LDP waits. Night broken by a flash: SDP is blocked, LDP is satisfied.
• YOUR TURN — A short-day plant is grown under 10 hours of light and 14 hours of darkness, but a 10-minute light flash is given at midnight. Will it flower? Answer: no — the flash splits the long night into two short ones, and a short-day plant needs one uninterrupted long night.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Short-day plant" does not mean it flowers in short days because the day matters — it flowers because the night is long. Questions that keep the same day length but interrupt the night are testing exactly this. Also, the stimulus is perceived in the leaves: covering only the shoot tip changes nothing.

Phytochrome — The Red and Far-Red Switch
• DEFINITION — Phytochrome is the light-sensitive pigment protein through which the plant measures day and night. It exists in two interconvertible forms: Pr, which absorbs red light, and Pfr, which absorbs far-red light. The two forms are a molecular switch flipped by the colour of the last light the leaf received.
✔ KEY POINT — The wavelength pair is fixed exam data: Pr absorbs red light of about 660 nm and converts into Pfr; Pfr absorbs far-red light of about 730 nm and converts back into Pr. Red builds the active form; far-red dismantles it.
• DEFINITION — Pfr is the physiologically active form. In daylight, red light dominates, so phytochrome accumulates as Pfr. In darkness Pfr slowly reverts to Pr (and is broken down), so by dawn after a long night very little Pfr remains — the plant can literally measure how long the night was by how much Pfr is left.
• EXAMPLE — The red/far-red reversibility is the classic demonstration: a brief flash of red light during the night acts exactly like a night break, but if the red flash is immediately followed by far-red light, the effect is cancelled — the last flash wins, because it sets the final state of the switch.
✔ KEY POINT — Pfr's verdict on flowering depends on the plant's class: in short-day plants a high Pfr level inhibits flowering, so SDP flower only after a long night has drained Pfr away; in long-day plants Pfr promotes flowering, so LDP flower when short nights leave Pfr high. Same molecule, opposite instructions — the class of the plant writes the meaning.
• EXAMPLE — Phytochrome also guards the nursery: light-sensitive seeds such as lettuce germinate when red light converts Pr to Pfr, while far-red light can hold them dormant — one reason seeds buried deep, where only far-red-rich filtered light reaches, often refuse to germinate until the soil is disturbed.
• WHY a reversible switch beats a simple light meter — measuring night length requires a signal that rises in light and falls in darkness at a steady, predictable rate. Pfr is exactly that: charged by day, discharging through the night. Its level at dawn is a readout of night length with no moving parts.
• WORKED PATTERN — In any phytochrome question, track the switch state at the decision moment: last light red or daylight means Pfr high; far-red or a long dark period means Pfr low. Then apply the class rule: SDP flowers when Pfr is low; LDP flowers when Pfr is high. Two steps, no memorised tables needed.
• YOUR TURN — At midnight a short-day plant receives a flash of red light, immediately followed by a flash of far-red light. Predict its flowering. Answer: it will flower — the far-red flash converts Pfr back to Pr, cancelling the night break, so the long night is effectively uninterrupted.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not swap the numbers: 660 nm is red and builds Pfr; 730 nm is far-red and removes it. A second classic swap: Pfr is the active form but "active" does not mean "always promotes flowering" — in short-day plants the active form is precisely the inhibitor.

Vernalisation — Flowering Only After Winter
• DEFINITION — Vernalisation is the phenomenon by which flowering in certain plants is promoted by a preceding period of low temperature. Many plants will not flower at all until they have experienced a spell of cold; the cold is not a nutrient or a luxury — it is a requirement the plant counts.
• DEFINITION — Plants sort into spring and winter varieties on this basis. The spring variety is planted in spring and flowers the same season without any cold treatment. The winter variety must be planted in autumn so that it experiences winter cold and flowers in the following season; planted in spring, deprived of its cold spell, it fails to flower and remains vegetative.
• EXAMPLE — Wheat, barley and rye come in both varieties, and the farmer's sowing calendar is written by vernalisation: sow winter wheat in spring and you harvest leaves, not grain. The plant is healthy — it is simply still waiting for a winter that never came.
• DEFINITION — Many biennials — plants that normally flower and die in their second season, such as sugar beet, cabbage and carrot — can be made to behave by the same logic: subjecting the young plant to low temperature stimulates its subsequent flowering response, synchronising seed production on the grower's schedule.
✔ KEY POINT — Vernalisation prevents precocious reproductive development late in the growing season: it stops a plant from rushing into flowering when there is no time left to set mature seed. The cold requirement guarantees the plant has sufficient time to reach maturity before it reproduces.
• WHY a plant would demand winter before flowering — flowering is a one-way commitment of the whole meristem. A winter annual that flowered in its first autumn would set seed into frost. Requiring the cold first converts temperature into information: winter has passed, spring is real, and it is now safe to reproduce.
• WORKED PATTERN — Vernalisation questions always stage the same play: a plant, a cold treatment given or withheld, and a flowering outcome. If cold was withheld from a winter variety or a biennial, the answer is "remains vegetative, does not flower". If cold was supplied artificially (chilled seedlings or seeds), the answer is early or induced flowering — the treatment substitutes for winter.
• YOUR TURN — Sugar beet, a biennial, is grown for its root in year one and normally flowers in year two after winter cold. A seed producer wants flowers in the very first season. What treatment achieves it? Answer: expose the young plants to a period of low temperature — artificial vernalisation — which stimulates the flowering response ahead of schedule.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Vernalisation is about low temperature, photoperiodism about light duration — options cross them constantly ("flowering induced by long days after cold" mixes two separate cues). Also, the stimulus of vernalisation is cold experienced by the growing plant or germinating seed, not cold applied to dry stored seed in a packet.

Seed Dormancy — Sleeping on Purpose
• DEFINITION — Seed dormancy is the state in which a viable seed fails to germinate even when conditions — water, oxygen, suitable temperature — appear favourable. Dormancy is not death and not failure: it is a controlled refusal, imposed by the seed's own blocks.
• DEFINITION — NCERT lists the blocks plainly: seeds may carry impermeable and hard seed coats that shut out water and oxygen; embryos may be immature and need time to finish developing; and chemical inhibitors — abscisic acid above all, along with phenolic acids — can be present inside the seed, vetoing germination.
✔ KEY POINT — Abscisic acid is the dormancy hormone of this chapter: it induces and maintains dormancy during seed maturation, helps the seed withstand desiccation, and must fall before germination can proceed. Gibberellin is its opposite number — applying GA is one of the standard ways to break dormancy.
• EXAMPLE — Nature breaks dormancy with a toolkit the exam expects you to know: the chilling of a cold winter (moist chilling, or stratification) releases many temperate seeds; light triggers light-sensitive seeds; and passage through an animal's digestive tract weakens stubborn coats while carrying the seed far from its parent.
• DEFINITION — Artificial methods mirror the natural ones: scarification — mechanical abrasion or weakening of the seed coat — lets water in; chilling treatments substitute for winter; and chemical applications, including gibberellic acid and nitrates, can substitute for the release signals the seed is waiting for.
• WHY dormancy is worth marks and worth having — a seed that germinates at the first warm rain of a false spring dies in the frost that follows. Dormancy staggers germination across time and, with coat toughness, across the journeys of animals — spreading one plant's offspring across seasons and landscapes instead of betting everything on one week.
• WORKED PATTERN — Dormancy questions come in two shapes: "why did these seeds not germinate despite favourable conditions?" — answer with a block (hard coat, immature embryo, inhibitors like ABA); and "how can this dormancy be broken?" — answer with the matching key (scarify the coat, chill the seed, apply GA). Block first, then the key that fits that block.
• YOUR TURN — Seeds with a rock-hard, water-proof coat are soaked for a week and still do not germinate, though the embryos inside are alive. What single treatment most directly addresses the problem? Answer: scarification — mechanically abrading or weakening the seed coat so water and oxygen can enter.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Dormancy is not imposed by "unfavourable conditions" alone — NCERT's framing is that seeds fail to germinate even under favourable conditions because of internal blocks. And do not hand dormancy-breaking to ABA: ABA maintains the sleep; gibberellin and ethylene are the wake-up signals.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Growth is an irreversible permanent increase in size; plants grow throughout life because meristems persist — apical meristems give primary growth (length), lateral meristems (vascular and cork cambium) give secondary growth (girth)
✔ KEY POINT — Arithmetic growth: one daughter cell keeps dividing, the other matures — linear curve, Lt = L0 + rt; a root elongating at a constant rate is the example
✔ KEY POINT — Geometric growth: both daughters keep dividing — exponential curve, W1 = W0 e raised to rt, where r is the relative growth rate or efficiency index; the zygote-to-embryo divisions are the example
✔ KEY POINT — The sigmoid (S-shaped) growth curve has lag, log (exponential) and stationary phases — it is geometric growth limited by resources, not a separate division type
✔ KEY POINT — Absolute growth rate is total growth per unit time; relative growth rate is growth per unit initial size — NCERT leaves: both gain 5 square centimetres, but the small leaf's relative rate is 100 percent against the big leaf's 10 percent
✔ KEY POINT — Differentiation makes dividing cells mature for a job; dedifferentiation lets mature cells divide again (interfascicular cambium, cork cambium, callus); redifferentiation matures the products of a dedifferentiated tissue (secondary xylem and phloem, cork cells)
✔ KEY POINT — Development equals growth plus differentiation; plasticity gives different structures from one genome — heterophylly by life phase in cotton, coriander and larkspur, and by environment in buttercup (air versus water leaves)
✔ KEY POINT — The promoter trio is auxins, gibberellins and cytokinins; abscisic acid is the inhibitor and stress hormone; ethylene is a gas that mostly inhibits but also ripens fruit and breaks dormancy
✔ KEY POINT — Auxins (Went; IAA): apical dominance, rooting of cuttings, pineapple flowering, parthenocarpy in tomato; synthetic NAA and 2,4-D, the selective herbicide that kills dicot weeds and spares monocots
✔ KEY POINT — Gibberellins (Kurosawa, bakanae of rice; GA3): stem elongation, grape stalk length, apple shape, delayed fruit senescence, faster malting, bolting in rosette plants, sugarcane yield up to 20 tonnes per acre
✔ KEY POINT — Cytokinins (Skoog and Miller; kinetin is a modified adenine, zeatin the natural form): cell division, made in root apices and young fruits, overcome apical dominance, nutrient mobilisation delays leaf senescence
✔ KEY POINT — Ethylene (Cousins): a gas from ripening fruit; apical hook and axis swelling in seedlings, respiratory climactic in ripening, breaks seed and bud dormancy, deep-water rice elongation; ethephon is its agricultural source
✔ KEY POINT — Abscisic acid: carotenoid derivative, reported as inhibitor-B, abscisin II and dormin; closes stomata, induces and maintains seed dormancy, inhibits germination, and acts antagonistic to gibberellins
✔ KEY POINT — Photoperiodism (Garner and Allard): SDP flower below the critical light period, LDP above it, DNP regardless — but the real signal is the length of the uninterrupted night; the stimulus is perceived by mature leaves
✔ KEY POINT — Phytochrome: Pr absorbs red light (660 nm) to become active Pfr; Pfr absorbs far-red (730 nm) and reverts; Pfr inhibits flowering in short-day plants and promotes it in long-day plants; a long night drains Pfr
✔ KEY POINT — Vernalisation: a low-temperature requirement for flowering — winter wheat sown in spring stays vegetative; spring varieties need no cold; biennials like sugar beet, cabbage and carrot flower after winter cold
✔ KEY POINT — Seed dormancy is imposed by hard impermeable coats, immature embryos and inhibitors (ABA, phenolic acids); it is broken by scarification, chilling (stratification), light, and application of gibberellic acid or nitrates

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Calling the sigmoid curve a third type of growth division. Why it is wrong: division behaviour is only arithmetic or geometric; the sigmoid is geometric growth drawn against time when resources become limiting. Correct principle: lag-log-stationary describes a curve, not a cell lineage.
• Mistake 2: Saying both daughter cells stop dividing in arithmetic growth. Why it is wrong: one daughter continues to divide while the other differentiates — that steady single line of division is exactly what produces a constant rate. Correct principle: arithmetic means one divides, one matures; geometric means both divide.
• Mistake 3: Awarding "more growth" to whichever leaf ends bigger in the NCERT leaf comparison. Why it is wrong: both leaves gained the same absolute amount (5 square centimetres); the small leaf's relative rate was ten times higher. Correct principle: match the computation to the term used — absolute is raw gain, relative is gain per unit initial size.
• Mistake 4: Giving interfascicular cambium as an example of differentiation. Why it is wrong: it forms when already-differentiated parenchyma cells regain division capacity — the reverse direction. Correct principle: division regained is dedifferentiation; secondary xylem formed afterwards is redifferentiation.
• Mistake 5: Assigning Went to gibberellins or Kurosawa to auxins. Why it is wrong: Went isolated auxin from oat coleoptile tips; Kurosawa reported bakanae disease and led to gibberellins; Skoog and Miller belong to cytokinins; Cousins to ethylene. Correct principle: scientist and hormone are a fixed pair — learn them as one unit.
• Mistake 6: Calling kinetin a natural cytokinin from coconut milk. Why it is wrong: kinetin is a synthetic, modified adenine crystallised in the laboratory; the natural cytokinin is zeatin, isolated from corn kernels and coconut milk. Correct principle: kinetin equals laboratory adenine derivative; zeatin equals natural.
• Mistake 7: Saying 2,4-D kills all weeds indiscriminately. Why it is wrong: 2,4-D is selective — it kills dicotyledonous weeds and does not affect mature monocotyledonous plants, which is why it can be used on lawns and cereal fields. Correct principle: selectivity is the entire point of 2,4-D.
• Mistake 8: Explaining short-day plants by day length. Why it is wrong: the plant measures the dark period; a brief light flash interrupting a long night blocks SDP flowering even though the total hours of light barely change. Correct principle: SDP means long uninterrupted night; judge every regime by its night.
• Mistake 9: Swapping the phytochrome wavelengths or the Pfr verdicts. Why it is wrong: Pr absorbs red (660 nm) to form Pfr; Pfr absorbs far-red (730 nm) to revert — and high Pfr inhibits SDP flowering while promoting LDP flowering. Correct principle: 660 builds, 730 removes; Pfr's meaning depends on the plant's class.
• Mistake 10: Making ABA the hormone that breaks dormancy. Why it is wrong: ABA induces and maintains dormancy and inhibits germination; germination is released when ABA falls and gibberellin (or ethylene) action dominates. Correct principle: ABA sleeps, GA wakes.
• Mistake 11: Crediting ethylene with closing stomata in drought. Why it is wrong: stomatal closure under stress is ABA's signature; ethylene's signatures are ripening, abscission, the seedling trio and dormancy breaking. Correct principle: drought and stomata mean ABA; gas and ripening mean ethylene.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A root tip is 2.0 cm long and elongates at a constant rate of 0.2 cm per day. Find its length after 15 days and name the growth type.
• Given: Constant rate of elongation; initial length 2.0 cm; time 15 days.
• Principle: A constant increment means arithmetic growth, modelled by Lt = L0 + rt.
• Steps: Lt = 2.0 + (0.2 x 15) = 2.0 + 3.0 = 5.0 cm. The graph of length against time would be a straight line.
• Answer: 5.0 cm; arithmetic growth.
• Reasoning: Only one daughter cell at the tip keeps dividing while the other matures, so the root adds the same length each day.
• Mistake to avoid: Reaching for the exponential formula — exponential growth needs a rate proportional to current size, and a constant daily increment denies that.
• Example 2 — Problem: Leaves A and B gain 5 square centimetres each in one day. A grows from 5 to 10 square centimetres, B from 50 to 55. Compare their absolute and relative growth rates.
• Given: Equal absolute gains of 5 square centimetres; initial areas 5 (A) and 50 (B) square centimetres.
• Principle: Absolute growth rate is the raw gain; relative growth rate is the gain divided by the initial size, expressed as a percentage.
• Steps: Absolute: both 5 square centimetres per day — equal. Relative: A = 5/5 x 100 = 100 percent; B = 5/50 x 100 = 10 percent.
• Answer: Absolute rates equal; relative rate of A (100 percent) is ten times that of B (10 percent).
• Reasoning: Relative rate corrects for starting size; the small leaf doubled itself while the big leaf barely noticed the same gain. Absolute size is not a rate.
• Mistake to avoid: Declaring B the faster grower because it is bigger — final size is not a rate.
• Example 3 — Problem: During secondary growth in a dicot stem, parenchyma cells lying between vascular bundles begin to divide and form a strip of cambium. Name the process, and name what happens when that cambium's products mature into secondary xylem.
• Given: Fully differentiated parenchyma resumes division to form interfascicular cambium; its derivatives then mature into secondary xylem.
• Principle: Regaining division capacity is dedifferentiation; the renewed specialisation of its products is redifferentiation.
• Steps: Parenchyma to cambium: division capacity regained — dedifferentiation. Cambium to secondary xylem: products specialise and stop dividing — redifferentiation.
• Answer: Dedifferentiation, followed by redifferentiation.
• Reasoning: NCERT's cambium examples exist precisely to test the direction of travel between dividing and mature states.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling the first step differentiation — differentiation moves away from division, not towards it.
• Example 4 — Problem: A tea estate keeps its bushes low and densely branched by regular pruning of shoot tips. Explain the hormonal basis and name the synthetic auxin a nurseryman might instead use to root the pruned cuttings.
• Given: Removal of shoot apices releases lateral buds; cuttings need root initiation.
• Principle: Apical dominance is maintained by auxin from the apical bud; decapitation removes the auxin source. Auxins also initiate rooting in stem cuttings — NAA is the synthetic auxin used for this.
• Steps: Pruning removes the tip and its auxin; freed from inhibition, lateral buds grow out, making the bush dense. For the cuttings, auxin (NAA) applied at the base initiates adventitious roots.
• Answer: Apical dominance is being broken by decapitation; NAA promotes rooting of the cuttings.
• Reasoning: One hormone explains both the bush shape and the nursery trick — the exam loves pairing them in one stem.
• Mistake to avoid: Crediting cytokinin for the pruning effect — cytokinins can overcome apical dominance chemically, but pruning works by removing the auxin source.
• Example 5 — Problem: Rice seedlings in one corner of a nursery are pale, weak and far taller than the rest, though all received the same care. A fungal infection is suspected. Name the disease, the fungus and the class of compound responsible.
• Given: Abnormally elongated, weak rice seedlings; fungal association.
• Principle: This is the historical bakanae (foolish seedling) presentation reported by Kurosawa, caused by Gibberella fujikuroi secreting gibberellic acid.
• Steps: Foolish elongation of rice equals bakanae; the pathogen is the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi; its secreted product belongs to the gibberellins.
• Answer: Bakanae disease; Gibberella fujikuroi; gibberellins (gibberellic acid).
• Reasoning: The discovery story is the identification — examiners describe the disease and expect the hormone.
• Mistake to avoid: Answering auxin because "it promotes growth" — auxin excess does not produce this classic foolish-seedling picture, and the fungus clue belongs to gibberellin history.
• Example 6 — Problem: A short-day plant will flower only if given 14-hour nights. In the experiment it receives 14-hour nights, but each night a one-minute flash of red light is given at the midpoint, followed immediately by a flash of far-red light. Predict flowering and justify it through phytochrome.
• Given: SDP with a critical requirement of long nights; night interrupted by red then far-red flashes.
• Principle: Red light converts Pr to Pfr, which would break the night for an SDP — but far-red immediately converts Pfr back to Pr, cancelling the break. The last flash determines the switch state.
• Steps: Red flash raises Pfr (night broken); far-red flash drops Pfr back (night restored). Net effect: the night is uninterrupted, Pfr stays low, and the SDP's flowering block is lifted.
• Answer: The plant flowers; the far-red flash reverses the red flash, so the long night counts as continuous.
• Reasoning: Photoreversibility is the signature experiment of phytochrome — whoever controls the last flash controls the outcome.
• Mistake to avoid: Ignoring the far-red flash and declaring "night broken, no flowering" — the sequence matters, not just the presence of a flash.
• Example 7 — Problem: A farmer sowed winter wheat in April, in spring. The crop grew lush and green all season but produced no ears. His neighbour's spring wheat, sown the same week, yielded normally. Explain the failure and state how the winter variety could still be brought to flower in such a schedule.
• Given: Winter variety deprived of low temperature; spring variety needs no cold.
• Principle: Winter wheat requires vernalisation — a period of low temperature — before it can flower; without it, the plant remains vegetative. Artificial chilling of the young plants can substitute for winter.
• Steps: The winter variety never experienced cold, so its flowering requirement was never met — it stayed vegetative. The spring variety has no cold requirement, so it flowered on schedule. Chilling the winter variety's germinating seeds or young plants would supply the missing vernalisation.
• Answer: The winter wheat failed for want of vernalisation; pre-chilling the young plants (artificial vernalisation) would let it flower.
• Reasoning: The green, healthy crop is the clue — nothing is wrong with nutrition or water; a specific environmental key was never turned.
• Mistake to avoid: Blaming photoperiod — day length was identical for both plots, and the spring variety flowered under it; the missing factor was cold, not light.

Practice Questions — Self-Test Set
• Q1. A root grows from 3 cm to 8 cm in 10 days, adding exactly 0.5 cm every day. Name the growth type and write its equation form.
• Q2. Which meristem is responsible for the increase in girth of a dicot stem, and is that primary or secondary growth?
• Q3. A callus is raised from a piece of mature leaf tissue in culture. Which process made the mature cells divide again?
• Q4. Name the PGR and the scientist: the hormone isolated from oat coleoptile tips, and the hormone whose story begins with foolish seedlings of rice.
• Q5. A lawn is sprayed to remove broad-leaved weeds while the grass survives untouched. Name the compound and explain the selectivity.
• Q6. A short-day plant under a long night fails to flower when the night is interrupted by a flash of light. Which pigment system mediates this, and in which form does the pigment sit after a long uninterrupted night?
• Q7. Why does winter wheat sown in spring fail to produce grain, and what is this phenomenon called?
• Q8. A batch of seeds refuses to germinate despite water, air and warmth. Give two internal causes from this chapter and one treatment that can release them.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. Arithmetic growth — the daily increment is constant. Its equation form is Lt = L0 + rt (here 3 + 0.5 x 10 = 8 cm). Misconception watch: constant addition is arithmetic even over many days; do not let the tidy final number seduce you into "geometric".
• A2. The vascular cambium (a lateral meristem, with the cork cambium) — this is secondary growth. Misconception watch: the apical meristems handle length (primary growth); girth questions always belong to lateral meristems.
• A3. Dedifferentiation — differentiated leaf cells regained the capacity to divide, producing the undifferentiated callus. Misconception watch: callus is not a tissue that was always meristematic; its cells had specialised first, which is the whole point.
• A4. Auxin — isolated by F. W. Went from oat coleoptile tips (Darwin and son had shown the tip's influence earlier, in canary grass). Gibberellin — the bakanae story reported by Kurosawa, from the fungus Gibberella fujikuroi. Misconception watch: the two discovery stories are the most swapped pair in the chapter; anchor oat to auxin and rice to gibberellin.
• A5. 2,4-D, a synthetic auxin used as a herbicide. It kills dicotyledonous weeds but does not affect mature monocotyledonous plants — and lawn grass is a monocot, so the weeds die and the lawn survives. Misconception watch: 2,4-D does not "know" weeds; its effect simply differs between dicots and mature monocots.
• A6. Phytochrome. After a long uninterrupted night, Pfr has reverted and been depleted, so the pigment sits overwhelmingly in the Pr form — the low-Pfr state that permits a short-day plant to flower. Misconception watch: the flash works by creating Pfr, not by "waking the plant"; and Pfr inhibits SDP flowering rather than promoting it.
• A7. Winter wheat requires a period of low temperature before it can flower; sown in spring it never experiences winter cold, so it remains vegetative and sets no grain. The phenomenon is vernalisation. Misconception watch: the crop is not diseased or underfed — a specific cold requirement went unmet, unlike the spring variety beside it, which has no such requirement.
• A8. Internal causes: an impermeable, hard seed coat blocking water and oxygen; immature embryos; or chemical inhibitors such as abscisic acid and phenolic acids — any two. A releasing treatment: scarification, moist chilling (stratification), or gibberellic acid. Misconception watch: dormancy persists even under favourable external conditions — that is its definition; more water does not fix an internal block.

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: Growth is irreversible increase, driven for life by meristems — apical for length, lateral for girth — on two division arithmetics: one daughter dividing (arithmetic, linear, Lt = L0 + rt) or both dividing (geometric, exponential), which limited resources bend into the sigmoid curve of lag, log and stationary phases.
• Summary: Cells travel between states — differentiation into jobs, dedifferentiation back into division (cambium from parenchyma, callus in culture), redifferentiation into new jobs — and development, the sum of growth and differentiation, stays plastic: one genome builds different leaves for different life phases (cotton, coriander, larkspur) and different waters (buttercup).
• Summary: Five PGRs steer everything: auxins (dominance, rooting, parthenocarpy, 2,4-D), gibberellins (bakanae to bolting and malting), cytokinins (division; zeatin natural, kinetin synthetic), ethylene the gas (ripening climactic, dormancy breaking, deep-water rice), and ABA (stress, stomatal closure, dormancy) — promoters and inhibitors constantly negotiating.
• Summary: The environment keeps three calendars: night length, read through phytochrome (Pr to Pfr by 660 nm red, back by 730 nm far-red), decides photoperiodic flowering; winter cold vernalises wheat and biennials; and dormancy — imposed by coats, immature embryos and ABA — lifts with scarification, chilling, light, GA and nitrates.
