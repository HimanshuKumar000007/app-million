Introduction — The Reaction That Feeds the World
• Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy, building glucose from carbon dioxide and water and releasing oxygen as a by-product. Nearly every living thing on Earth eats either a photosynthesiser or something that ate one — this chapter is the biochemistry of your entire food chain.
• DEFINITION — Photosynthesis: the synthesis of organic food (glucose) from inorganic raw materials, carbon dioxide and water, using sunlight absorbed by chlorophyll, with oxygen released. The overall equation reads: six molecules of carbon dioxide plus twelve of water give one glucose, six of oxygen and six of water, in the presence of light and chlorophyll.
• DEFINITION — Photosynthesis has two stages that run in different rooms of the chloroplast: the light reactions (photochemical phase) on the thylakoid membranes, which make ATP and NADPH and release oxygen; and the biosynthetic phase (Calvin cycle) in the stroma, which spends that ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into sugar.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — the IAT and NEET-style papers mine it relentlessly: the Z scheme, the source of the released oxygen, ATP and NADPH accounting per glucose, RuBisCO's double life, and the C3-versus-C4 comparison table. Every one of those is a fixed fact pattern, not a thinking question — drill the patterns and the marks are mechanical.
• EXAMPLE — A maize field in June is running this chapter at industrial scale: millions of chloroplasts per leaf, carbon dioxide entering through stomata, water climbing from the roots, and oxygen drifting out as waste. The plant keeps the sugar; the planet keeps the oxygen.
• WORKED PATTERN — The master key for the whole chapter: keep the two phases in two columns. Left column (light reactions): needs light, happens on thylakoids, splits water, releases oxygen, produces ATP and NADPH. Right column (Calvin cycle): needs no light directly, happens in stroma, consumes ATP and NADPH, fixes carbon dioxide into sugar. Any question in this chapter can be sorted by asking which column it belongs to.
• YOUR TURN — A leaf is kept in the dark but supplied with ready-made ATP and NADPH in its chloroplasts. Which phase can still run briefly? Answer: the biosynthetic phase (Calvin cycle) — it uses ATP and NADPH, not light itself; the light reactions stop at once because they are driven by photons.
★ HIGHLIGHT — One sentence to carry through the chapter: light reactions convert light into two currencies, ATP and NADPH; the Calvin cycle spends both currencies to turn carbon dioxide into food.

Early Experiments — How We Know What We Know
• DEFINITION — The history of photosynthesis is a chain of clean experiments, and examiners ask who showed what. Joseph Priestley (1770s) showed that a plant restores air "injured" by a burning candle or a breathing mouse — the first hint that plants release something animals need.
• DEFINITION — Jan Ingenhousz showed that sunlight is essential and that only the green parts of plants release oxygen: an aquatic plant in bright sunlight bubbled oxygen, while in darkness it did not. He also showed plants can purify air only in light.
• DEFINITION — Julius von Sachs showed that green parts are where glucose is made, and that the glucose is stored as starch — which is why the iodine test on a destarched leaf is still the school proof of photosynthesis.
• DEFINITION — T. W. Engelmann split light into its spectrum with a prism and watched aerobic bacteria cluster along an algal filament (Cladophora): the bacteria crowded in the blue and red regions, mapping the first action spectrum of photosynthesis and showing oxygen release is maximal in blue and red light.
• DEFINITION — Cornelius van Niel, studying purple and green sulphur bacteria, proposed that water is the hydrogen donor in plant photosynthesis and that the released oxygen comes from water, not carbon dioxide. Ruben and Kamen later confirmed it using the heavy isotope of oxygen, oxygen-18: plants given labelled water released labelled oxygen.
• WHY these names matter — each experiment answers exactly one exam question: Priestley equals plants restore air; Ingenhousz equals sunlight plus green parts; Sachs equals glucose made in green parts and stored as starch; Engelmann equals action spectrum, blue and red most effective; van Niel equals water is the hydrogen donor; Ruben and Kamen equals isotope proof that oxygen comes from water.
• EXAMPLE — The isotope experiment in numbers: supply water containing oxygen-18 and normal carbon dioxide, and the released oxygen gas is the labelled kind. Reverse the labels — normal water, labelled carbon dioxide — and the released oxygen is normal. The oxygen tracks the water every time.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any "who proved" question, match the verb: restored air is Priestley; sunlight essential is Ingenhousz; starch as stored product is Sachs; bacteria clustering on a spectrum is Engelmann; hydrogen donor idea is van Niel; isotope confirmation is Ruben and Kamen.
• YOUR TURN — An examiner describes bacteria crowding in the blue and red bands of a spectrum projected on an algal filament. Whose experiment, and what did it measure? Answer: Engelmann's; it gave the first action spectrum — the rate of photosynthesis (tracked by oxygen-seeking bacteria) at each wavelength.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from water, never from carbon dioxide. This is the single most repeated trap in the chapter — van Niel proposed it and the oxygen-18 experiments proved it.

Where Photosynthesis Happens — The Chloroplast Stage
• DEFINITION — In higher plants photosynthesis is carried out mainly by the leaves, and inside leaf cells by chloroplasts — lens-shaped organelles aligned along the walls of mesophyll cells so they can position themselves for maximum light capture.
• DEFINITION — A chloroplast has a double outer membrane, an internal fluid matrix called the stroma, and a third membrane system, the thylakoids — flattened membranous sacs stacked like piles of coins into grana, connected by stroma lamellae. The space inside a thylakoid is its lumen.
• DEFINITION — The labour is divided by location: the light reactions sit on the thylakoid membrane because that is where chlorophyll and the electron carriers are embedded; the enzymes of the Calvin cycle float in the stroma, which is also where starch grains form.
• WHY the address matters — nearly every diagram question in this chapter is an address question: water is split on the lumen side of the thylakoid membrane, protons pile up inside the lumen, ATP is released into the stroma, and sugar is built in the stroma. Learn the floor plan once and the Z scheme and chemiosmosis sections become geography, not memorisation.
• EXAMPLE — Chloroplasts move inside the cell: under weak light they spread along the walls facing the light to catch more; under harsh light they line up edge-on to avoid damage. The organelle treats light as a resource to be dosed, not just collected.
• WORKED PATTERN — Address drill: chlorophyll and photosystems — thylakoid membrane. Water splitting — inner (lumen) side of the membrane near photosystem II. Proton reservoir — thylakoid lumen. ATP synthase exit and ATP release — stroma side. Calvin cycle and RuBisCO — stroma. Repeat this until the addresses are automatic.
• YOUR TURN — A thylakoid is punctured so its lumen leaks into the stroma. Which product of the light reactions collapses first, NADPH or ATP? Answer: ATP — its synthesis depends on the proton gradient across an intact membrane; NADP reduction by electrons can continue briefly.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Grana are stacks of thylakoids; the membrane of the thylakoid is the factory floor of the light reactions, and the stroma is the assembly hall of the Calvin cycle.

Pigments — Catching Light by Colour
• DEFINITION — Photosynthetic pigments are molecules that absorb particular wavelengths of light. The leaf's set includes chlorophyll a (bright or blue-green in colour), chlorophyll b (yellow-green), xanthophylls (yellow) and carotenoids (yellow to yellow-orange).
• DEFINITION — Chlorophyll a is the chief pigment: it alone converts light energy into chemical energy directly, forming the reaction centres of the photosystems. The other pigments are accessory pigments — they absorb light and hand the energy to chlorophyll a, widening the wavelengths the plant can use, and they also shield chlorophyll from photo-oxidation.
• DEFINITION — An absorption spectrum shows how much light a pigment absorbs at each wavelength. An action spectrum shows the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength. Plotted together, the two curves overlap closely, with peaks in the blue and red regions — proof that chlorophylls drive photosynthesis.
• WHY leaves look green — chlorophyll absorbs blue and red strongly but reflects and transmits green, so green light bounces off the leaf into your eye. The plant is not using the colour you see; it is throwing it away. Green is the leftover, not the main course.
• EXAMPLE — Autumn leaves turn yellow and orange because chlorophyll breaks down first and unmasks the carotenoids and xanthophylls that were there all along. The autumn colours are accessory pigments finally visible.
• WORKED PATTERN — Pigment questions ask three things: which is chief (chlorophyll a), which does what (accessories absorb and transfer, and protect), and which colours are absorbed best (blue and red, worst is green). Anchor: reaction centre of photosystem II is P680 and of photosystem I is P700 — named after the wavelength, in nanometres, at which each absorbs best.
• YOUR TURN — If only accessory pigments could absorb light and chlorophyll a were missing, could photosynthesis run? Answer: no — accessories can only pass energy along; without chlorophyll a reaction centres the energy has nowhere to become chemistry.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Chlorophyll a is the only pigment that directly converts light energy to chemical energy; do not credit chlorophyll b or carotenoids with reaction centres. And a pigment's colour is the light it reflects, not the light it absorbs.

Absorption and Action Spectra — The Overlap That Convicts Chlorophyll
• DEFINITION — The absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a peaks sharply in the blue region and again in the red region of visible light, dipping in between — which is exactly where Engelmann's bacteria clustered thickest.
• DEFINITION — The action spectrum of photosynthesis follows the same blue-and-red twin-peak shape. The near-overlap of the action spectrum with the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a (with a contribution from chlorophyll b) is the evidence that chlorophylls are the pigments responsible for photosynthesis.
• WHY the overlap is the argument — if photosynthesis ran fastest at wavelengths chlorophyll barely absorbs, chlorophyll would be acquitted of the crime. Instead the rate curve tracks the absorption curve peak for peak, so the pigment absorbing the light must be the one driving the reaction.
• EXAMPLE — A plant grown under pure green light photosynthesises poorly and looks starved, even in bright light — most green photons are reflected unused. Under mixed red and blue light, the same plant thrives at lower brightness.
• WORKED PATTERN — Graph questions: read the wavelength axis first (blue end around 400 to 450 nanometres, red end around 650 to 680), then match peaks. Absorption peak of chlorophyll a equals blue and red. Action peak equals blue and red. Conclusion sentence: photosynthesis is most efficient in blue and red light because chlorophyll a absorbs maximally there.
• YOUR TURN — At which visible colour is the action spectrum lowest, and why? Answer: green — chlorophyll absorbs green least and reflects it, so few green photons are captured to drive the reaction.
[diagram — absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a overlaid on the action spectrum of photosynthesis; the twin peaks in blue and red nearly coincide]
★ HIGHLIGHT — Engelmann's bacteria were the first action spectrum; the modern graph says the same thing: photosynthesis rides on the blue and red peaks of chlorophyll absorption.

Two Photosystems — The Light Reactions Begin
• DEFINITION — The light reactions (photochemical phase) run on the thylakoid membrane. Pigments are organised into two light-harvesting complexes, photosystem II (PS II) and photosystem I (PS I), each a cluster of pigments around one reaction-centre chlorophyll a molecule.
• DEFINITION — The reaction centre of PS II absorbs best at 680 nanometres and is called P680; the reaction centre of PS I absorbs best at 700 nanometres and is called P700. The systems were named in the order of their discovery, not the order in which electrons visit them — electrons flow PS II first, then PS I.
• DEFINITION — A light-harvesting complex (antenna complex) contains hundreds of pigment molecules — chlorophyll b, xanthophylls, carotenoids and many chlorophyll a — all funnelling absorbed energy to the single reaction centre, like a satellite dish focusing a signal.
• WHY two systems are needed — one photon boost is not enough to lift an electron from water all the way up to NADP. So the electron is boosted twice: PS II lifts it from water to an intermediate carrier chain, and PS I lifts it again to the energy level needed to reduce NADP. Two lifts, two photosystems, one famous zigzag.
• EXAMPLE — Think of it as a two-storey escalator: water is at the ground floor (low energy), NADPH is on the second floor (high energy). PS II is the first escalator, the carrier chain a landing where the electron gives up some energy to make ATP, and PS I the second escalator.
• WORKED PATTERN — Order drill for any Z-scheme question: light hits PS II first (despite the numbering), water is split there, electrons travel down the carrier chain (plastoquinone, cytochrome complex, plastocyanin), then PS I absorbs a second photon, and electrons pass via ferredoxin to NADP reductase, which makes NADPH on the stroma side.
• YOUR TURN — Which photosystem was discovered first, and which acts first in the electron flow? Answer: PS I was discovered first (hence its name), but in the flow of electrons PS II acts first — discovery order and working order are opposite.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — PS II comes before PS I in the pathway, and PS II is associated with water splitting and oxygen evolution; PS I alone runs the cyclic pathway. Swapping the two systems' jobs is the most common Z-scheme error.

The Z Scheme — Non-Cyclic Electron Flow
• DEFINITION — In non-cyclic photophosphorylation, electrons expelled from PS II are replaced by electrons from splitting water, travel through a carrier chain to PS I, and end by reducing NADP plus a hydrogen ion to NADPH. Because both photosystems work together, the products are ATP, NADPH and oxygen.
• DEFINITION — Photolysis: the light-driven splitting of water at PS II, on the lumen side. Two water molecules split into four protons, four electrons and one oxygen molecule. The electrons refill P680, the protons add to the lumen pool, and the oxygen diffuses out — this is the oxygen we breathe.
• DEFINITION — As electrons pass from PS II down the carrier chain to PS I, they lose energy; part of that energy pumps protons from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen, building the gradient that chemiosmosis will spend on ATP. The chain's downhill slide is what pays for the uphill proton pumping.
• WHY it is called the Z scheme — plot electron energy against the path and the line zigzags: up at PS II, downhill along the carriers, up again at PS I, then a short drop to NADPH — a sideways Z drawn by two light boosts.
• EXAMPLE — Every breath connects here: the oxygen released by a paddy field's photolysis left a water molecule seconds earlier, having donated its electrons to refill P680. Atmospheric oxygen is photolysis exhaust.
• WORKED PATTERN — Trace one electron pair end to end: water (at PS II) to P680, boosted by light, down through plastoquinone, cytochrome complex and plastocyanin, into P700, boosted again by light, through ferredoxin, onto NADP reductase, ending in NADPH in the stroma. If a question starts anywhere on this chain, walk it in this order.
• YOUR TURN — A herbicide blocks electron flow between PS II and PS I. Which products stop: oxygen, ATP, NADPH — all, some? Answer: all three eventually — water splitting halts once P680 cannot pass electrons on, NADP is never reduced, and the proton pump stalls, so ATP synthesis dies too.
[diagram — the Z scheme: electron energy plotted against the path from water through P680, the carrier chain and P700 up to NADPH, with oxygen released at PS II]
★ HIGHLIGHT — The Z scheme in one line: water to PS II to carriers to PS I to NADPH — with oxygen out at the start, and proton pumping (hence ATP) paid for by the downhill stretch in the middle.

Chemiosmosis — Protons Pay for ATP
• DEFINITION — Chemiosmosis is ATP synthesis driven by a proton gradient across a membrane. In the chloroplast, protons accumulate inside the thylakoid lumen, and their flow back into the stroma through ATP synthase powers the joining of ADP and inorganic phosphate into ATP.
• DEFINITION — Three sources fill the lumen with protons: water splitting releases protons directly into the lumen; the plastoquinone shuttle carries protons from the stroma into the lumen as electrons pass; and NADP reductase removes protons from the stroma when it makes NADPH, raising the gradient further.
• DEFINITION — ATP synthase has two parts: CF0, a hydrophobic stalk embedded in the membrane forming the proton channel, and CF1, a head that projects into the stroma and actually builds ATP. Protons pass through CF0, and for each ATP made, protons flow down the gradient through this complex.
• WHY a membrane is non-negotiable — a gradient is stored separation; puncture the membrane and protons leak everywhere, the separation vanishes, and ATP synthesis stops even though electrons may still flow. Chemiosmosis is a dam: the lumen is the reservoir, ATP synthase the turbine, and a leaky dam turns no turbine.
• EXAMPLE — Uncoupler chemicals prove the point: they make the thylakoid membrane leaky to protons, so illuminated chloroplasts keep splitting water and releasing oxygen but make almost no ATP. Electron flow and ATP synthesis are coupled only through the gradient.
• WORKED PATTERN — Site questions on chemiosmosis: gradient builds across the thylakoid membrane; protons are high in the lumen, low in the stroma; breakdown of the gradient is exergonic and drives ATP formation; ATP appears on the stroma side, exactly where the Calvin cycle will spend it. Note the contrast examiners love: in mitochondria, protons are pumped out of the matrix; in chloroplasts, into the lumen — but in both, ATP is made where the sugar or cell work happens.
• YOUR TURN — If the stroma were made artificially acidic (protons added), would ATP synthesis rise or fall? Answer: fall — the gradient depends on lumen high, stroma low; acidifying the stroma flattens the difference that drives ATP synthase.
[diagram — thylakoid membrane cross-section showing protons accumulating in the lumen from water splitting and the plastoquinone shuttle, then flowing through ATP synthase (CF0-CF1) to make ATP in the stroma]
⚠ EXAM TRAP — ATP from the light reactions is made in the chloroplast by chemiosmosis across the thylakoid membrane — not the inner chloroplast membrane, and the protons accumulate in the lumen, not the stroma.

Cyclic Photophosphorylation — ATP Only, Please
• DEFINITION — In cyclic photophosphorylation only PS I works. The electron expelled from P700 travels through carriers and returns to the same P700 — a closed loop. The loop pumps protons, so ATP is made, but no water is split, no oxygen is released and no NADPH is formed.
• DEFINITION — Cyclic flow is expected when only ATP is needed, or when the wavelength of light available is beyond 680 nanometres, which excites PS I but not PS II. Living stroma cells and the bundle sheath cells of C4 plants lean on cyclic flow for extra ATP.
• WHY a second pathway exists — the Calvin cycle needs more ATP than NADPH (three ATP for every two NADPH per carbon dioxide). Non-cyclic flow alone can leave the cell short of ATP; cyclic flow is the top-up generator that pays the ATP bill without making unneeded NADPH.
• EXAMPLE — The C4 pump in bundle sheath cells runs largely on cyclic photophosphorylation: those cells often have few or no PS II complexes, make ATP aplenty for running the carbon pump, and release little oxygen near RuBisCO — a bonus, since oxygen near RuBisCO invites photorespiration.
• WORKED PATTERN — Cyclic versus non-cyclic in four rows: photosystems — only PS I, versus PS II plus PS I. Source of electrons — recycled from P700 itself, versus water. Products — ATP only, versus ATP plus NADPH plus oxygen. Photolysis — absent, versus present. Learn it as a table; the exam asks it as a table.
• YOUR TURN — Light of 720 nanometres shines on a leaf. Which pathway dominates and why? Answer: cyclic flow — 720 nanometres excites P700 of PS I only, since PS II needs wavelengths at or below about 680; without PS II there is no photolysis and no NADPH.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Cyclic equals PS I alone, ATP alone, no oxygen, no NADPH. If any of those three "no" facts flips in a question, it is describing non-cyclic flow.

The Calvin Cycle — Building Sugar in the Stroma
• DEFINITION — The Calvin cycle (biosynthetic phase) runs in the stroma and fixes carbon dioxide into carbohydrate using the ATP and NADPH from the light reactions. It does not need light directly, but it depends on the light reactions' products, so it soon stops in darkness.
• DEFINITION — Step one, carboxylation: carbon dioxide joins the five-carbon acceptor RuBP (ribulose bisphosphate), catalysed by the enzyme RuBisCO — ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase — producing an unstable six-carbon intermediate that splits at once into two molecules of the three-carbon acid PGA (3-phosphoglyceric acid), the first stable product.
• DEFINITION — Step two, reduction: PGA is reduced to triose phosphate (PGAL) using ATP and NADPH — for every carbon dioxide fixed, two ATP and two NADPH are spent in the cycle overall, with the reduction step consuming the NADPH. Step three, regeneration: most of the triose phosphate is recycled, spending more ATP, to rebuild RuBP so the cycle can turn again; the surplus triose phosphate leaves to build glucose, sucrose and starch.
• WHY RuBisCO gets a whole biography — it is probably the most abundant enzyme on Earth, and the slowest important one: it works leisurely, so plants compensate by packing leaves with enormous quantities of it. Its active site also makes the chapter's famous mistake — binding oxygen instead of carbon dioxide — which is photorespiration, two sections ahead.
• EXAMPLE — Count the turns: to net one glucose (six carbons), the cycle must fix six carbon dioxide molecules — six turns. After three turns, one triose phosphate is the net gain while the rest regenerate the three RuBP acceptors. Examiners love this arithmetic.
• WORKED PATTERN — Calvin questions fall into three slots: first product equals PGA (three carbons); acceptor equals RuBP (five carbons); enzyme equals RuBisCO. If a question offers phosphoenol pyruvate or oxaloacetic acid, it has switched cycles — that is C4 territory, not Calvin.
• YOUR TURN — A stroma extract is given RuBP, ATP and NADPH but no carbon dioxide. Does glucose form? Answer: no — ATP and NADPH are only the spending power; without carbon dioxide there is no carbon to fix, so no sugar can be built.
[diagram — the Calvin cycle loop: RuBP accepting carbon dioxide, PGA formed, reduction to triose phosphate using ATP and NADPH, sugar leaving, and RuBP regenerated with further ATP]
★ HIGHLIGHT — Fix the triad in memory: acceptor RuBP (5C), first product PGA (3C), enzyme RuBisCO. Half the chapter's MCQs are this triad wearing different costumes.

The Cost Sheet — ATP and NADPH Accounts
• DEFINITION — For fixing one carbon dioxide molecule, the Calvin cycle spends three ATP and two NADPH. For one glucose molecule — six carbon dioxides — the bill is eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH. These are the two numbers the whole chapter is audited against.
• DEFINITION — The NADPH and part of the ATP are supplied by non-cyclic flow, but the ATP demand exceeds what non-cyclic flow alone comfortably yields alongside NADPH — the shortfall is exactly why cyclic photophosphorylation exists as a supplementary ATP source.
• WHY bookkeeping wins marks — paper setters phrase cost questions in disguises: per glucose, per triose phosphate netted, per three turns. The unit conversions are the question. One net triose phosphate (three carbons) costs nine ATP and six NADPH; one glucose (six carbons) costs eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH; one turn (one carbon dioxide) costs three ATP and two NADPH.
• EXAMPLE — A mesophyll cell fixes twelve carbon dioxide molecules in a burst. The bill: thirty-six ATP and twenty-four NADPH, for two glucose equivalents. If a question's options include 18 ATP and 12 NADPH, it has priced one glucose — check the carbon count before choosing.
• WORKED PATTERN — Always convert the question to carbon dioxide molecules first: n carbon dioxides fixed means 3n ATP and 2n NADPH. Glucose means n equals six; one triose phosphate out means n equals three. Write the conversion line before touching the options.
• YOUR TURN — How many ATP and NADPH are needed to produce three molecules of glucose? Answer: fifty-four ATP and thirty-six NADPH — eighteen carbon dioxides fixed at three ATP and two NADPH each.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not report the glucose bill as 12 ATP and 18 NADPH by swapping the pair. The ATP number is always the bigger one: eighteen ATP, twelve NADPH per glucose; three ATP, two NADPH per carbon dioxide.

Photorespiration — RuBisCO's Costly Mistake
• DEFINITION — Photorespiration is the process in which RuBisCO binds oxygen instead of carbon dioxide: RuBP plus oxygen gives one molecule of PGA (three carbons) and one of phosphoglycolate (two carbons). The salvage pathway that follows, the C2 cycle, runs through chloroplasts, peroxisomes and mitochondria.
• DEFINITION — Photorespiration is called wasteful because in it no sugar, no ATP and no NADPH is synthesised; worse, previously fixed carbon dioxide is released. In C3 plants — wheat, rice, soybean — a significant part of the fixed carbon can be lost this way, especially on hot, bright, dry days.
• WHY the mistake happens — RuBisCO's active site evolved when air held little oxygen, so it discriminates poorly. When stomata close at midday to save water, carbon dioxide inside the leaf falls and oxygen from photolysis builds up — the oxygenation reaction wins more often precisely when the day is hottest.
• EXAMPLE — A rice crop on a blazing afternoon with closed stomata is photorespiring hard: carbon dioxide is scarce inside the leaf, oxygen is plentiful, and RuBisCO keeps grabbing the wrong substrate. This is the evolutionary opening that C4 plants exploited.
• WORKED PATTERN — RuBisCO questions ask which substrate gives which first product: carbon dioxide gives two PGA (3C plus 3C); oxygen gives one PGA (3C) plus one phosphoglycolate (2C). If an option shows phosphoglycolate, the reaction described is oxygenation — photorespiration — whatever the question calls it.
• YOUR TURN — In photorespiration, is ATP consumed or produced overall, and is carbon dioxide released or fixed? Answer: ATP is consumed in salvaging, none is produced, and carbon dioxide is released — the exact reverse of photosynthesis's profit sheet.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Photorespiration produces no ATP and no NADPH and releases carbon dioxide. Any option crediting it with energy gain or carbon fixation is wrong — and it occurs only in the light, in photosynthetic (C3) cells.

The C4 Pathway — A Carbon Pump
• DEFINITION — In C4 plants such as maize and sorghum, carbon dioxide is fixed twice. First, in mesophyll cells, the three-carbon acceptor phosphoenol pyruvate (PEP) joins carbon dioxide via the enzyme PEP carboxylase, forming the four-carbon acid oxaloacetic acid (OAA) — the first product that gives C4 its name.
• DEFINITION — OAA is converted to malic or aspartic acid and shipped to bundle sheath cells, where it is decarboxylated — releasing concentrated carbon dioxide around RuBisCO, which then runs the ordinary Calvin cycle there. The three-carbon remainder returns to the mesophyll to regenerate PEP, costing ATP.
• DEFINITION — Kranz anatomy is the wreath-like leaf structure of C4 plants: large bundle sheath cells, packed with chloroplasts, ring the vascular bundles, surrounded by mesophyll cells. "Kranz" means wreath — anatomy and pathway are one package: mesophyll fixes first, bundle sheath runs the Calvin cycle.
• DEFINITION — PEP carboxylase has a much higher affinity for carbon dioxide than RuBisCO and, crucially, no oxygenase activity. The pump therefore floods RuBisCO with carbon dioxide and starves photorespiration — in C4 plants photorespiration does not occur, because the oxygenase activity of RuBisCO is negligible at the pumped-up carbon dioxide concentration.
• WHY pay for a pump — the C4 cycle spends extra ATP (two extra ATP equivalents per carbon dioxide, five ATP total per carbon dioxide against three in C3) but buys near-zero photorespiration and the ability to photosynthesise fast in hot, bright, dry climates with stomata half closed. In the tropics the trade is hugely profitable.
• EXAMPLE — Sugarcane and maize outgrow wheat in hot fields for exactly this reason: their bundle sheath cells run the Calvin cycle in a carbon-dioxide jacuzzi, while the wheat leaf's RuBisCO is forever distracted by oxygen.
• WORKED PATTERN — C4 identification drill: first product equals OAA (4C); first enzyme equals PEP carboxylase; first cell equals mesophyll; Calvin cell equals bundle sheath; anatomy equals Kranz; photorespiration equals absent. Six slots — a C4 question fills one and asks another.
• YOUR TURN — A leaf section shows large bundle sheath cells full of chloroplasts ringing the veins, and its first fixation product is a four-carbon acid. C3 or C4, and which enzyme made that first product? Answer: C4; PEP carboxylase in the mesophyll cells.
[diagram — Kranz anatomy: mesophyll cells around a bundle sheath ring, carbon dioxide fixed by PEP carboxylase into OAA, converted to malate, carried to the bundle sheath, and released to RuBisCO and the Calvin cycle]
★ HIGHLIGHT — The C4 trick in one line: fix carbon dioxide twice — once cheaply by PEP carboxylase in the mesophyll, once properly by RuBisCO in the bundle sheath — so RuBisCO never sees a low-carbon-dioxide moment.

CAM — Photosynthesis on Night Shift
• DEFINITION — CAM (crassulacean acid metabolism) is a timing variant found in desert plants such as cacti, pineapple and Kalanchoe: stomata open at night, when evaporation is low, and carbon dioxide is fixed by PEP carboxylase into organic acids (mainly malic acid) stored in vacuoles.
• DEFINITION — By day, with stomata firmly shut against the heat, the stored acid is decarboxylated and the released carbon dioxide feeds the Calvin cycle in the same cells. CAM separates the two fixations in time; C4 separates them in space.
• WHY the night shift pays — a desert plant that opened stomata at noon would bleed water. By opening at night and banking carbon as acid, the CAM plant photosynthesises all day behind closed doors, at the cost of slow growth limited by its vacuole storage.
• EXAMPLE — A pineapple field fixes much of its carbon while you sleep: taste the logic — pineapple leaves are slightly more acidic at dawn (full of stored malic acid) and less acidic by dusk (the acid spent on sugar-making).
• WORKED PATTERN — CAM questions reduce to three facts: stomata open at night; first fixation by PEP carboxylase into malic acid stored in the vacuole; Calvin cycle by day in the same cell. If a question separates fixation between two cell types, that is C4; if between night and day in one cell, that is CAM.
• YOUR TURN — Two desert plants: plant A has Kranz anatomy and fixes carbon dioxide in mesophyll by day; plant B lacks Kranz but its leaves are most acidic at dawn. Classify them. Answer: A is C4 (spatial separation); B is CAM (temporal separation).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — CAM plants do run the Calvin cycle in daylight like everyone else — what they do at night is only the first fixation. "Photosynthesis at night" is a headline, not the mechanism; the light reactions still need the sun.

C3 versus C4 — The Head-to-Head
• DEFINITION — The two pathways differ at the first step: C3 plants fix carbon dioxide directly with RuBisCO into PGA (three carbons) in mesophyll cells; C4 plants fix it first with PEP carboxylase into OAA (four carbons) in mesophyll, then refix it with RuBisCO in bundle sheath cells.
• DEFINITION — The consequence column: C4 plants show no photorespiration, tolerate higher temperatures, and use water more efficiently; C3 plants avoid the pump's extra ATP cost and perform relatively better in cool, moist, shaded conditions.
• WHY the comparison is the examiners' favourite table — it compresses the entire chapter into columns: first product, first enzyme, cell types, anatomy, photorespiration, cost per carbon dioxide (three ATP in C3 versus five ATP in C4, counting the pump), and example plants (rice and wheat versus maize and sorghum).
• EXAMPLE — Put both in one field trial: a cool, wet spring favours the wheat (C3); a hot, dry summer flips the advantage to maize (C4), whose carbon pump keeps RuBisCO saturated even with stomata nearly shut.
• WORKED PATTERN — Table completion drill, row by row: first stable product — PGA versus OAA. Primary acceptor — RuBP (5C) versus PEP (3C). Fixing enzyme first used — RuBisCO versus PEP carboxylase. Calvin cycle site — mesophyll versus bundle sheath. Photorespiration — significant versus absent. Extra energy cost — none versus two additional ATP per carbon dioxide. Any single row identifies the column.
• YOUR TURN — A plant's leaf lacks Kranz anatomy, its first product is PGA, and it loses fixed carbon on hot afternoons. Pathway and one example? Answer: C3 — as in rice or wheat; the hot-afternoon loss is photorespiration.

Factors Affecting Photosynthesis — Blackman's Law
• DEFINITION — Blackman's law of limiting factors (1905) states that when a process depends on several factors, its rate is limited by the factor nearest its minimum value. Raise the limiting factor and the rate rises — until another factor becomes limiting.
• DEFINITION — Light: as intensity increases, photosynthesis rises linearly at first, then levels off at light saturation — around ten percent of full sunlight for many plants — after which more light adds nothing and very intense light can even damage chlorophyll and slow the rate.
• DEFINITION — Carbon dioxide: the atmosphere holds about 0.03 to 0.04 percent carbon dioxide, and this is often the limiting factor in nature. Raising carbon dioxide concentration increases fixation up to about 0.05 percent, beyond which it can become inhibitory at high levels; C3 plants respond to enrichment over a longer range than C4 plants, whose pump already saturates RuBisCO.
• DEFINITION — Temperature: the light reactions are barely temperature-sensitive, but the enzymatic dark reactions are — the rate roughly doubles for each ten-degree rise within limits, peaks at an optimum (higher for C4 than for C3 plants), then crashes as enzymes denature. Water stress acts mainly by closing stomata and cutting off carbon dioxide.
• WHY limiting-factor thinking is the skill — graph questions in this area are pure Blackman: a plateau means something else is limiting. If raising light stops helping, the plant is short of carbon dioxide or warmth; the flat part of every curve is a second factor taking over.
• EXAMPLE — Greenhouse growers exploit this daily: they enrich the air with carbon dioxide and add warmth on bright days, because in a glasshouse at noon the limiting factor is usually carbon dioxide, not light — spending on lamps alone would waste money.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any rate-versus-factor graph: the rising arm names the factor being varied as limiting; the plateau names a hidden second factor. State both in the answer: "rate is limited by light intensity here; beyond the plateau, carbon dioxide concentration limits." That two-part sentence is the complete mark scheme.
• YOUR TURN — A plant's photosynthetic rate stops rising when light is doubled, but rises again when extra carbon dioxide is supplied. What was limiting at the plateau? Answer: carbon dioxide — light had ceased to be the minimum factor, exactly as Blackman's law predicts.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Carbon dioxide enrichment helps C3 plants more than C4 plants: the C4 pump already keeps RuBisCO saturated, so extra air carbon dioxide adds little. Reversing this pair is a favourite examiner's swap.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Photosynthesis equation: carbon dioxide plus water, in light and chlorophyll, gives glucose plus oxygen — and the oxygen comes from the water, proved by oxygen-18 labelling (van Niel proposed, Ruben and Kamen confirmed).
✔ KEY POINT — History map: Priestley, plants restore air; Ingenhousz, sunlight and green parts release oxygen; Sachs, glucose made in green parts, stored as starch; Engelmann, action spectrum by bacterial clustering in blue and red.
✔ KEY POINT — Pigments: chlorophyll a is the chief pigment and the only one forming reaction centres; chlorophyll b, xanthophylls and carotenoids are accessories that absorb, transfer energy and protect. Absorption and action spectra overlap with peaks in blue and red.
✔ KEY POINT — Reaction centres: PS II is P680, PS I is P700 — but electrons flow PS II first. Light-harvesting complexes funnel energy to the reaction centre.
✔ KEY POINT — Z scheme (non-cyclic): water split at PS II gives electrons, protons and oxygen; electrons pass plastoquinone, cytochrome complex, plastocyanin to PS I, then via ferredoxin to NADP reductase; products ATP, NADPH and oxygen.
✔ KEY POINT — Chemiosmosis: protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen (from water splitting and the plastoquinone shuttle); ATP is made as protons flow through ATP synthase (CF0 channel, CF1 head) into the stroma.
✔ KEY POINT — Cyclic flow: PS I only, electron returns to P700; ATP only — no photolysis, no oxygen, no NADPH; favoured by bundle sheath cells and light beyond 680 nanometres.
✔ KEY POINT — Calvin cycle: acceptor RuBP (5C), enzyme RuBisCO, first product PGA (3C); three phases — carboxylation, reduction, regeneration; cost three ATP and two NADPH per carbon dioxide, eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH per glucose.
✔ KEY POINT — Photorespiration: RuBisCO binds oxygen, giving PGA plus phosphoglycolate; the C2 salvage cycle releases carbon dioxide and produces no ATP, no NADPH and no sugar — wasteful, significant in C3 plants on hot days.
✔ KEY POINT — C4 pathway: first fixation by PEP carboxylase in mesophyll into OAA (4C); decarboxylation in bundle sheath feeds RuBisCO; Kranz anatomy; no photorespiration; total cost five ATP per carbon dioxide; examples maize and sorghum.
✔ KEY POINT — CAM: stomata open at night, carbon dioxide stored as malic acid in vacuoles, Calvin cycle runs by day in the same cells — C4 separates fixation in space, CAM in time; examples cactus and pineapple.
✔ KEY POINT — Factors: Blackman's law of limiting factors; light saturates around ten percent of full sunlight; ambient carbon dioxide is 0.03 to 0.04 percent and often limiting, enrichment helps C3 more than C4; temperature optimum is higher in C4 plants.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Saying the released oxygen comes from carbon dioxide. Why it is wrong: isotope labelling shows the oxygen atoms track water; carbon dioxide's oxygen ends up in glucose and water. Correct principle: oxygen evolved equals water split at PS II — photolysis is the source.
• Mistake 2: Placing PS I before PS II in electron flow because of the numbering. Why it is wrong: the numbers record discovery order; the pathway begins at PS II, whose electrons are replaced by water. Correct principle: flow order is PS II, carrier chain, PS I, NADPH.
• Mistake 3: Crediting cyclic photophosphorylation with NADPH or oxygen. Why it is wrong: the electron returns to P700, no water is split and NADP is never reduced. Correct principle: cyclic equals ATP only; oxygen and NADPH mean non-cyclic.
• Mistake 4: Making PGA the first product of the C4 pathway. Why it is wrong: PGA is the first product of the Calvin cycle (C3); C4's first fixation, by PEP carboxylase in mesophyll, gives the four-carbon OAA. Correct principle: first product PGA equals C3; OAA equals C4.
• Mistake 5: Quoting the glucose bill as twelve ATP and eighteen NADPH. Why it is wrong: the pair is swapped — per carbon dioxide it is three ATP and two NADPH, so per glucose it scales to eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH. Correct principle: ATP is always the larger number.
• Mistake 6: Saying photorespiration produces energy or fixes carbon. Why it is wrong: the C2 cycle consumes ATP, releases previously fixed carbon dioxide, and synthesises no ATP, NADPH or sugar. Correct principle: photorespiration is a loss process tied to RuBisCO's oxygenase activity.
• Mistake 7: Locating the Calvin cycle in bundle sheath cells of all plants. Why it is wrong: bundle-sheath Calvin cycles are the C4 arrangement; in C3 plants the Calvin cycle runs in mesophyll cells, the only photosynthetic tissue present. Correct principle: ask the pathway first — C4 splits the work between cells, C3 does not.
• Mistake 8: Claiming green light drives photosynthesis best because leaves are green. Why it is wrong: leaves are green because green is reflected, not absorbed; absorption and action spectra bottom out in green and peak in blue and red. Correct principle: a pigment's colour is the light it rejects.
• Mistake 9: Saying the proton gradient in chloroplasts builds in the stroma. Why it is wrong: protons are pumped into and released inside the thylakoid lumen; the stroma is the low-proton side where ATP is delivered. Correct principle: lumen high, stroma low — ATP synthase drains the lumen into the stroma.
• Mistake 10: Describing CAM plants as photosynthesing at night. Why it is wrong: only the first carbon fixation (PEP carboxylase, into malic acid) happens at night; the light reactions and Calvin cycle still run in daytime. Correct principle: CAM separates fixation in time, but sugar is still made by day.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A chloroplast suspension is illuminated with water labelled with oxygen-18, while the carbon dioxide is unlabelled. What is labelled in the products?
• Given: Photolysis uses water as the electron donor; the Calvin cycle uses carbon dioxide as the carbon source.
• Principle: The evolved oxygen comes entirely from water; the carbon of glucose comes from carbon dioxide.
• Steps: Water labelled means photolysis releases labelled oxygen gas. Glucose carbon comes from unlabelled carbon dioxide, so glucose is unlabelled.
• Answer: The released oxygen is labelled with oxygen-18; the glucose is not.
• Reasoning: This is the Ruben-Kamen design — the label follows the water into oxygen only, which is how the source of photosynthetic oxygen was settled.
• Mistake to avoid: Labelling the glucose "because photosynthesis makes it from water and carbon dioxide" — the carbon skeleton is purely carbon dioxide's contribution.
• Example 2 — Problem: A leaf uses a herbicide that specifically blocks the cytochrome complex between the two photosystems. Predict the effect on oxygen release, NADPH formation and ATP synthesis.
• Given: The cytochrome complex sits on the single carrier chain between PS II and PS I in non-cyclic flow, and its operation helps pump protons.
• Principle: Blocking the only road stops everything downstream and, by back-pressure, everything upstream too.
• Steps: Electrons cannot reach PS I, so NADP is not reduced — no NADPH. P680 cannot unload electrons, so water splitting stalls — oxygen release stops. Proton pumping by the chain stops, so chemiosmotic ATP synthesis stops (a little cyclic flow around PS I may persist briefly if the block is partial, but the main ATP supply ends).
• Answer: All three fall to near zero; non-cyclic photophosphorylation is shut down as a unit.
• Reasoning: The Z scheme is a single pipeline, not parallel routes — one blockage starves the whole line.
• Mistake to avoid: Claiming oxygen continues because "PS II still works" — PS II works only while it can pass electrons onward.
• Example 3 — Problem: Calculate the ATP and NADPH cost of synthesising two molecules of glucose from carbon dioxide, and state how many turns of the Calvin cycle are involved.
• Given: One glucose needs six carbon dioxides; per carbon dioxide the cost is three ATP and two NADPH; one turn fixes one carbon dioxide.
• Principle: Scale the per-carbon-dioxide bill by the total carbons fixed.
• Steps: Two glucoses need twelve carbon dioxides, so twelve turns. ATP: twelve times three equals thirty-six. NADPH: twelve times two equals twenty-four.
• Answer: Thirty-six ATP and twenty-four NADPH; twelve turns of the cycle.
• Reasoning: Every cost question reduces to counting fixed carbon dioxides first; the multipliers three and two do the rest.
• Mistake to avoid: Answering eighteen ATP and twelve NADPH — that prices one glucose, not two; the question doubled the product.
• Example 4 — Problem: A C3 leaf and a C4 leaf are both placed in bright light with stomata nearly closed at noon. Which keeps fixing carbon faster, and why?
• Given: Closed stomata cut carbon dioxide entry; internal carbon dioxide falls and oxygen from photolysis accumulates in the C3 leaf.
• Principle: RuBisCO oxygenation rises as the carbon dioxide to oxygen ratio falls; the C4 pump maintains high carbon dioxide around RuBisCO regardless.
• Steps: In the C3 leaf, RuBisCO increasingly binds oxygen — photorespiration climbs, net fixation falls. In the C4 leaf, PEP carboxylase still scavenges the little carbon dioxide entering, and decarboxylation keeps bundle sheath carbon dioxide high — Calvin cycle runs near full speed.
• Answer: The C4 leaf fixes carbon faster; the C3 leaf loses ground to photorespiration.
• Reasoning: The pump is insurance against exactly this midday squeeze — its ATP premium buys carbon dioxide security.
• Mistake to avoid: Choosing C3 because it "spends less ATP" — economy per carbon is irrelevant once the substrate itself runs out.
• Example 5 — Problem: Bundle sheath cells of maize are found to contain very few PS II complexes but abundant PS I. How do they make the large amounts of ATP the carbon pump and Calvin cycle need, and what side benefit follows?
• Given: PS I alone can run cyclic photophosphorylation; ATP is needed to regenerate PEP and to run the Calvin cycle (three ATP per carbon dioxide there).
• Principle: Cyclic flow around PS I produces ATP without NADPH and without splitting water.
• Steps: Electrons loop from P700 through carriers back to P700, pumping protons each round; ATP synthase converts the gradient into ATP continuously. Since no water is split, little oxygen is generated inside the bundle sheath.
• Answer: By cyclic photophosphorylation around PS I — and the side benefit is low oxygen around RuBisCO, further suppressing photorespiration.
• Reasoning: Anatomy, pathway and photorespiration all interlock: agranal bundle sheath chloroplasts are the physical basis of this answer.
• Mistake to avoid: Saying they import all ATP from mesophyll cells — the cells are largely self-sufficient via cyclic flow.
• Example 6 — Problem: In an experiment, raising light intensity increases the photosynthetic rate up to a point; beyond it, only adding carbon dioxide raises the rate further. Explain the plateau using a named law, and name the limiting factor in each region.
• Given: Two successive behaviours — light-responsive rise, then light-insensitive plateau that carbon dioxide relieves.
• Principle: Blackman's law of limiting factors: the rate is set by the factor nearest its minimum.
• Steps: On the rising arm, light is the minimum factor, so the rate tracks it. On the plateau, light is in excess and carbon dioxide (about 0.03 percent in air) has become the minimum — hence only carbon dioxide addition helps.
• Answer: Blackman's law; light limits first, carbon dioxide limits on the plateau.
• Reasoning: A plateau is never "the plant is full" — it is always another factor's turn to be the bottleneck.
• Mistake to avoid: Attributing the plateau to chlorophyll damage — that needs very intense light and produces a decline, not a stable plateau relieved by carbon dioxide.
• Example 7 — Problem: A researcher measures the first stable labelled product after three seconds of photosynthesis in labelled carbon dioxide in two plants: plant X gives a three-carbon acid, plant Y a four-carbon acid. Identify each plant's pathway, first enzyme and the cell where its Calvin cycle runs.
• Given: First product chemistry is the signature of the first fixation step.
• Principle: PGA (3C) means direct RuBisCO fixation (C3); OAA (4C) means PEP carboxylase first (C4).
• Steps: Plant X: C3 pathway; first enzyme RuBisCO; Calvin cycle in mesophyll cells. Plant Y: C4 pathway; first enzyme PEP carboxylase (in mesophyll); Calvin cycle in bundle sheath cells, where the OAA-derived carbon dioxide is released.
• Answer: X is C3 (RuBisCO, mesophyll Calvin cycle); Y is C4 (PEP carboxylase first, bundle sheath Calvin cycle).
• Reasoning: Three seconds is too short for secondary labelling to blur the signature — the first product is the pathway's fingerprint.
• Mistake to avoid: Naming RuBisCO as plant Y's "first enzyme" because it also has RuBisCO — every C4 plant has RuBisCO, but its first fixation is PEP carboxylase's work.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. From water. Van Niel proposed water as the hydrogen donor; Ruben and Kamen confirmed it with oxygen-18 labelled water. Misconception watch: carbon dioxide supplies the carbon of glucose, not the evolved oxygen — keep the two products' parentage separate.
• A2. PS II is P680 and PS I is P700, named for their peak absorption wavelengths in nanometres. PS II acts first in the flow. Misconception watch: the numbering is discovery order, and examiners bank on you reading it as working order.
• A3. Protons accumulate in the thylakoid lumen; they return to the stroma through ATP synthase — CF0 the channel, CF1 the catalytic head. Misconception watch: ATP is released on the stroma side, where the Calvin cycle spends it; saying "matrix" imports mitochondria vocabulary into the wrong organelle.
• A4. Nine ATP and six NADPH — three ATP and two NADPH per carbon dioxide, scaled by three. Misconception watch: do not quote the per-glucose figures (eighteen and twelve) for anything but six carbon dioxides.
• A5. Photorespiration begins with RuBisCO fixing oxygen instead of carbon dioxide; the salvage of phosphoglycolate through the C2 cycle consumes ATP and releases carbon dioxide without synthesising ATP, NADPH or sugar. It is significant in C3 plants. Misconception watch: it is not "respiration in light" in the mitochondrial sense — no ATP is harvested at all.
• A6. Oxaloacetic acid (OAA), a four-carbon acid, made by PEP carboxylase in the mesophyll cells. Misconception watch: PGA is the Calvin cycle's first product, but in maize the Calvin cycle runs second, in bundle sheath cells, on carbon dioxide delivered from OAA breakdown.
• A7. CAM (crassulacean acid metabolism); malic acid, stored in the vacuole after night-time fixation by PEP carboxylase. Misconception watch: dawn acidity is the fingerprint — by evening the acid is spent, so a dusk measurement would miss the diagnosis.
• A8. The plateau means light is no longer the factor nearest its minimum — another factor limits the rate; in ordinary air that is usually carbon dioxide concentration (about 0.03 to 0.04 percent). Misconception watch: "the plant cannot use more light" is incomplete — name the new limiting factor to complete the answer.
