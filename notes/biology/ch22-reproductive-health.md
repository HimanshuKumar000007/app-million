Introduction — Reproductive Health Is Total Well-Being
• Every other chapter asked how reproduction works; this one asks how society keeps reproduction healthy — planned pregnancies, safe methods, infection-free tracts, help for couples who cannot conceive. It is the most applied chapter in the book: nearly every line is a programme year, a method name, a number window or a mechanism, and the exam tests those.
• DEFINITION — According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), reproductive health means a total well-being in all aspects of reproduction — physical, emotional, behavioural and social. Read it slowly: not merely the absence of disease or infertility, but complete well-being across four named dimensions.
• WHY the definition is wider than "no disease" — a person can be disease-free yet reproductively unhealthy: an unplanned pregnancy at the wrong time, anxiety, misinformation and social myths all damage reproductive well-being without any microbe. The four WHO dimensions are the checklist that exposes this.
• EXAMPLE — Picture two couples. Couple one is infection-free but terrified by myths, spacing children by guesswork. Couple two has accurate information, a chosen contraceptive and children by plan. Only the second couple is reproductively healthy in the WHO sense — same bodies, completely different well-being.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sort every statement in this chapter into one of five bins before answering: definition or programme (WHO, RCH, years), contraceptive method (seven families), law and safety (MTP), infection (STIs), or technology (ART). Most wrong options here are correct facts filed in the wrong bin — one method's feature pasted onto another.
• YOUR TURN — A textbook claims reproductive health means "the absence of reproductive disease." What is missing? Answer: the positive half — total well-being in the physical, emotional, behavioural and social aspects of reproduction. Absence of disease is only one slice of it.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The whole chapter in one breath: India built programmes (1951, RCH) to spread awareness; couples choose from seven families of contraception; the law permits MTP under strict conditions; STIs are preventable and mostly curable if caught early; and ART — plus adoption — completes the picture for couples who cannot conceive.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Reproductive health is not a synonym for birth control. Contraception is one section; the WHO definition, programmes, MTP, STIs, infertility and ART carry equal marks, and papers weight the non-contraception sections.

India's Programmes — From Family Planning to RCH
• DEFINITION — India was amongst the first countries to initiate national-level action plans to attain total reproductive health as a social goal. These family planning programmes were initiated in 1951 and periodically assessed over the following decades.
• DEFINITION — Improved programmes covering wider reproduction-related areas run under the Reproductive and Child Health Care programme — RCH — launched in 1997. Its two major tasks: creating awareness about reproduction, and providing facilities and support for a reproductively healthy society.
• WHY awareness is the first task, before services — a contraceptive in a warehouse changes nothing; one understood, accepted and correctly used changes a family. That is why the programmes push information through print and electronic media via governmental and non-governmental agencies, with parents, relatives, teachers and friends as part of the channel.
• EXAMPLE — Sex education is the school-level arm: introducing sex education in schools is encouraged, because right information given at the right time discourages children from believing myths and misconceptions about sex-related aspects. Silence does not protect adolescents; verified information does.
• WORKED PATTERN — Pin the three founding years to their owners and never let them swap: 1951 belongs to family planning programmes, 1971 to the legalisation of MTP, and 1997 to the launch of RCH. Year questions are won or lost on these three pegs alone.
• YOUR TURN — A question says: "Programmes covering wider reproduction-related areas, currently operational, are called..." Which name and which launch year? Answer: Reproductive and Child Health Care (RCH) programmes, launched in 1997 — not "family planning," the 1951 name for the earlier, narrower effort.
✔ KEY POINT — Successful implementation needs strong infrastructural facilities, professional expertise and material support — awareness alone is not a programme. The strategies also target awareness of uncontrolled population growth and of social evils like sex abuse and sex-related crimes.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Amniocentesis samples the fluid around the foetus for diagnosis; the statutory ban is on its misuse for sex determination, to legally check the increasing menace of female foeticide. Options calling amniocentesis itself illegal, or a contraceptive, are wrong — the ban targets one misuse of a test.

The Population Story — Why Stabilisation Matters
• DEFINITION — The population problem in one line: better living conditions and health facilities drove a rapid decline in the death rate, the maternal mortality rate (MMR) and the infant mortality rate (IMR), while the number of people in the reproducible age kept increasing — so the growth rate climbed steeply.
• WHY falling death rates, not rising birth rates, drive the explosion — a population grows when far fewer die young and far more survive through their reproductive years. The success of medicine and sanitation, unaccompanied by a matching fall in family size, produces the steep curve — a success problem, not a failure problem.
• EXAMPLE — Follow one generation. Earlier: of ten babies, several died in infancy and mothers faced real risk in childbirth. Now: nearly all survive to reproductive age. Even if every couple has fewer children than their grandparents did, the sheer number of couples keeps total births — and population — rising.
✔ KEY POINT — The consequences the chapter names are absolute scarcity of the basic requirements of life: food, shelter and clothing. Population stabilisation is therefore presented as an essential, urgent goal — not a preference — and the government was forced to take serious measures against the growth rate.
• WORKED PATTERN — Separate the three lists the exam loves to shuffle. Causes: decline in death rate, MMR and IMR, plus more people in the reproducible age. Consequences: scarcity of food, shelter, clothing. Measures: motivate smaller families via contraceptives, give incentives to small families, raise the statutory marriageable age.
• YOUR TURN — Which legal measure attacks the start of reproductive life directly, and what are its numbers? Answer: the statutory marriageable age — 18 years for females, 21 for males. Delaying marriage shortens the reproductive span and pushes the first pregnancy later.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not write "rising birth rate" as the chapter's cause of population explosion: NCERT's stated causes are the declines (death rate, MMR, IMR) and the larger reproducible-age population. An option crediting better health facilities as a cause is correct; an option blaming a sudden rise in birth rate is the planted error.

Choosing Contraception — What an Ideal Method Looks Like
• DEFINITION — Contraceptive methods are procedures and devices that prevent conception — fusion of sperm and ovum, or its consequences — and an ideal contraceptive should be user-friendly, easily available, effective and reversible, with no or least side-effects.
✔ KEY POINT — The second half of the checklist is the one students drop: an ideal contraceptive should in no way interfere with the sexual drive, desire and/or the sexual act of the user. A method that works biologically but is unacceptable to the couple fails the chapter's definition of ideal.
• WHY reversibility splits the chapter in two — every method is either reversible (stop using it and fertility returns: natural, barrier, IUDs, pills, injectables, implants) or terminal (surgical methods, whose reversibility is very poor). Exam questions constantly probe which side of this line a method stands on.
• EXAMPLE — Run the checklist on a condom: user-friendly yes, easily available yes, effective when used correctly, reversible instantly, side-effects essentially none, no interference with drive or desire. A surgical method: supremely effective, but reversibility fails — which is why surgery is reserved for couples whose families are complete.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify first, mechanism second. The seven families are: natural or traditional methods, barrier methods, intra uterine devices (IUDs), oral contraceptive pills, injectables, implants, and surgical methods. Given any named method in a question, speak its family name first — half the options usually die at that step.
• YOUR TURN — A newly married couple wants to delay their first child by two years and asks for "the strongest method available." Why is the surgical method the wrong suggestion despite being the most effective? Answer: effectiveness is only one line of the checklist; surgical methods are terminal, so a reversible family — IUD, pills, barriers — fits a two-year delay.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Only one family here also protects against STIs: barriers, specifically condoms. Pills, IUDs, injectables, implants, natural and surgical methods prevent pregnancy only. Any option giving an IUD or pill an anti-STI role is false.

Natural Methods — Timing, Withdrawal and Breastfeeding
• DEFINITION — Natural (traditional) methods work on a single principle: avoid the chances of the ovum and the sperms meeting. Because no medicines or devices are used, side-effects are almost nil — but the chances of failure are fairly high, the honest trade-off the chapter states up front.
• DEFINITION — Periodic abstinence: the couple abstains from coitus from day 10 to day 17 of the menstrual cycle, the fertile period when ovulation could be expected and the chances of fertilisation are very high. Pure calendar: identify the dangerous window and stay out of it.
• DEFINITION — Withdrawal, or coitus interruptus: the male partner withdraws his penis from the vagina just before ejaculation, so that insemination is avoided. It needs no device and no preparation — and depends entirely on timing and self-control at the least controlled moment, which is why its failure rate is high.
• DEFINITION — Lactational amenorrhea means the absence of menstruation during intense lactation following parturition. It rests on the fact that ovulation — and therefore the cycle itself — does not occur during intense lactation after childbirth: no egg, no conception.
✔ KEY POINT — Lactational amenorrhea is effective only up to a maximum of six months following parturition, and only while lactation is intense (fully breastfed). While the mother breast-feeds fully, chances of conception are almost nil — but the protection has a hard expiry date.
• WHY the failure rate of natural methods is fairly high — the body is not a clock. Cycles vary, ovulation can arrive early or late, deposited sperm cannot be recalled by withdrawal, and a breastfeeding pattern can thin out gradually. Each natural method leaks where it trusts biology to be regular.
• EXAMPLE — Count a 28-day cycle on your fingers: menstruation begins on day 1, the fertile window runs from day 10 to day 17 with ovulation expected mid-window. A periodic-abstinence couple plans intimacy only outside days 10 to 17 — eight days of abstinence buying a cycle of protection, if the cycle behaves.
• WORKED PATTERN — Match each natural method to the event it blocks. Periodic abstinence blocks the meeting by timing (no coitus in the window). Withdrawal blocks insemination itself (no semen deposited). Lactational amenorrhea blocks ovulation (no egg released at all). Three methods, three different blocked events — the exam shuffles the middle column.
• YOUR TURN — A mother whose baby is eight months old and now takes solid food alongside breast milk asks whether she still needs another contraceptive. Answer: yes — it is effective only up to six months and only during intense, full lactation; at eight months with partial feeding, both conditions have expired.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The fertile window in NCERT is days 10 to 17 — not "the week of menstruation," not days 14 to 21. And lactational amenorrhea works because ovulation is absent, not because the uterus is "resting": the method failing after six months even with full breastfeeding is the chapter's own stated maximum.

Barrier Methods — Blocking the Meeting of Gametes
• DEFINITION — In barrier methods, ovum and sperms are physically prevented from meeting. The barriers available to both males and females are condoms: thin rubber or latex sheaths worn so that ejaculated semen never enters the female reproductive tract.
• DEFINITION — The male condom is worn on the penis; the female condom is designed to cover the vagina and the cervix. Nirodh is a popular brand of condom for the male. Condoms are disposable (single use), are self-inserted, and thereby give privacy to the user — three practical virtues the chapter lists explicitly.
• DEFINITION — Diaphragms, cervical caps and vaults are the other barrier family: rubber devices inserted into the female reproductive tract to cover the cervix during coitus. They prevent conception by blocking the entry of sperms through the cervix — and unlike condoms, they are reusable.
✔ KEY POINT — Spermicidal creams, jellies and foams are usually used along with these barriers to increase their contraceptive efficiency. The spermicide is only the chemical assistant — the barrier does the blocking; questions crediting the cream alone have reversed the partnership.
• WHY condoms stand alone in the whole chapter — they are the only contraceptive that plays two roles at once: they prevent conception, and they protect the user from contracting sexually transmitted infections and AIDS. Every other method guards against pregnancy only; the latex wall stops pathogens as well as sperms.
• EXAMPLE — Compare the two rubber strategies. A condom is a sheath that travels with the act and is thrown away after it. A diaphragm is a cap that stays parked over the cervix and is washed and reused. Same principle — a physical wall between sperm and cervix — opposite habits of use, and the exam tests both.
• WORKED PATTERN — Barrier questions are identification questions: a sheath on the penis or lining the vagina points to condom; a rubber dome over the cervix points to diaphragm, cervical cap or vault. Then check the adjectives: disposable and self-inserted belong to condoms; reusable belongs to diaphragms, caps and vaults. One adjective usually decides the answer.
• YOUR TURN — A couple wants a method the woman controls, that can be used many times, and that needs no hormones. Which family and devices? Answer: barrier methods — a diaphragm, cervical cap or vault, reusable rubber barriers covering her cervix; add a spermicidal cream or jelly for efficiency.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Condoms can be reused after washing" is always false — condoms are disposable. The reusable labels belong to diaphragms, cervical caps and vaults. And the female condom covers the vagina and cervix — options swapping the wearers test attention, not knowledge.

IUDs — Small Devices Inside the Uterus
• DEFINITION — Intra uterine devices (IUDs) are small devices inserted by doctors or expert nurses into the uterus through the vagina. Once in place they work for years without daily attention, which is why IUDs are among the most widely accepted methods of contraception in India.
• DEFINITION — The chapter sorts IUDs into three kinds. Non-medicated IUDs, such as the Lippes loop, release nothing. Copper-releasing IUDs — CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375 — release copper ions. Hormone-releasing IUDs — Progestasert, LNG-20 — release progestogen-type hormones.
• DEFINITION — The shared background mechanism: IUDs increase phagocytosis of sperms within the uterus. Each kind adds its own weapon — copper ions suppress sperm motility and the fertilising capacity of sperms.
✔ KEY POINT — The hormone-releasing IUDs (Progestasert, LNG-20) work at two addresses: they make the uterus unsuitable for implantation and the cervix hostile to the sperms. Learn that pair as a pair — uterus-implantation, cervix-sperms — because options routinely swap the two sites between the two effects.
• WHY an IUD suits spacing better than a pill for many couples — no daily routine, no action at coitus, effectiveness in years, full reversibility on removal. The chapter's own summary: IUDs are ideal for females who want to delay pregnancy and/or space children.
• EXAMPLE — Decode three prescription slips. Slip one says CuT: copper-releasing — Cu ions against sperm motility. Slip two says LNG-20: hormone-releasing — an implantation-proof uterus and a hostile cervix. Slip three says Lippes loop: non-medicated — the background effect, increased phagocytosis of sperms in the uterus.
• WORKED PATTERN — IUD questions are a three-row table read aloud: name, category, mechanism. Lippes loop — non-medicated — phagocytosis. CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375 — copper — Cu ions suppress motility and fertilising capacity. Progestasert, LNG-20 — hormonal — uterus unsuitable for implantation, cervix hostile. An option filing Multiload 375 under hormones falls whole.
• YOUR TURN — A woman wants her next child after four years, dislikes daily pills, and wants the device removed the day she decides. Name the family and one example, with its working principle. Answer: an IUD — for instance CuT; its copper ions suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity, and removal restores fertility.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Copper IUDs do not release hormones and hormone IUDs do not release copper — the middle names are the mechanisms. Also, IUDs are inserted through the vagina into the uterus by doctors or expert nurses; they are not skin implants and not swallowed — each wrong option relocates the device.

Oral Pills — Daily Pills and the Weekly Saheli
• DEFINITION — Oral contraceptives are tablets — called pills — taken by females. They contain small doses of either progestogens alone, or a progestogen-estrogen combination. Taken correctly they are very effective with lesser side effects and well accepted by females.
• DEFINITION — The schedule is a rhythm, not a suggestion: pills are taken daily for 21 days, starting preferably within the first five days of the menstrual cycle. After a gap of seven days, during which menstruation occurs, the pattern repeats as long as pregnancy is to be prevented.
• DEFINITION — Pills work at three points: they inhibit ovulation, they inhibit implantation, and they alter the quality of cervical mucus to prevent or retard the entry of sperms. One small tablet, three defensive lines — no egg, a hostile mucus gate, and a uterus that will not accept an embryo.
✔ KEY POINT — Saheli is the chapter's celebrity pill: a new oral contraceptive for females, developed by scientists at the Central Drug Research Institute (CDRI) in Lucknow, India. It contains a non-steroidal preparation, is taken only once a week, and combines very few side effects with high contraceptive value.
• WHY Saheli breaks the daily-pill pattern — ordinary pills maintain a steady hormonal blockade; Saheli's non-steroidal preparation instead blocks estrogen receptors in the uterus, preventing the fertilised egg from implanting. Different chemistry, different rhythm (weekly, not daily), different main mechanism (implantation, not ovulation).
• EXAMPLE — Do the calendar arithmetic once and the schedule never confuses you again. A woman starts her first strip on day 3 of her cycle: pills run from day 3 through day 23 (21 tablets), the seven-day gap covers days 24 to 30 while menstruation occurs, and the next strip begins on day 31 — the same pattern, cycle after cycle.
• WORKED PATTERN — Pill questions test three slots: composition (progestogens, or progestogen-estrogen), schedule (21 on, 7 off, start within the first five days), mechanism (inhibit ovulation and implantation, alter cervical mucus). Saheli adds three slots: non-steroidal, once a week, CDRI Lucknow. Fill the slots and the correct option fills itself.
• YOUR TURN — A student writes: "Saheli is a steroidal pill taken daily, developed at CDRI Delhi." Count the errors. Answer: three — non-steroidal, not steroidal; once a week, not daily; CDRI is in Lucknow, not Delhi.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The pill-free gap is seven days and menstruation occurs during it — the gap is part of the method, not a break from it. And pills inhibit ovulation; they do not destroy eggs or block tubes. Options describing pills as mini-surgeries or barriers are planted category errors.

Injectables, Implants and the 72-Hour Window
• DEFINITION — The hormones of the pill story also come in long-acting forms: progestogens alone, or in combination with estrogen, can be used by females as injections or as implants placed under the skin. Their mode of action is similar to that of pills — the same hormonal blockade, delivered slowly.
✔ KEY POINT — What changes between a pill, an injection and an implant is not the principle but the effective period: administration of these is far less frequent because their effective periods are much longer. Daily discipline is traded for a single procedure — which is why these forms suit users who cannot maintain a daily routine.
• DEFINITION — Emergency contraception is the chapter's rescue clause: progestogens, or progestogen-estrogen combinations, or IUDs, given within 72 hours of coitus are very effective as emergency contraception. Its stated occasions: avoiding possible pregnancy due to rape or casual unprotected intercourse.
• WHY an IUD appears in the emergency list — inserted within the window, an IUD prevents implantation locally — and keeps working as long-term contraception afterwards. The pill forms act hormonally; the IUD acts locally. Two routes into the same 72-hour deadline.
• EXAMPLE — Build the chapter's five clocks side by side and timing questions become trivial: fertile window, days 10 to 17; emergency contraception, within 72 hours of coitus; pill rhythm, 21 days on, 7 days off; lactational protection, up to 6 months; MTP relative safety, the first 12 weeks. Every number here is one of these five clocks.
• WORKED PATTERN — When a question gives you a time, name the clock before anything else. "Ninety-six hours after coitus" — the 72-hour clock has run out. "Day 12 of the cycle" — inside the fertile window. "Eight months after delivery" — the 6-month clock has expired. Time first, method second.
• YOUR TURN — A woman had unprotected intercourse 48 hours ago and wants to avoid pregnancy. What does the chapter offer? Answer: emergency contraception — progestogens or progestogen-estrogen combinations, or an IUD — effective within 72 hours of coitus; at 48 hours she is inside the window, with a day to spare.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Emergency contraception is a rescue for exceptional situations (rape, casual unprotected intercourse), not a routine method — and the 72 hours count from coitus, not from the missed period. Options moving the deadline have swapped in a different clock.

Surgical Methods — Vasectomy and Tubectomy
• DEFINITION — Surgical (sterilisation) methods are the terminal methods: surgical intervention blocks gamete transport and thereby prevents conception. They are advised for the male or female partner as a permanent, one-way means to prevent pregnancies thereafter.
• DEFINITION — Vasectomy is the male operation: a small part of the vas deferens is removed or tied up through a small incision on the scrotum. The sperms' road out is cut; sperm production and male hormones are untouched — only transport is blocked.
• DEFINITION — Tubectomy is the female operation: a small part of the fallopian tube is removed or tied up, through a small incision in the abdomen or through the vagina. The ovum's road is cut — transport blocked, while ovulation and ovarian hormones continue.
✔ KEY POINT — The surgical pair in one line: vasectomy cuts the vas deferens in the male (through the scrotum); tubectomy cuts the fallopian tube in the female (through the abdomen or vagina). Both are highly effective — and their reversibility is very poor, the words that define the family.
• WHY reversibility fails even though the organs remain — the cut ends scar and seal, and rejoining microscopic tubes years later rarely restores a working passage. Counselling before sterilisation therefore stresses permanence: the decision is treated as final because, in practice, it is.
• EXAMPLE — Hold the contrast that examiners love: a vasectomised man still produces sperms and still ejaculates — the fluid simply carries no sperms, because the vas deferens no longer connects testis to urethra. A tubectomised woman still ovulates — her egg has no road to the uterus. Production continues; transport is zero.
• WORKED PATTERN — Surgical questions reduce to three coordinates: whose body (male or female), which tube (vas deferens or fallopian tube), which opening (scrotum, or abdomen/vagina). Any option with a wrong coordinate — "vasectomy ties the fallopian tube" — collapses instantly. Then attach the family label: highly effective, reversibility very poor.
• YOUR TURN — A couple with three children asks for a method they never have to think about again, accepting that it cannot be undone. Name both options and the structure cut in each. Answer: sterilisation — vasectomy (part of the vas deferens removed or tied, via the scrotum) or tubectomy (part of the fallopian tube removed or tied, via the abdomen or vagina).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Sterilisation is not castration and does not reduce sexual drive, desire or performance — it blocks gamete transport only, satisfying half the ideal checklist while deliberately failing reversibility. And vasectomy is through the scrotum, not the abdomen; the abdominal route is tubectomy's.

MTP — Medical Termination of Pregnancy
• DEFINITION — Medical termination of pregnancy (MTP) is the intentional or voluntary termination of a pregnancy before full term; it is also called induced abortion. It is the legal, medical route — performed under the law, by qualified medical professionals, under stated conditions.
• DEFINITION — The scale is larger than most students guess: nearly 45 to 50 million MTPs are performed in a year all over the world, about one-fifth of the total number of conceived pregnancies in a year. One pregnancy in five ends this way — which makes the safety question a public-health question.
• DEFINITION — The legal frame: the Government of India legalised MTP in 1971, with strict conditions to avoid its misuse. The restrictions matter because the technology is misused for the opposite purpose — the check on indiscriminate and illegal female foeticide is a central purpose of the legal conditions.
✔ KEY POINT — Safety is trimester-stamped: MTPs are considered relatively safe during the first trimester — up to 12 weeks of pregnancy — while second-trimester abortions are much more riskier. "Relatively safe" is the chapter's exact grading — never "risk-free."
• WHY the law wraps MTP in conditions at all — unrestricted, the procedure invites two abuses: termination of female foetuses after sex determination, and illegal abortions by unqualified quacks, which the chapter warns are not only unsafe but could be fatal. Legalisation with strict conditions is the chapter's middle path between prohibition and misuse.
• EXAMPLE — The amendment in outline: the Medical Termination of Pregnancy (Amendment) Act, 2017 was enacted by the Government of India with the intention of reducing the incidence of illegal abortion and the consequent maternal mortality and morbidity. Under it, a pregnancy may be terminated within the first 12 weeks on the opinion of one registered medical practitioner, and between 12 and 20 weeks on the opinion of two.
• WORKED PATTERN — Keep the year's owner straight (1971 legalised MTP; 1951 started family planning) and keep the two week-numbers in their lanes: 12 weeks marks the first-trimester safety boundary and the one-practitioner zone; 20 weeks marks the outer limit the 2017 amendment describes, needing two practitioners between 12 and 20. Most MTP options fail on one swapped number.
• YOUR TURN — A report says a woman at 10 weeks was advised MTP is "relatively safe at this stage." Is the report using the chapter's language correctly, and what changes at 16 weeks? Answer: yes — 10 weeks is inside the first trimester (up to 12 weeks), the relatively safe zone; at 16 weeks she is in the second trimester, where abortions are much more riskier, and two registered medical practitioners must advise it.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — MTP was legalised in 1971, not 1951 — that year belongs to family planning. The worldwide figure is 45 to 50 million per year, one-fifth of conceived pregnancies; options quoting one-fourth or one-third are arithmetic plants. Also: debates about whether to accept or legalise MTP continue on emotional, ethical, religious and social grounds — the chapter reports the debate, it does not settle it.

STIs — Infections That Travel Silently
• DEFINITION — Infections or diseases transmitted through sexual intercourse are collectively called sexually transmitted infections (STI), venereal diseases (VD) or reproductive tract infections (RTI). The pathogens include bacteria, viruses, protozoa and fungi — almost every microbe kingdom appears on this list.
• DEFINITION — The chapter's named cast: gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes, chlamydiasis, genital warts, trichomoniasis, hepatitis-B and, the most discussed worldwide, infection with the human immunodeficiency virus — HIV, leading to AIDS.
✔ KEY POINT — The curability line, in the chapter's own grouping: except for hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV infections, all other STIs are completely curable if detected early and treated properly. Three names on the incurable side; everything else can be completely cured — early detection is the condition.
• WHY these infections travel silently — early symptoms are minor: itching, fluid discharge, slight pain and swelling in the genital region. Worse, infected females may often be asymptomatic — carrying and transmitting while feeling nothing — and so remain undetected for long.
• EXAMPLE — Follow one untreated infection forward and the complication list writes itself: pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), abortions, still births, ectopic pregnancies, infertility, and even cancer of the reproductive tract. The engine is blunt: weak symptoms plus social stigma make people hesitate to consult a doctor — and late detection converts a curable infection into permanent damage.
• DEFINITION — Two members have second routes: hepatitis-B and HIV also spread (a) by sharing injection needles or surgical instruments with an infected person, (b) by transfusion of blood, and (c) from an infected mother to her foetus through the placenta.
• WORKED PATTERN — Triage any STI question with two questions. First: is it on the not-fully-curable list — hepatitis-B, genital herpes, HIV? Second: what kind of pathogen — bacterial (gonorrhoea, syphilis, chlamydiasis), viral (genital herpes, genital warts, hepatitis-B, HIV), or protozoan (trichomoniasis)? The two answers together settle nearly every STI item in the paper.
• YOUR TURN — A clinic record shows a 19-year-old with untreated chlamydiasis discovered two years after infection, now with pelvic inflammatory disease. Which facts of the chapter does her story combine? Answer: incidence is highest at ages 15 to 24; infected females may be asymptomatic and remain undetected; and delayed treatment leads to complications such as PID — though chlamydiasis itself is completely curable when caught early.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Genital warts are caused by a virus (human papilloma virus) and trichomoniasis by a protozoan — options filing warts under bacteria or trichomoniasis under fungi fail the pathogen sort. And "all STIs are curable" is false: the chapter carves out hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV — while syphilis and gonorrhoea ARE completely curable if detected early and treated properly.

Preventing STIs — Three Rules That Actually Work
• DEFINITION — The chapter's prevention prescription has exactly three clauses: (i) avoid sex with unknown partners or multiple partners; (ii) always use condoms during coitus; (iii) in case of doubt, go to a qualified doctor for early detection, and get complete treatment if diagnosed with a disease.
• WHY clause (iii) says "complete treatment" — partial treatment can silence symptoms while the pathogen survives, manufacturing exactly the silent carrier the chapter warns about. Early detection plus a complete course cures; stopping early returns the patient — and their partners — to the transmission chain.
• EXAMPLE — Audit a risky biography against the three clauses. Unknown partners: clause (i) broken. Unprotected coitus: clause (ii) broken. Symptoms ignored for a year: clause (iii) broken. The system is small enough to memorise in a line, and every clause maps to a real failure mode.
• WORKED PATTERN — Prevention questions are clause-matching: "a couple in a mutually faithful relationship, both tested" leans on clause (i); "which contraceptive also prevents STIs" is clause (ii) and answers condom; "discharge noticed, what now" is clause (iii) and answers consult a qualified doctor early and complete the treatment. Identify the clause in the stem; the option follows.
• YOUR TURN — A young man tests positive for syphilis, takes antibiotics for three days, feels fine and stops. Which clause has he violated and what risk remains? Answer: clause (iii) — treatment must be complete; stopping early risks a surviving infection, continued transmission and long-term complications, though syphilis is completely curable when treated properly.
✔ KEY POINT — STIs are a largely preventable class of disease: the three clauses are the whole system, and their target audience is precise — incidences are highest among persons aged 15 to 24, exactly the group awareness programmes most need to reach.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — A pill, an IUD or sterilisation offers zero STI protection — clause (ii) names condoms specifically. Options inventing a fourth rule (vaccination, "being careful afterwards") are decorations. The three clauses, in order, are the whole answer.

Infertility — When a Baby Does Not Arrive
• DEFINITION — Infertility is the inability of a couple to produce children despite unprotected sexual cohabitation. The definition is built on exposure: the couple has had the opportunity for conception and conception has not occurred — a couple using contraception is not infertile, just protected.
• DEFINITION — The causes are a wide field: physical, congenital, diseases, drugs, immunological, or even psychological. Infertility is not one disease but a symptom with many possible addresses — male partner, female partner, or the interaction between them.
✔ KEY POINT — The chapter corrects a social reflex explicitly: in India, blame for a childless couple is often put entirely on the female partner — but quite often, the problem lies with the male partner. Any option assigning infertility to women by default contradicts the text.
• WHY specialised clinics exist — because the cause list is so wide, diagnosis must precede treatment: specialised health care units — infertility clinics — diagnose the actual cause and give corrective treatment for at least some disorders. Test first, treat the cause, escalate only when correction is impossible.
• EXAMPLE — Two clinic stories from the same waiting room. In the first, testing finds a treatable cause — a hormonal disorder — and corrective treatment restores natural conception. In the second, the cause cannot be corrected — and only then does the couple move to the assisted technologies of the next section.
• WORKED PATTERN — The chapter's escalation ladder has three rungs: (1) diagnose at a specialised infertility clinic; (2) apply corrective treatment where the disorder allows it; (3) where such corrections are not possible, help the couple have children through assisted reproductive technologies (ART). Questions asking "what is the next step" are answered by locating the couple on this ladder.
• YOUR TURN — A couple has used condoms for three years of marriage and has no child. Are they infertile by the chapter's definition? Answer: no — infertility means inability to conceive despite unprotected cohabitation; a contracepting couple has never run the test the definition requires.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Infertility is not surgically induced sterility and not a synonym for impotence: it is defined over the couple, after unprotected cohabitation, with causes spanning both partners and six categories (physical, congenital, diseases, drugs, immunological, psychological). Options narrowing it to one partner or one cause are under-inclusive.

ART — Test-Tube Babies and Their Cousins
• DEFINITION — Assisted reproductive technologies (ART) are special techniques that help couples have children when corrections are not possible. Their flagship is in vitro fertilisation (IVF): fertilisation outside the body, in almost similar conditions as those in the body, followed by embryo transfer (ET) — the test tube baby programme.
• DEFINITION — In the test tube baby programme, ova from the wife or a donor female and sperms from the husband or a donor male are collected and induced to form a zygote in the laboratory. What happens next depends on the embryo's age — the dividing line is eight blastomeres.
• DEFINITION — ZIFT — zygote intra fallopian transfer: the zygote, or early embryos with up to 8 blastomeres, go into the fallopian tube. IUT — intra uterine transfer: embryos with more than 8 blastomeres go into the uterus. Same laboratory beginning, two destinations, one deciding number.
• DEFINITION — GIFT — gamete intra fallopian transfer: an ovum collected from a donor is transferred into the fallopian tube of another female who cannot produce one but can provide a suitable environment for fertilisation and development. Note the cargo: a gamete, not an embryo — fertilisation happens inside her body.
• DEFINITION — ICSI — intra cytoplasmic sperm injection: a specialised procedure in which a single sperm is directly injected into the ovum to form an embryo in the laboratory. AI — artificial insemination: semen from the husband or a healthy donor is artificially introduced into the vagina, or into the uterus — the uterus version being intra-uterine insemination, IUI.
✔ KEY POINT — AI and IUI answer a male-side problem: the technique is used when the male partner is unable to inseminate the female, or has very low sperm counts in his ejaculates. Semen placement substitutes for the step the male partner cannot perform; the rest of conception proceeds naturally.
• DEFINITION — One more outline entry belongs to this family: in surrogacy, an embryo formed by IVF is transferred to the uterus of another woman — a surrogate — who carries the pregnancy for a couple unable to carry one. Keep it at outline depth; the chapter's closing emphasis falls not on this route but on adoption.
• WHY the fine print matters — these technologies require extremely high precision handling, expensive instrumentation, and facilities available only in a very few cities — so their benefits reach only a limited number of people. Emotional, religious and social factors are also deterrents to adopting these methods in this country.
• EXAMPLE — Run the site matrix until it is reflex. Zygote at 4 cells: up to 8 blastomeres, so ZIFT — fallopian tube. Embryo at 16 cells: more than 8, so IUT — uterus. Donor ovum, recipient's tubes healthy: GIFT — her fallopian tube, fertilisation in the body. Husband's sperm count extremely low: ICSI in the laboratory, or AI/IUI.
• WORKED PATTERN — Every ART question asks one of three things: what is transferred (zygote, embryo, gamete, sperm, semen), where it lands (tube or uterus), or whose problem it solves (no ovum — GIFT or donor IVF; weak insemination or low count — AI/IUI or ICSI). Name the transferred object first.
• YOUR TURN — After IVF, an embryo is ready at the 32-blastomere stage, and a second couple's embryo is at the 6-blastomere stage. Assign each its transfer. Answer: the 32-blastomere embryo goes to the uterus — IUT; the 6-blastomere embryo goes to the fallopian tube — ZIFT.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — GIFT transfers an ovum (a gamete) and fertilisation occurs inside the body — calling GIFT a form of embryo transfer, or moving it to the uterus, breaks both its defining features. And the chapter ends the technology tour with a human footnote: India has so many orphaned and destitute children who would not survive without care — legal adoption is one of the best methods for couples looking for parenthood.

Matching the Method to the Couple
• DEFINITION — Method selection is the applied end of the chapter: given a couple's situation, choose the family whose properties fit it, scoring every candidate against the ideal-contraceptive checklist.
• WORKED PATTERN — Real method choice is constraint matching. List the couple's constraints — delay or finished family, breastfeeding status, STI protection needed, tolerance for daily routine — then walk the seven families and strike out every family that violates one. What survives is the answer, usually a single family.
• WHY no single method wins — couples differ in goals, health, routine and risk, so a method perfect for one list fails another; the skill is matching, not memorising a champion.
• EXAMPLE — Constraint set one: newly married, first child wanted after three years, both dislike daily routines. Strike surgical (not finished), pills (daily routine), natural (reliability) — the survivor is an IUD: years of cover, no routine, fully reversible on removal. The chapter says it in one line: IUDs are ideal for females who want to delay pregnancy and/or space children.
• EXAMPLE — Constraint set two: family complete with two children, no further pregnancies wanted ever. Now reversibility stops being a virtue; the survivor is the surgical family — vasectomy or tubectomy — highly effective, one procedure, reversibility very poor and no longer a drawback.
• EXAMPLE — Constraint set four: baby is three months old, fully breastfed, couple wants a natural bridge for now. Lactational amenorrhea fits — but the plan must carry its expiry date: at six months, or when feeding is no longer intense, switch before the first method silently lapses.
• YOUR TURN — A couple's constraint list reads: spacing of four years between children, wife forgets daily tablets, no objection to a clinic procedure, wants fertility back on demand. Strike and solve. Answer: pills fall (daily routine), natural falls (reliability), surgical falls (more children wanted) — an IUD such as CuT or LNG-20 survives every constraint and is removed the day they decide.
✔ KEY POINT — Notice what this section just proved: there is no "best contraceptive" in the chapter, only best-for-a-constraint-list. The ideal-method checklist (user-friendly, available, effective, reversible, least side-effects, no interference with the sexual act) is the scoring sheet; each couple's life fills in the scores.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Beware recommending the most effective method when the stem describes a temporary need — effectiveness without reversibility fails a spacing question; reversibility without reliability fails a completed-family one. The stem's constraint words (delay, space, never again, breastfeeding) are the question; the method is just its answer.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Reproductive health (WHO): total well-being in all aspects of reproduction — physical, emotional, behavioural and social; not merely the absence of disease or infertility.
✔ KEY POINT — India was amongst the first countries to launch national reproductive-health action plans: family planning programmes began in 1951; today's umbrella is the Reproductive and Child Health Care (RCH) programme, launched in 1997 — awareness plus facilities and support.
✔ KEY POINT — Population growth is driven by falling death rate, MMR and IMR, with more people in the reproducible age; the named consequence is scarcity of food, shelter and clothing; counter-measures: incentives for small families, marriageable ages 18 (female) and 21 (male).
✔ KEY POINT — An ideal contraceptive is user-friendly, easily available, effective and reversible, with no or least side-effects, and must not interfere with sexual drive, desire or the sexual act. Seven families: natural, barrier, IUDs, pills, injectables, implants, surgical.
✔ KEY POINT — Natural methods avoid the meeting of ovum and sperms: periodic abstinence on days 10 to 17, withdrawal before ejaculation, lactational amenorrhea — no ovulation during intense lactation, up to six months after parturition. Side-effects almost nil; failure chances fairly high.
✔ KEY POINT — Barriers block the gametes physically: condoms (Nirodh the popular male brand) are disposable and self-inserted, and alone among contraceptives also protect against STIs and AIDS; diaphragms, cervical caps and vaults cover the cervix and are reusable; spermicides raise barrier efficiency.
✔ KEY POINT — IUDs sit in the uterus (inserted via the vagina by doctors or expert nurses) and raise phagocytosis of sperms there: non-medicated Lippes loop; copper-releasing CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375 (Cu ions suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity); hormone-releasing Progestasert, LNG-20 (uterus unsuitable for implantation, cervix hostile). Ideal for delaying and spacing.
✔ KEY POINT — Pills are progestogens or progestogen-estrogen combinations, taken daily for 21 days starting within the first five days of the cycle, then a seven-day gap in which menstruation occurs; they inhibit ovulation and implantation and alter cervical mucus. Saheli is the non-steroidal, once-a-week pill from CDRI Lucknow.
✔ KEY POINT — Injectables and implants deliver progestogens (alone or with estrogen) with the same action as pills but much longer effective periods. Emergency contraception — progestogens, combinations, or an IUD within 72 hours of coitus — covers rape and casual unprotected intercourse.
✔ KEY POINT — Surgical methods block gamete transport: vasectomy removes or ties part of the vas deferens through a scrotal incision; tubectomy removes or ties part of the fallopian tube through the abdomen or vagina. Highly effective; reversibility very poor.
✔ KEY POINT — MTP is voluntary termination of pregnancy before full term: 45 to 50 million are performed yearly worldwide (about one-fifth of conceived pregnancies); legalised in India in 1971 under strict conditions checking illegal female foeticide; relatively safe in the first trimester (up to 12 weeks), much riskier in the second.
✔ KEY POINT — STIs (also VD or RTI) include gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes, chlamydiasis, genital warts, trichomoniasis, hepatitis-B and HIV/AIDS; all are completely curable if detected early and treated properly EXCEPT hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV. Prevention: avoid unknown or multiple partners, always use condoms, see a qualified doctor early, complete treatment.
✔ KEY POINT — Infertility is the inability to conceive despite unprotected cohabitation; causes may be physical, congenital, diseases, drugs, immunological or psychological, and the problem quite often lies with the male partner. Clinics diagnose and correct what can be corrected; the rest is ART territory.
✔ KEY POINT — ART: IVF forms the zygote in the laboratory (test tube baby programme); the embryo is then transferred — ZIFT into the fallopian tube at up to 8 blastomeres, IUT into the uterus at more than 8; GIFT places a donor ovum into the tube; ICSI injects one sperm into the ovum; AI/IUI places semen in the vagina or uterus.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Dating family planning to 1971 and MTP legalisation to 1951. Why it is wrong: the two founding years are swapped — family planning began in 1951, MTP was legalised in 1971, and RCH was launched in 1997. Correct principle: 1951 plans families, 1971 legalises MTP, 1997 widens into RCH.
• Mistake 2: Calling Saheli a steroidal daily pill. Why it is wrong: Saheli contains a non-steroidal preparation and is taken once a week; developed at CDRI Lucknow, it acts mainly by blocking estrogen receptors in the uterus so the fertilised egg cannot implant. Correct principle: Saheli is the exception pill — non-steroidal, weekly, Lucknow, implantation-focused.
• Mistake 3: Giving copper IUDs a hormonal mechanism, or LNG-20 a copper one. Why it is wrong: CuT, Cu7 and Multiload 375 release copper ions, which suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity; Progestasert and LNG-20 release hormones, making the uterus unsuitable for implantation and the cervix hostile. Correct principle: the middle name is the mechanism.
• Mistake 4: Replacing "days 10 to 17" with "the days of menstruation" for periodic abstinence. Why it is wrong: abstinence must cover the fertile period when ovulation is expected — days 10 to 17 of the cycle. Correct principle: the method avoids the ovulation window, whose address is 10 to 17.
• Mistake 5: Claiming lactational amenorrhea protects for a year or more, or during partial feeding. Why it is wrong: the method rests on absent ovulation during intense lactation and is effective only up to a maximum of six months following parturition. Correct principle: two conditions, both required — intense (full) breastfeeding, within six months of delivery.
• Mistake 6: Saying pills, IUDs or sterilisation also protect against STIs. Why it is wrong: among contraceptives, only condoms protect against STIs and AIDS; all other methods prevent pregnancy only. Correct principle: infection protection is barrier-only — no latex wall, no anti-STI role.
• Mistake 7: Describing vasectomy as removal of the testes, or claiming it ends sperm production and male hormone action. Why it is wrong: vasectomy removes or ties only a small part of the vas deferens through a scrotal incision; it blocks gamete transport. Correct principle: surgery here cuts roads, not factories — production continues, transport stops.
• Mistake 8: Saying MTP is equally safe at any stage of pregnancy. Why it is wrong: MTPs are relatively safe during the first trimester, up to 12 weeks; second-trimester abortions are much more riskier. Correct principle: safety is trimester-stamped — earlier relatively safe, later much riskier.
• Mistake 9: Listing hepatitis-B, genital herpes or HIV among the completely curable STIs. Why it is wrong: the chapter explicitly excepts these three — all other listed STIs are completely curable if detected early and treated properly. Correct principle: memorise the exception trio first; everything else is curable with early, complete treatment.
• Mistake 10: Sending an embryo with more than 8 blastomeres into the fallopian tube, or calling GIFT an embryo transfer. Why it is wrong: up to 8 blastomeres goes to the fallopian tube (ZIFT); more than 8 goes to the uterus (IUT); and GIFT transfers a gamete — a donor ovum — into the tube, with fertilisation inside the body. Correct principle: count the blastomeres to pick the destination; GIFT's cargo is an egg, not an embryo.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A clinic poster lists four unnamed methods: (a) a device whose ions suppress sperm motility, (b) a tablet taken once a week, (c) a rubber dome placed over the cervix, (d) an operation tying the vas deferens. Name each method or device family.
• Given: Four one-line mechanisms, each unique to one member of the chapter's cast.
• Principle: Every method in this chapter owns exactly one signature mechanism; match the signature, not the story.
• Steps: (a) Ions suppressing sperm motility are copper ions — a copper IUD (CuT, Cu7 or Multiload 375). (b) A once-a-week tablet is Saheli. (c) A rubber dome over the cervix is a barrier — diaphragm (or cervical cap or vault). (d) Tying the vas deferens is vasectomy.
• Answer: (a) Copper IUD. (b) Saheli. (c) Diaphragm family of barriers. (d) Vasectomy.
• Reasoning: Signatures are exclusive: no pill releases ions, no IUD is swallowed weekly, only one operation touches the vas deferens. One signature per method is the fastest identification system in the chapter.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling (a) a hormone-releasing IUD because both are IUDs — inside the family, the released substance (ions versus hormones) is the deciding detail.
• Example 2 — Problem: A couple uses periodic abstinence. Her cycle is regular at 28 days and menstruation begins on 3 March (day 1). On which calendar dates must they abstain, and why those dates?
• Given: Cycle day 1 is 3 March; the fertile period is days 10 to 17 of the cycle.
• Principle: Periodic abstinence avoids coitus during the fertile window when ovulation is expected and fertilisation chances are very high.
• Steps: Day 10 is 12 March (3 March plus 9 days); day 17 is 19 March (plus 16 days). The abstinence interval is 12 to 19 March inclusive.
• Answer: Abstain from 12 March to 19 March — cycle days 10 to 17.
• Reasoning: The only arithmetic is anchoring: day N is the start date plus N minus 1. The window's position, not its width, is what students misplace.
• Mistake to avoid: Counting 10 to 17 days forward from the END of menstruation, or from the date of the question. The window is counted from day 1 of the cycle — the first day of menstruation.
• Example 3 — Problem: A woman begins her first pill strip on day 4 of her menstrual cycle, taking one tablet daily. On which cycle day does she take the last tablet of the strip, which days form the gap, and what happens in the gap?
• Given: Pills are taken daily for 21 days, starting preferably within the first five days of the cycle (day 4 qualifies); then a gap of 7 days follows.
• Principle: The strip is 21 days on, 7 days off, repeated in the same pattern.
• Steps: Tablets run from day 4 through day 24 (day 4 plus 20 more days makes 21). The gap is days 25 to 31, during which menstruation occurs; the next strip starts on day 32.
• Answer: Last tablet on day 24; gap on days 25 to 31, during which she menstruates; new strip from day 32.
• Reasoning: The schedule is pure counting once the two numbers (21 and 7) are fixed; examiners test whether the gap is treated as part of the method rather than as an interruption of it.
• Mistake to avoid: Restarting the strip immediately after the last tablet (no gap) or waiting for menstruation to end before restarting — the pattern is fixed at 21 plus 7 regardless of the bleeding days.
• Example 4 — Problem: A woman cannot produce ova, but her fallopian tubes and uterus are healthy and her husband's sperms are normal. A donor is willing to give ova. Which ART fits, and what exactly is transferred, and where?
• Given: The defect is ovum production only; the site of fertilisation and development inside her body is fully functional; donor ova are available.
• Principle: GIFT — gamete intra fallopian transfer — exists for exactly this anatomy: a female who cannot produce an ovum but can provide a suitable environment for fertilisation and further development.
• Steps: Collect the donor ovum. Transfer it (a gamete, not an embryo) into her fallopian tube. Fertilisation by the husband's sperms occurs inside her body, and development proceeds naturally.
• Answer: GIFT — donor ovum transferred into her fallopian tube.
• Reasoning: Match the missing piece: she lacks the gamete, so the technique supplies it and lets her body do the rest. IVF with donor ova could also work, but the chapter's designed answer for "cannot produce, can provide environment" is GIFT.
• Mistake to avoid: Answering ZIFT — ZIFT transfers a zygote formed in the laboratory; here nothing needs the laboratory, because her tube can host fertilisation itself.
• Example 5 — Problem: An IVF laboratory has two embryos from two couples: embryo P at the 8-blastomere stage and embryo Q at the 32-blastomere stage. State the transfer technique and destination for each.
• Given: The chapter's dividing line is 8 blastomeres: up to 8 goes one way, more than 8 goes the other.
• Principle: ZIFT places zygotes or early embryos (up to 8 blastomeres) into the fallopian tube; IUT places embryos with more than 8 blastomeres into the uterus.
• Steps: Embryo P has exactly 8 blastomeres — "up to 8" includes 8, so P qualifies for the tube. Embryo Q has 32 — more than 8 — so Q goes to the uterus.
• Answer: P — ZIFT, into the fallopian tube. Q — IUT, into the uterus.
• Reasoning: The boundary is the trap: 8 itself belongs to ZIFT, because the phrase is "up to 8 blastomeres." Everything above the line changes both the technique's name and the destination.
• Mistake to avoid: Memorizing only "8 means tube" and then sending an 8-blastomere embryo to the uterus "to be safe" — the inclusive boundary is precisely what the question is testing.
• Example 6 — Problem: Three patients are diagnosed today: one with syphilis, one with genital herpes, one with trichomoniasis. For each, state the pathogen type and whether complete cure is possible with proper treatment.
• Given: The chapter's curability rule excepts hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV from the completely curable set.
• Principle: Classify each disease by pathogen first (bacterium, virus, protozoan), then apply the exception list.
• Steps: Syphilis is bacterial (Treponema), not on the exception list — completely curable if detected early and treated properly. Genital herpes is viral (herpes simplex virus) and IS excepted — not completely curable. Trichomoniasis is protozoan (Trichomonas), not excepted — completely curable.
• Answer: Syphilis — bacterial, curable. Genital herpes — viral, not completely curable. Trichomoniasis — protozoan, curable.
• Reasoning: Two independent sorts decide every STI item: the pathogen sort and the exception sort. A disease can be viral and curable (genital warts) — the sorts must not merge into "viral equals incurable."
• Mistake to avoid: Marking trichomoniasis incurable because it is unfamiliar, or herpes curable because its sores heal — symptom relief is not cure, and the exception list is the authority.
• Example 7 — Problem: A mother has relied on lactational amenorrhea since delivery. Her baby is now seven months old, still breastfed on demand but also eating family foods twice a day. Is she still protected? What should she do?
• Given: The method is effective only up to six months following parturition, and rests on ovulation being absent during intense lactation.
• Principle: Both conditions of the method must hold simultaneously — within six months AND intense (full) lactation.
• Steps: Condition one, time: seven months exceeds the six-month maximum — failed. Condition two, intensity: family foods twice a day mean lactation is no longer intense — weakened. Either failure alone ends protection; here both have failed.
• Answer: She is no longer reliably protected; she should switch now to another method — an IUD or a barrier — rather than extending a lapsed one.
• Reasoning: Natural methods fail silently: nothing announces the return of ovulation, which can precede the first returning period. A method with a printed expiry date must be replaced before the date, not after a scare.
• Mistake to avoid: Extending the method because "she still has no periods" — absence of menstruation is the method's name, not its guarantee; the chapter's guarantee is bounded by six months and intense lactation.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. Reproductive health means total well-being in all aspects of reproduction — physical, emotional, behavioural and social (WHO). Misconception watch: "absence of disease" is the popular shorthand, not the definition; the four dimensions are the answer's skeleton.
• A2. The IUD family. Copper example: CuT (or Cu7, Multiload 375) — copper ions suppress sperm motility and fertilising capacity. Hormone example: LNG-20 (or Progestasert) — uterus unsuitable for implantation, cervix hostile to sperms. Both share the background effect: increased phagocytosis of sperms in the uterus. Misconception watch: do not assign the hormone mechanism to the copper device; the released substance decides the mechanism.
• A3. ZIFT transfers zygotes or early embryos with up to 8 blastomeres into the fallopian tube; IUT transfers embryos with more than 8 blastomeres into the uterus. The boundary is 8, belonging to ZIFT. Misconception watch: the techniques differ in both stage and destination — swapping only one is still wrong.
• A4. Hepatitis-B, genital herpes and HIV are the three not completely curable. The contraceptive that also protects against STIs is the condom; the popular male brand is Nirodh. Misconception watch: every other listed STI is completely curable if treated early and properly — do not add syphilis or gonorrhoea to the incurable side.
• A5. Starting on day 2, twenty-one tablets end on day 22; the gap is seven days (days 23 to 29), during which menstruation occurs. Misconception watch: the gap is part of the prescribed rhythm, not a rest from contraception — and day 2 is a legal start, being within the first five days.
• A6. Vasectomy only removes or ties part of the vas deferens — the road sperms travel. Testes keep producing sperms and hormones, glands keep producing semen fluid, and nothing touches drive or erection; the ejaculate is simply sperm-free because transport, not production, was blocked. Misconception watch: vasectomy is not castration; confusing the tube with the gland is the single most common error in this section.
• A7. At two months with exclusive, intense breastfeeding, both conditions hold — within six months of parturition, ovulation suppressed — so chances of conception are almost nil and no additional method is required yet. The answer changes by six months after delivery (or earlier if feeding stops being intense): adopt another method before the protection lapses. Misconception watch: the guarantee is not "while periods are absent" — it is bounded by the six-month maximum the chapter prints.
• A8. Because infertility has many possible causes — physical, congenital, diseases, drugs, immunological or psychological — spread across both partners; infertility clinics diagnose the actual cause and correct some disorders, reserving ART for cases where correction is impossible. The wife-at-fault assumption is wrong: the problem quite often lies with the male partner. Misconception watch: ART is the ladder's top rung, not its first step — skipping diagnosis wastes the corrective rung and misassigns blame.

• Summary: Reproductive health, in the WHO sense, is total well-being — physical, emotional, behavioural and social — in all aspects of reproduction. India's answer was organisational first: family planning from 1951, widened into RCH in 1997, built on awareness, facilities and professional support, against population growth driven by falling death, maternal and infant mortality rates.
