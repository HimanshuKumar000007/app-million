Introduction — The Molecule That Carries the Instructions
• Every cell in your body began as one fertilised egg, yet a neuron and a liver cell do utterly different jobs. The difference is not the instruction book — both carry the same DNA — but which pages each cell chooses to read. This chapter is the story of that book: how DNA is built, how science proved it carries heredity, how it copies itself, and how its coded sentences become proteins.
• DEFINITION — The genetic material is the molecule that stores hereditary information, copies it faithfully, passes it on, and yet can change just enough, through mutation, to make evolution possible. In nearly all organisms it is DNA; in a few viruses it is RNA.
• EXAMPLE — The numbers alone tell you this is an information molecule, not a building brick: the DNA of one human cell, stretched out, would run about 2.2 metres, yet it fits into a nucleus a few micrometres wide and still issues instructions for roughly thirty thousand proteins.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — it is the most fact-dense chapter in Class 12 biology: exact dimensions (3.4 nm, 0.34 nm, 10 base pairs), named experiments with years (Griffith 1928, Hershey and Chase 1952, Meselson and Stahl 1958), named enzymes, and one operon diagram examiners redraw endlessly. Every fact here is finite and repeatedly asked.
• WORKED PATTERN — Ask four questions of any topic in this chapter: (1) What is the structure? (2) Which experiment proved it? (3) Which enzyme does the work? (4) What number is attached? Structure, experiment, enzyme, number — carry this grid and the chapter stops being a flood.
• YOUR TURN — A virus stores its genes as RNA, not DNA. Does that disprove the claim that DNA is the genetic material? Answer: no — DNA is the genetic material of cellular organisms and most viruses; a few viruses, such as TMV and the QB bacteriophage, use RNA. The rule has named exceptions, and examiners love the exceptions.
★ HIGHLIGHT — One line to carry through the chapter: DNA is a long, stable instruction tape; replication copies it, transcription reads a strip of it into RNA, and translation turns that reading into protein. Everything else is machinery and evidence.

DNA — A Double Helix of Paired Bases
• DEFINITION — DNA is a polymer of deoxyribonucleotides joined into two long chains that wind around each other as a double helix. Each nucleotide carries a nitrogenous base, the pentose sugar deoxyribose and a phosphate group, and neighbouring nucleotides in one chain are linked by 3 prime to 5 prime phosphodiester bonds.
• DEFINITION — The two strands are antiparallel and complementary. Adenine on one strand always pairs with thymine on the other through two hydrogen bonds; guanine pairs with cytosine through three hydrogen bonds. A purine (double-ringed adenine or guanine) always faces a pyrimidine (single-ringed cytosine, thymine or uracil), which is why the helix keeps a uniform diameter of about 2 nanometres.
✔ KEY POINT — The dimensions NCERT asks as a set: the helix is right-handed; one complete turn (the pitch) spans 3.4 nanometres and holds 10 base pairs; the rise between two neighbouring base pairs is 0.34 nanometres; the diameter is about 2 nanometres. Pitch 3.4, pairs 10, rise 0.34, width 2 — quote them together or not at all.
• WHY antiparallel matters — the backbones run in opposite directions, one 5 prime to 3 prime, the other 3 prime to 5 prime. This is not decoration: every copying enzyme works in one direction only, so the antiparallel layout creates the leading and lagging strands you will meet at the replication fork.
• EXAMPLE — Picture a twisted ladder: the ropes are the sugar-phosphate backbones, the rungs are the paired bases, and the rungs come in only two sizes — A-T and G-C — because a purine plus a pyrimidine always adds up to the same width.
• WORKED PATTERN — The bond drill: when a question asks which pair is harder to separate, count hydrogen bonds. Three bonds in G-C beat two in A-T, so G-C-rich DNA needs more heat to melt apart. When a question asks which base can pair with adenine, the answer is locked: thymine in DNA, uracil never appears in DNA at all.
• YOUR TURN — One strand of a DNA segment reads 5 prime-ATGCC-3 prime. Write the complementary strand in its correct orientation. Answer: 3 prime-TACGG-5 prime — A pairs T, T pairs A, G pairs C, C pairs G, and the complement must run antiparallel, 3 prime to 5 prime.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The strands are antiparallel, not parallel, and the bases — not the phosphates — meet in the middle of the helix. The classic swap is the bond count: A-T has two hydrogen bonds, G-C has three. Examiners also flip the pitch: 3.4 nanometres is one full turn holding 10 base pairs, while 0.34 nanometres is the gap between just two neighbouring pairs.

Measuring DNA — Lengths, Turns and Chargaff Arithmetic
• DEFINITION — Chargaff's rule states that in double-stranded DNA the amount of adenine equals the amount of thymine and the amount of guanine equals the amount of cytosine, so total purines equal total pyrimidines. It is not an extra fact to memorise; it is the arithmetic shadow of base pairing itself.
• DEFINITION — DNA length is measured in base pairs, and length follows from the rise: total length equals the number of base pairs multiplied by 0.34 nanometres. This single multiplication powers the two most famous lengths in the chapter.
✔ KEY POINT — Escherichia coli carries 4.6 x 10^6 base pairs, which multiply out to a DNA length of about 1.36 millimetres. A human diploid cell carries 6.6 x 10^9 base pairs, about 2.2 metres of DNA. Bacterium: 4.6 million pairs, 1.36 millimetres. Human: 6.6 billion pairs, 2.2 metres — the pairs of numbers are asked as pairs.
• EXAMPLE — Run the human calculation once and it never leaves you: 6.6 x 10^9 base pairs times 0.34 x 10^-9 metres per pair gives about 2.24 metres. The arithmetic is trivial; what examiners test is whether you know which two numbers to multiply.
• WORKED PATTERN — The Chargaff drill for double-stranded DNA: (1) set the given base's partner equal to it; (2) subtract the pair's total from 100; (3) split the remainder equally between the other two bases. Three moves, no exceptions — and the drill is valid only for double-stranded DNA, never for single-stranded RNA.
• EXAMPLE — A DNA sample is 22 percent guanine. Cytosine must also be 22 percent, so G plus C take 44 percent. The remaining 56 percent is adenine plus thymine, split equally: adenine 28 percent, thymine 28 percent. Ten seconds, one full mark.
• YOUR TURN — In a double-stranded DNA molecule, adenine is 30 percent of the bases. What percentage is cytosine? Answer: thymine equals adenine at 30 percent, leaving 40 percent for guanine plus cytosine — cytosine is 20 percent.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Chargaff's equalities hold between partners only: A equals T and G equals C. They do not say A equals G, and they collapse entirely for single-stranded nucleic acids. Also watch the units trap: E. coli DNA is 1.36 millimetres, not metres — the human figure of 2.2 metres is planted beside it precisely to catch a careless swap.

Packing Two Metres into a Nucleus — The Nucleosome
• DEFINITION — A nucleosome is the basic packing unit of eukaryotic DNA: a stretch of about 200 base pairs of DNA wrapped around a protein core called the histone octamer. Strung along the DNA, nucleosomes look like beads on a string, and that beaded string is chromatin in its loosest form.
• DEFINITION — Histones are small, positively charged basic proteins rich in the amino acids lysine and arginine. The octamer contains two molecules each of four histone types — H2A, H2B, H3 and H4. A fifth histone, H1, sits at the entry and exit of the wrapped DNA and seals the nucleosome, linking one bead to the next.
✔ KEY POINT — The packing ladder: negatively charged DNA wraps the positively charged octamer (opposite charges are why the grip works); nucleosomes coil into a chromatin fibre of about 30 nanometres; at division, further packing with non-histone chromosomal proteins condenses chromatin into visible chromosomes.
• EXAMPLE — Do the division the examiners set: a mammalian cell holds 6.6 x 10^9 base pairs, and each nucleosome accounts for about 200 base pairs, so the nucleus manages roughly 3.3 x 10^7 nucleosomes — about thirty-three million beads on one string, all inside a sphere a few micrometres across.
• WHY two chromatin textures exist — loosely packed chromatin stains light and is called euchromatin; it is transcriptionally active, open for reading. Densely packed chromatin stains dark, is called heterochromatin, and is transcriptionally inactive, locked away. Packing is therefore also a switch: what is wound tight cannot be read.
• WORKED PATTERN — Any packaging question answers itself with three numbers and one charge: 200 base pairs per nucleosome, four histone types in the octamer (H2A, H2B, H3, H4), H1 as the linker seal, and positive histones gripping negative DNA. If an option says histones are acidic or negatively charged, it is wrong before you read the rest.
• YOUR TURN — How many nucleosomes would package a DNA molecule of 4 x 10^5 base pairs? Answer: divide by 200 — about 2 x 10^3, two thousand nucleosomes.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — H1 is not part of the octamer. The octamer is exactly two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4; H1 sits outside as the linker histone. The second trap is charge direction: DNA is negative because of its phosphates, histones are positive — options that reverse either charge are testing whether you know why the wrapping holds at all.

The Search for Genetic Material — Griffith and the Transforming Principle
• DEFINITION — The transforming principle was Frederick Griffith's name, in 1928, for whatever substance passed from dead virulent bacteria to living harmless ones and permanently converted them into killers. He never identified the substance; he proved that something chemical carried a hereditary instruction.
• EXAMPLE — The experiment, in four scenes. Griffith worked with Streptococcus pneumoniae: the smooth S strain, in a polysaccharide coat, kills mice; the rough R strain, coatless, does not. Live S alone: mouse dies. Heat-killed S alone: mouse lives. The famous mixture — heat-killed S plus live R: the mouse died, and living S were recovered from its blood. Dead bacteria had donated the instruction "make a coat and kill" to living ones.
✔ KEY POINT — Griffith's conclusion, stated exactly: some transforming principle transferred from the heat-killed S strain enabled the R strain to synthesise a smooth polysaccharide coat and become virulent. The R bacteria were not merely helped; they were transformed — changed in a heritable way by material from dead cells.
• WHY Griffith alone did not settle the question — his extract was a mixture: the principle could have been protein, RNA or DNA, and most biologists bet on protein, the more versatile molecule. Identifying it needed a second team willing to purify and test for sixteen years.
• WORKED PATTERN — Learn every classic experiment as a four-line card: worker and year, material used, the one manipulation that matters, the conclusion it forces. Griffith 1928: bacteria and mice; dead S mixed with live R; living S recovered; a chemical principle transforms heredity. Cards like this survive any rewording.
• YOUR TURN — In Griffith's mixture, why did the mouse not die simply because a few S bacteria survived the heating? Answer: the control settles it — heat-killed S injected alone left mice alive, so no S had survived; the living S in the mixture must have arisen by transformation of R.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Heat-killed S alone never kills, and live R alone never kills; only the mixture does. Questions that describe mice dying from heat-killed S alone are describing an experiment that never happened. Also, Griffith demonstrated transformation but did NOT identify DNA — crediting him with discovering that DNA is the genetic material steals the next section's experiment.

The Verdict — Avery's Enzymes and the Hershey-Chase Blender
• DEFINITION — Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty (1933 to 1944) purified biochemical fractions from heat-killed S cells and asked which fraction alone could transform R cells. Only the DNA fraction transformed. Their proof was subtractive: digest the extract with proteases and transformation survived; digest it with RNase and it survived; digest it with DNase and transformation was destroyed.
✔ KEY POINT — The enzyme logic is the whole experiment: if removing protein leaves the principle active, the principle is not protein; if removing RNA leaves it active, it is not RNA; if removing DNA kills activity, the transforming principle is DNA. Avery's team concluded that DNA alone, from the S bacteria, caused the transformation — the first direct chemical identification of genetic material.
• DEFINITION — Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase (1952) delivered the verdict most textbooks treat as final, using bacteriophage T2, a virus made of only DNA and protein that infects Escherichia coli. They labelled the two components separately: phosphorus-32 marks DNA, because DNA contains phosphorus but protein does not; sulphur-35 marks protein, because protein contains sulphur but DNA does not.
• EXAMPLE — The blender experiment: radioactive phages infected bacteria; a blender sheared the empty phage coats off the cells; centrifugation separated the heavy bacteria (pellet) from the light coats (supernatant). With phosphorus-32, radioactivity sat inside the bacteria, in the pellet. With sulphur-35, it stayed outside, in the supernatant. DNA entered the cell; protein stayed at the door.
• WHY the labels could not be swapped — the chemistry is forced. Phosphorus is in every nucleotide but almost no protein; sulphur is in some amino acids but never in DNA. Each isotope reports on exactly one molecule — that clean separation made the experiment decisive.
• WORKED PATTERN — The radioactivity drill: ask "which isotope, which location?" Phosphorus-32 with DNA: pellet, inside the cells. Sulphur-35 with protein: supernatant, in the discarded coats. Two isotopes, two locations, one conclusion — DNA is the genetic material.
• YOUR TURN — If Hershey and Chase had labelled the phage DNA with sulphur-35 instead, what would they have found? Answer: nothing — DNA contains no sulphur, so the DNA would have carried no label at all. The isotope must match the chemistry of the molecule it is meant to trace.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — It was DNase, and only DNase, that destroyed Avery's transforming activity — options offering protease or RNase as the killer invert the result. In Hershey-Chase, remember the direction of travel: DNA went in, protein stayed out. A question claiming radioactivity from sulphur-35 appeared inside the bacteria has the experiment exactly backwards.

DNA versus RNA — Which Molecule Makes the Better Heredity Carrier
• DEFINITION — A molecule qualifies as good genetic material only if it passes four tests: it must replicate, so copies exist; it must be chemically and structurally stable, so the message survives; it must allow slow change through mutation, so evolution is possible; and it must express itself, ideally in the form of proteins.
✔ KEY POINT — DNA wins the stability contest on two chemical counts. First, its sugar lacks the reactive 2 prime hydroxyl group that RNA carries on every nucleotide — that free OH makes RNA labile and easily degraded. Second, DNA uses thymine where RNA uses uracil, and thymine confers additional stability. Double-strandedness with complementary strands adds a third shield: damaged information can be repaired using the partner strand as the reference.
• EXAMPLE — The reactivity gap is measurable: RNA in a cell may survive only hours, which suits a temporary message, while DNA persists for the life of the organism — and fragments can be recovered from specimens thousands of years old. A filing cabinet must outlast the memos it stores.
• WHY RNA still beats DNA at one job — RNA can directly code for protein synthesis, while DNA depends on RNA machinery to express itself. RNA is the better messenger and worker; DNA is the better archive. The cell uses each where it is strongest — the division of labour the central dogma describes.
• WORKED PATTERN — When a question asks why DNA, and not RNA, is the genetic material of cells, answer in the NCERT order: stability first (no reactive 2 prime OH, thymine instead of uracil, double-stranded with repair possible), then the expression point made in DNA's favour by delegation — DNA expresses through RNA, so nothing is lost. When the question asks why RNA came first historically, flip to the RNA world: it could both store and catalyse.
• YOUR TURN — A mutation must be rare but not impossible. Which property of the genetic material covers this, and which molecule handles it better in cells? Answer: the criterion of allowing slow change; both DNA and RNA can mutate, but RNA mutates faster, while DNA's slower, repairable change suits stable long-term storage.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Uracil is not "thymine minus stability" in reverse: it is DNA that gained stability by using thymine (5-methyl uracil) instead of uracil. And never claim RNA cannot be genetic material at all — in TMV and the QB bacteriophage, RNA is precisely that. The correct statement is comparative: DNA is the better, more stable carrier, so most organisms use it.

The RNA World — Life Before DNA
• DEFINITION — The RNA world hypothesis holds that early life ran on RNA alone: RNA was both the genetic material and the catalyst. Essential life processes — metabolism, translation and even splicing — are held to have evolved around RNA before DNA took over storage and proteins took over most catalysis.
✔ KEY POINT — The evidence examiners expect is RNA's double talent: it is a nucleic acid that can store information, and some RNA molecules act as enzymes — catalysts called ribozymes. A molecule that can both carry the recipe and do the cooking is the only serious candidate for the first self-sustaining system.
• EXAMPLE — The modern cell still shows fossils of that world: the ribosome does its core chemistry with RNA — in bacteria, the 23S rRNA itself catalyses peptide-bond formation. Protein synthesis, life's most fundamental process, still runs on an RNA engine.
• WHY DNA eventually replaced RNA as the store — DNA is the same information written in a more durable hand: no reactive 2 prime hydroxyl, thymine in place of uracil, and a double strand that permits repair. Once life's archive grew large and long-lived, durability beat versatility, and RNA kept the active, short-lived roles.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sequence the three ages whenever a history question appears: RNA first (stores and catalyses), then proteins take over catalysis (better, more varied catalysts), then DNA takes over storage (better, more stable archive). Each takeover is explained by one property — that property is what the question is really asking for.
• YOUR TURN — Why would a ribozyme be a stronger argument for the RNA world than an ordinary enzyme? Answer: an enzyme is a protein, and proteins cannot be made without the translation machinery — a chicken-and-egg problem. A ribozyme needs no protein to exist, so RNA alone could, in principle, run both storage and catalysis.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The RNA world is about origins, not about the present cell. Do not write that RNA is the genetic material of eukaryotes, and do not claim DNA came first because it is "more advanced" — the NCERT order is RNA first, precisely because it can catalyse as well as store.

Meselson and Stahl — The Experiment That Weighed Replication
• DEFINITION — Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl (1958) proved that DNA replication is semiconservative — each daughter molecule keeps one parental strand and builds one new strand. Their tool was density: DNA made with the heavy isotope nitrogen-15 is measurably heavier than DNA made with ordinary nitrogen-14, and the two can be separated by centrifugation in a caesium chloride density gradient.
• EXAMPLE — The experiment as a timeline. Escherichia coli grew for many generations in medium whose only nitrogen source was heavy 15NH4Cl, so every base in every DNA molecule was heavy. The bacteria were then shifted to ordinary 14N medium, and samples were taken as the cells divided. After one generation (about 20 minutes), all the DNA banded at a hybrid, intermediate density — exactly half-heavy DNA. After two generations (about 40 minutes), there were two bands in equal amounts: hybrid DNA and light DNA.
✔ KEY POINT — Read the bands like verdicts. Conservative replication would have kept one all-heavy band and added an all-light band after generation one — never seen. Dispersive replication would have blurred to ever-lighter single bands. Only semiconservative replication predicts: generation one, one hundred percent hybrid; generation two, fifty percent hybrid and fifty percent light; later generations, the hybrid share halves each round while light DNA piles up.
• WHY caesium chloride was the perfect tool — in a CsCl gradient each molecule settles exactly where the gradient's density equals its own, so heavy, hybrid and light DNA park in three distinct positions. An invisible atomic difference becomes a photographable band: the experiment is a weighing, done with a centrifuge.
• WORKED PATTERN — The generation drill: write the DNA population as fractions. Generation 0: all heavy. Generation 1: all hybrid. Generation 2: half hybrid, half light. Generation 3: one quarter hybrid, three quarters light. Generation 4: one eighth hybrid, seven eighths light. The hybrid fraction is one divided by two raised to (generations minus one); the light fraction is the rest. Every numerical on this experiment is that one line in disguise.
• EXAMPLE — After four generations in light medium, what fraction of DNA molecules are hybrid? Hybrids are the original two parental strands, each still paired with a new light strand — two hybrid molecules out of sixteen total, one eighth. The parental heavy strands are never destroyed and never diluted below those two molecules.
• YOUR TURN — A culture is grown in heavy nitrogen for many generations, then shifted to light medium. At what generation does light-only DNA first appear, and in what proportion? Answer: the second generation (about 40 minutes), making up half the DNA — the other half is still hybrid.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — After the first generation the band is hybrid, not a mixture of heavy and light — that single observation is what killed the conservative model. And mind the clock: 20 minutes is one generation, hybrid only; 40 minutes is two generations, hybrid plus light in equal amounts. A parallel proof in plants, by Taylor and colleagues in 1958 using radioactive thymidine on Vicia faba root tips, showed chromosomes also replicate semiconservatively — a favourite one-line addition.

DNA Replication — The Fork, the Enzymes and the Two Strands
• DEFINITION — Replication is the synthesis of two identical DNA molecules from one parental molecule, each daughter double helix conserving one parental strand — the semiconservative scheme. It begins at defined sites called origins of replication and proceeds through a small opening in the helix, the replication fork, where the two strands separate.
• DEFINITION — The chief copying enzyme is DNA-dependent DNA polymerase, which builds the new strand in the 5 prime to 3 prime direction only, reading its template in the 3 prime to 5 prime direction. Polymerisation is powered by deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates, which serve two purposes at once: they are the substrates, and their hydrolysis supplies the energy for the reaction.
✔ KEY POINT — The speed and accuracy figures NCERT attaches to Escherichia coli: its 4.6 x 10^6 base pairs are fully replicated within 18 minutes, which works out to an average polymerisation rate of about 2000 base pairs per second, sustained with a high degree of accuracy. Any failure of cell division to follow replication would leave the cell with doubled DNA — polyploidy.
• EXAMPLE — The fork's division of labour follows from one restriction: polymerase can build only 5 prime to 3 prime. On the template strand running 3 prime to 5 prime, the new strand grows continuously towards the fork — the leading strand, made in one unbroken run. On the template running 5 prime to 3 prime, the new strand can only grow away from the fork, so it is built in short pieces — the lagging strand, made discontinuously as Okazaki fragments, later joined into one chain by the enzyme DNA ligase.
• WHY the supporting cast is needed — a fork is a traffic problem. Unwinding separates the strands, topoisomerase relieves the twisting strain ahead of the fork, and single-strand binding proteins keep the separated templates from snapping back until they are copied. NCERT asks chiefly for polymerase and ligase by name, but the unwinding roles complete the picture the diagram questions assume.
• WORKED PATTERN — Identify a strand from its description in three checks: (1) direction of synthesis — always 5 prime to 3 prime; (2) continuous or in fragments — continuous is leading, fragments is lagging; (3) template polarity — the leading strand's template reads 3 prime to 5 prime. Examiners permute the three clues; any one of them identifies the strand, and the other two must agree.
• YOUR TURN — A fork moves right, and the upper template runs 5 prime (left) to 3 prime (right). Is the new strand on it continuous or fragmented? Answer: the polymerase builds 5 prime to 3 prime, which here means building away from the fork in short pieces: a lagging strand of Okazaki fragments, sealed later by ligase.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — DNA polymerase cannot start a chain from nothing in the cell's design examiners assume, and it can never polymerise 3 prime to 5 prime — every "polymerase builds both directions" option is false. Second trap: the lagging strand is not built 3 prime to 5 prime either; it is built 5 prime to 3 prime in pieces. Third: DNA ligase joins fragments; it does not synthesise them. Keep each enzyme to its single job.

The Central Dogma — DNA to RNA to Protein
• DEFINITION — The central dogma, proposed by Francis Crick, states the direction of information flow in the cell: DNA replicates itself; DNA is transcribed into RNA; RNA is translated into protein. Information moves from nucleic acid to protein, and in the standard scheme it does not flow backwards from protein.
✔ KEY POINT — The three arrows carry three different processes and three different enzymes. Replication: DNA-dependent DNA polymerase copies DNA into DNA. Transcription: DNA-dependent RNA polymerase copies one DNA strand into RNA. Translation: no polymerase at all — the ribosome reads the RNA and assembles the protein. Matching each arrow to its process and machine is the examiner's favourite grid.
• EXAMPLE — Follow one instruction end to end. A stretch of DNA encoding a digestive enzyme is transcribed in the nucleus into messenger RNA; the mRNA travels to a ribosome; the ribosome translates its codons into amino acids, and the folded chain becomes the working enzyme. One gene, one message, one protein — the dogma is that journey, abstracted.
• WHY the middle step exists at all — DNA is the master archive, kept protected in the nucleus and never risked at the workbench. RNA copies are expendable photocopies: many can be made from one template, used hard at the ribosome and thrown away. The middle arrow is the cell's insurance for its only original.
• WORKED PATTERN — Direction questions collapse once you fix the compass: information flows DNA to RNA to protein. If an option sends information from protein back to DNA, reject it for the standard scheme. The one sanctioned reverse arrow is reverse transcription in some viruses, where RNA is copied back into DNA — an outline exception NCERT acknowledges in viruses, not a rewrite of the rule.
• YOUR TURN — A cell is treated so that all its RNA is destroyed but its DNA is untouched. Can it still make new protein tomorrow? Answer: yes, eventually — the DNA archive survives, so transcription can rebuild the RNA population and translation can resume. Destroy the DNA instead, and no new messages can ever be written.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The central dogma is about information flow, not about location or about which molecule is "most important". Also keep the discoverer straight: the dogma is Crick's proposal; the double helix is Watson and Crick's model; the transforming principle is Griffith's observation. Three credits, three different owners — examiners swap them deliberately.

Transcription — One Strand Copied into RNA
• DEFINITION — Transcription is the synthesis of a single-stranded RNA copy from one strand of DNA. Only the template strand, running 3 prime to 5 prime, is read; the other strand, running 5 prime to 3 prime with essentially the same sequence as the RNA (thymine in place of uracil), is called the coding strand. The stretch of DNA transcribed — promoter, structural gene and terminator — forms one transcription unit.
• DEFINITION — DNA-dependent RNA polymerase does the copying, polymerising ribonucleotides 5 prime to 3 prime. Bacteria manage with a single RNA polymerase that makes all three kinds of RNA; it starts at the promoter with the help of an initiation (sigma) factor and stops at the terminator with a termination (rho) factor.
✔ KEY POINT — Eukaryotes divide the labour among three nuclear polymerases, and the table is asked verbatim: RNA polymerase I transcribes the ribosomal RNAs 28S, 18S and 5.8S. RNA polymerase II transcribes the precursor of messenger RNA, called heterogeneous nuclear RNA or hnRNA. RNA polymerase III transcribes transfer RNA, 5S rRNA and small nuclear RNAs. One, ribosomal; two, messenger precursor; three, transfer and small.
• EXAMPLE — Why only one strand is copied: transcribing both would produce two different RNAs coding for two different proteins from one address — and the complementary RNAs could pair with each other and block translation entirely. One strand, one message per gene.
• WHY the coding strand is named backwards from intuition — it is called coding because its sequence matches the RNA product (with T in place of U), even though the polymerase never reads it. The strand actually read, the template, is the one whose sequence is complementary to the message. Examiners exploit exactly this naming twist.
• WORKED PATTERN — Given a template sequence, write the RNA: read the template 3 prime to 5 prime, lay down complementary RNA bases 5 prime to 3 prime, with A in DNA calling for U in RNA. Template 3 prime-TAC-5 prime gives RNA 5 prime-AUG-3 prime — which is why AUG, the start codon, appears so often in these drills.
• YOUR TURN — The coding strand of a gene reads 5 prime-ATGCCG-3 prime. Write the mRNA sequence. Answer: identical to the coding strand except uracil replaces thymine — 5 prime-AUGCCG-3 prime.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — RNA polymerase needs no primer, unlike DNA polymerase — a subtle contrast examiners slip into enzyme comparisons. Second trap: RNA polymerase II makes hnRNA, the precursor, not finished mRNA; the finished message exists only after processing, which is the next section. Third: in bacteria there is one RNA polymerase for all RNAs; the three-polymerase table belongs to eukaryotes alone.

Processing the Message — Capping, Tailing and Splicing
• DEFINITION — The primary transcript in eukaryotes, hnRNA, contains both exons — the coding sequences that will be expressed — and introns — intervening sequences that will not appear in the finished message. Before the transcript can work as mRNA it must be processed in three ways: splicing, capping and tailing.
• DEFINITION — Splicing removes the introns and joins the exons together in order. Capping attaches an unusual nucleotide, methyl guanosine triphosphate, to the 5 prime end of the RNA. Tailing attaches 200 to 300 adenylate residues, the poly-A tail, to the 3 prime end — added fresh, not copied from the DNA template.
✔ KEY POINT — The three operations in one line each: splicing edits the middle (introns out, exons joined), capping marks the front (methyl guanosine triphosphate at 5 prime), tailing pads the back (200 to 300 adenylates at 3 prime). The fully processed hnRNA is now called mRNA, and only then does it leave the nucleus for translation.
• EXAMPLE — Split genes are also evolutionary evidence: coding sequences interrupted by non-coding ones are an ancient feature, and RNA splicing echoes the old RNA world, where RNA handled its own chemistry.
• WHY bacteria skip all this — in prokaryotes there is no nucleus to cross and the mRNA needs no processing: transcription and translation can be coupled, with ribosomes starting on a message that is still being written. Processing is a eukaryotic necessity created by the nuclear membrane, not a universal step.
• WORKED PATTERN — Splicing arithmetic appears in exams: if a primary transcript has 6 exons and 5 introns, the finished mRNA contains only the 6 exons joined end to end, in their original order. Questions give exon and intron counts or lengths and ask for the mRNA's composition — always subtract every intron, never reorder the exons.
• YOUR TURN — A gene's transcript is 3000 nucleotides long before processing; its introns total 1800 nucleotides. How long is the mature mRNA, ignoring cap and tail? Answer: 1200 nucleotides — splicing removes all 1800 intron nucleotides and joins only the exons.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The cap goes on the 5 prime end and the tail on the 3 prime end; swapping them is the planted error. Second trap: the poly-A tail is not transcribed from a run of thymines in DNA — the adenylates are added enzymatically after transcription. Third: introns are removed from the RNA, not from the DNA; the gene itself keeps them forever.

The Genetic Code — Triplets That Name Amino Acids
• DEFINITION — The genetic code is the dictionary that relates sequences of nucleotides in mRNA to sequences of amino acids in protein. The code word for one amino acid is a codon: a sequence of three nucleotides. George Gamow reasoned that if bases are four and amino acids twenty, the code must be read in triplets — four cubed gives 64 codons, comfortably more than the 61 needed plus stop signals.
• DEFINITION — Of the 64 codons, 61 code for amino acids and three — UAA, UAG and UGA — code for none; they are stop codons that end the chain. AUG does double duty: it codes for methionine and also works as the initiator or start codon that opens nearly every message.
✔ KEY POINT — The four properties NCERT lists, each with its one-line meaning: the code is degenerate — some amino acids are coded by more than one codon; it is unambiguous and specific — one codon codes for one amino acid only; it is read in a contiguous fashion, without punctuation or overlap; and it is nearly universal — the same codons mean the same amino acids from bacteria to humans, with a few exceptions in mitochondria and some protozoans.
• EXAMPLE — Degeneracy and unambiguity side by side: the codons UUU and UUC both name phenylalanine — that is degeneracy, two words for one amino acid. But UUU never names anything else — that is unambiguity, one word never has two meanings. Both properties protect the cell: degeneracy cushions mutations, unambiguity prevents chaos.
• WHY the code-breakers are named in exams — Khorana developed the chemical method to synthesise defined RNA templates; Nirenberg's cell-free system cracked codons by experiment; Ochoa found polynucleotide phosphorylase, which polymerises RNA without a template, letting researchers make random test messages. Three names, three tools.
• WORKED PATTERN — Mutation-effect drill: change one base in a codon and ask what degeneracy predicts. If the new codon still names the same amino acid, the protein is unchanged — a silent outcome degeneracy exists to provide. If it names a different amino acid, one residue changes. If it creates UAA, UAG or UGA, the chain stops early — the most damaging outcome of the three.
• YOUR TURN — An mRNA codon reads UUU. A mutation changes its third base to C. What happens to the protein? Answer: nothing — UUC also codes for phenylalanine. This is degeneracy absorbing the mutation, exactly the cushion the property describes.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Degenerate does not mean ambiguous: several codons may name one amino acid, but one codon never names two. Second trap: AUG is both start codon and the methionine codon — questions that force you to choose one role are wrong in both directions. Third: stop codons code for no amino acid at all; there is no tRNA waiting with an amino acid for UAA, UAG or UGA.

Translation — The Ribosome Builds the Protein
• DEFINITION — Translation is the polymerisation of amino acids into a polypeptide, directed by the sequence of codons in mRNA. The machinery is the ribosome; the adaptor is transfer RNA, which reads a codon with one end and carries the matching amino acid at the other. Francis Crick proposed this adaptor's existence before it was found — hence tRNA's older name, adapter RNA.
• DEFINITION — The first step happens away from the ribosome: charging, or aminoacylation, in which each tRNA is loaded with its correct amino acid, powered by ATP. The loaded tRNA then delivers its amino acid to the ribosome whenever its anticodon — three bases complementary to the codon — pairs with the message.
✔ KEY POINT — The ribosome's working details NCERT asks: it moves along the mRNA codon by codon; it holds two amino acid-carrying tRNAs close enough for a peptide bond to form between their cargo; and in bacteria the bond-making catalyst is itself RNA — the 23S rRNA, a ribozyme. A release factor, not a tRNA, reads the stop codon and ends the process, freeing the completed polypeptide.
• EXAMPLE — Read a message: the ribosome settles at AUG and accepts methionine's tRNA. It steps to the next codon, accepts the next charged tRNA, bonds the two amino acids, and the first tRNA leaves empty. Step, accept, bond, release — repeated hundreds of times per protein.
• WHY untranslated regions matter — the mRNA carries sequences that are never translated: the UTRs, before the start codon at the 5 prime end and after the stop codon at the 3 prime end. They are required for efficient translation — handling instructions that never become protein.
• WORKED PATTERN — Direction drill for any translation figure: the ribosome reads the mRNA 5 prime to 3 prime; the polypeptide grows from its first amino acid (carried in by the AUG-reading tRNA) towards its last; the tRNA anticodon is antiparallel and complementary to the codon. Given any two of codon, anticodon and amino acid, the third follows from the code table and pairing rules.
• YOUR TURN — An mRNA codon is 5 prime-UUC-3 prime. Write the anticodon of the tRNA that reads it, in 3 prime to 5 prime orientation, and name the amino acid delivered. Answer: anticodon 3 prime-AAG-5 prime; the codon UUC names phenylalanine.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Charging happens before the ribosome: an uncharged tRNA cannot deliver anything. Second trap: the peptide bond catalyst in bacteria is the 23S rRNA, a ribozyme — not a protein enzyme — a point examiners link back to the RNA world. Third: the stop codon is read by a release factor; options that send in a special stop-carrying tRNA are invented.

The Lac Operon — Switching Genes with Milk Sugar
• DEFINITION — An operon is a cluster of genes under one switch: a regulator gene, a promoter, an operator and the structural genes they control, transcribed together as one unit. The lac operon of Escherichia coli, worked out by Francois Jacob and Jacques Monod, is the model system for gene regulation in prokaryotes.
• DEFINITION — The cast, in order along the DNA: the regulator gene i, which codes for the repressor protein; the promoter p, where RNA polymerase binds; the operator o, the switch segment the repressor grips; and three structural genes — z, coding for beta-galactosidase, which splits lactose into glucose and galactose; y, coding for permease, which imports lactose into the cell; and a, coding for transacetylase.
✔ KEY POINT — The two states, framed as the examiner frames them. No lactose: the repressor binds the operator, RNA polymerase is blocked, the operon is OFF. Lactose present: lactose (through its isomer allolactose) is the inducer — it binds and inactivates the repressor; the operator is freed, polymerase transcribes z, y and a, and the cell gains the enzymes to use lactose. Regulation by the repressor is negative regulation, and because the inducer switches the operon on, the system is called inducible.
• EXAMPLE — The economy of it: beta-galactosidase is useless in a glucose-only world, so the cell refuses to build it until lactose appears. The inducer is the substrate itself — supply creates its own demand, and when lactose runs out, fresh repressor (made continuously by the i gene) rebinds the operator and shuts the factory.
• WHY glucose still outranks lactose — when both sugars are present, the cell prefers glucose and keeps lactose use low until glucose is exhausted. NCERT's core logic is the repressor-operator switch; the glucose preference is common sense on top — never burn the second-choice fuel while the first choice lasts.
• WORKED PATTERN — Operon questions always reduce to two checks: (1) Is the repressor active and on the operator? That needs: no inducer present. (2) Is the inducer present to pull it off? Then the operon is ON. Mutations follow the same logic: a broken repressor or a broken operator that cannot bind repressor leaves the operon permanently ON; a repressor that cannot bind inducer leaves it permanently OFF.
• YOUR TURN — A mutant bacterium has a defective i gene and makes no functional repressor. Are its lac enzymes produced when lactose is absent? Answer: yes — with no repressor, the operator is never blocked, so the operon stays on constitutively, wasting resources even without lactose.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The repressor is the product of the i gene, and it is made all the time — the switch is thrown by the inducer inactivating the repressor, not by the cell starting or stopping repressor production. Second trap: permease (gene y) imports lactose; beta-galactosidase (gene z) splits it. Swapping z and y functions is the oldest error in this topic. Third: the operator is a DNA segment, not a gene product — nothing is synthesised from o.

The Human Genome Project — Reading Three Billion Letters
• DEFINITION — The Human Genome Project was the international effort, launched in 1990 and completed in 2003, to determine the complete sequence of the roughly 3 x 10^9 base pairs of human DNA and to identify the genes they contain. The total DNA of a cell was cut into overlapping fragments, cloned in bacteria and yeast using BAC and YAC vectors, and read piece by piece — sequencing a library by shredding it.
✔ KEY POINT — The findings NCERT quotes: the genome holds 3164.7 million bases; the average gene is about 3000 bases; total genes number about 30,000 — far fewer than the 80,000 to 140,000 once estimated; over 50 percent of genes have unknown functions; less than 2 percent of the genome codes for proteins; chromosome 1 carries the most genes, 2968, and Y the fewest, 231.
• EXAMPLE — The surprise in those numbers: nearly 98 percent of the genome does not code for protein at all. Much of it is repetitive sequence — stretches repeated hundreds or thousands of times, with no direct coding function, though they carry evidence about chromosome structure and evolution. The genome is mostly not genes.
• WHY SNPs matter beyond the sequence — at about 1.4 million locations, single bases differ between people; these single nucleotide polymorphisms, pronounced snips, are landmarks that let researchers trace disease genes and human history. The sequence was the map; the SNPs are the signposts pinned into it.
• WORKED PATTERN — Two sequencing philosophies appear in options and must not be merged: one approach first identifies all the genes that are expressed as RNA — the expressed sequence tags, or ESTs; the other, called sequence annotation, blindly sequences everything, coding and non-coding, and assigns functions afterwards. The project used both; questions ask which description belongs to which name.
• YOUR TURN — If less than 2 percent of the genome codes for protein, roughly how many base pairs of a 3 x 10^9 base-pair genome actually code? Answer: under 2 percent of 3 billion — fewer than about 6 x 10^7 base pairs, sixty million, out of three thousand million.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — 30,000 genes is the NCERT figure — not 100,000, which was the outdated estimate the project itself corrected. Second trap: chromosome 1 has the most genes (2968) and Y the fewest (231) — examiners swap the chromosomes. Third: the project ended in 2003, not 2000 or 2005; it began in 1990, a 13-year span.

DNA Fingerprinting — Identity Written in Repeats
• DEFINITION — DNA fingerprinting, developed by Alec Jeffreys, identifies individuals by the variable regions of their DNA. Scattered through the genome are satellite DNA sequences — short stretches repeated in tandem, again and again, whose repeat counts vary enormously from person to person. These are the variable number tandem repeats, or VNTRs, and no two people (except identical twins) share the same set of lengths.
✔ KEY POINT — The procedure in its NCERT sequence: isolate the DNA; digest it with restriction endonucleases; separate the fragments by size using gel electrophoresis; blot the separated fragments onto a membrane (Southern blotting); hybridise them with a labelled VNTR probe; and detect the pattern by autoradiography. The result is a ladder of bands whose positions are as individual as a fingerprint.
• EXAMPLE — Why the bands differ: a VNTR block may repeat 20 times in your DNA and 45 times in another person's, so the restriction fragment carrying it is short in you and long in them, and the two fragments travel to different positions on the gel. Multiply that across many VNTR loci and the combined pattern identifies one person out of billions.
• WHY the technique is so trusted — VNTRs are inherited, half from each parent, so the same test settles paternity; they do not change with age or tissue, so blood, hair or semen give the same profile; and with PCR amplification, even a trace sample yields enough DNA. Forensics, paternity disputes and tracking population diversity are its standing applications.
• WORKED PATTERN — Reading a fingerprint question: a child's band must come from one parent or the other. Any band in the child that matches neither the mother nor the tested man excludes that man as the father; a true father's bands fill exactly the gaps the mother leaves. Exclusion is absolute — one unexplained band ends the case.
• YOUR TURN — Two suspects leave profiles at a crime scene locus: the scene fragment runs to the same position as suspect A's fragment, not suspect B's. What can you conclude, and what can you not? Answer: B is excluded at this locus; A is not proven guilty by one locus alone — identification rests on matches across many loci. Precision about what a test cannot say earns as many marks as the match itself.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The probe in DNA fingerprinting is labelled and made from VNTR sequence — the bands detected are satellite DNA fragments, not genes. Second trap: satellite DNA shows high polymorphism, but it is the VNTR class (minisatellites) that fingerprinting uses. Third: identical twins share a profile — the stock exception every fingerprinting question keeps in reserve.

✔ KEY POINT — DNA dimensions: right-handed double helix of diameter about 2 nanometres; one turn (pitch) is 3.4 nanometres and carries 10 base pairs; rise per base pair is 0.34 nanometres; adenine pairs thymine with two hydrogen bonds, guanine pairs cytosine with three, and the strands are antiparallel
✔ KEY POINT — DNA lengths: Escherichia coli holds 4.6 x 10^6 base pairs, about 1.36 millimetres of DNA; a human diploid cell holds 6.6 x 10^9 base pairs, about 2.2 metres; Chargaff's rule for double-stranded DNA: A equals T, G equals C, purines equal pyrimidines
✔ KEY POINT — A nucleosome is about 200 base pairs of negatively charged DNA wrapped around a positively charged histone octamer of two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4, sealed by linker histone H1; loosely packed active chromatin is euchromatin, densely packed inactive chromatin is heterochromatin
✔ KEY POINT — The proof chain for DNA as genetic material: Griffith (1928) found the transforming principle in Streptococcus pneumoniae; Avery, MacLeod and McCarty showed only DNase destroyed it; Hershey and Chase (1952) showed phosphorus-32 labelled DNA enters bacteria while sulphur-35 labelled protein stays outside
✔ KEY POINT — DNA beats RNA as genetic material because it lacks the reactive 2 prime hydroxyl, uses thymine instead of uracil, and is double-stranded so damage can be repaired; RNA came first — the RNA world — because it can both store information and catalyse as ribozymes
✔ KEY POINT — Meselson and Stahl (1958), using nitrogen-15 and nitrogen-14 with caesium chloride gradient centrifugation, proved semiconservative replication: after one generation in light medium all DNA is hybrid density; after two generations hybrid and light DNA appear in equal amounts
✔ KEY POINT — Replication: DNA polymerase builds only 5 prime to 3 prime; the leading strand is continuous, the lagging strand is made as Okazaki fragments joined by DNA ligase; Escherichia coli copies 4.6 x 10^6 base pairs in 18 minutes, about 2000 base pairs per second, powered and fed by deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates
✔ KEY POINT — Central dogma (Francis Crick): DNA replicates to DNA, DNA is transcribed to RNA, RNA is translated to protein; transcription reads the template strand 3 prime to 5 prime and produces RNA matching the coding strand with uracil in place of thymine
✔ KEY POINT — Eukaryotic RNA polymerases: polymerase I makes 28S, 18S and 5.8S rRNA; polymerase II makes hnRNA, the precursor of mRNA; polymerase III makes tRNA, 5S rRNA and snRNA; bacteria use a single RNA polymerase for all their RNA
✔ KEY POINT — Processing of hnRNA: splicing removes introns and joins exons; capping adds methyl guanosine triphosphate at the 5 prime end; tailing adds 200 to 300 adenylate residues at the 3 prime end; only the fully processed molecule is mRNA
✔ KEY POINT — The genetic code is triplet, degenerate, unambiguous, contiguous and nearly universal: 61 codons name amino acids, UAA, UAG and UGA are stops, and AUG both starts translation and codes methionine; in translation the bacterial peptide-bond catalyst is the 23S rRNA ribozyme, and untranslated regions flank the coding span
✔ KEY POINT — Lac operon (Jacob and Monod): i makes the repressor, p is the promoter, o is the operator; z codes beta-galactosidase, y codes permease, a codes transacetylase; lactose as inducer inactivates the repressor — negative, inducible regulation; human genome: about 30,000 genes, chromosome 1 most (2968), Y fewest (231); DNA fingerprinting (Alec Jeffreys) reads VNTR lengths of satellite DNA

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Crediting Griffith with proving DNA is the genetic material. Why it is wrong: Griffith showed only that a transforming principle existed; he never isolated or identified it. Correct principle: Griffith discovered transformation; Avery's group identified the principle chemically as DNA; Hershey and Chase gave the widely accepted final proof.
• Mistake 2: Saying protease or RNase destroyed the transforming activity in Avery's experiment. Why it is wrong: transformation survived both digestions and died only when DNA was digested. Correct principle: DNase alone abolished transformation — that single sensitivity is the entire identification.
• Mistake 3: Placing sulphur-35 radioactivity inside the bacteria in the Hershey-Chase experiment. Why it is wrong: sulphur labels protein, and the protein coats were blended off and stayed in the supernatant; only the phosphorus-32 of DNA entered the cells and appeared in the pellet. Correct principle: 32P in, 35S out — DNA enters, protein stays at the surface.
• Mistake 4: Claiming that after one generation in the Meselson-Stahl experiment the DNA was a mixture of heavy and light molecules. Why it is wrong: generation one gives a single hybrid band of intermediate density, and that one band refutes the conservative model. Correct principle: one generation, all hybrid; two generations, half hybrid and half light.
• Mistake 5: Writing that the lagging strand is synthesised in the 3 prime to 5 prime direction. Why it is wrong: DNA polymerase polymerises only 5 prime to 3 prime on every strand; the lagging strand achieves apparent backward growth by being built in short 5 prime to 3 prime Okazaki fragments. Correct principle: direction of synthesis never changes; continuity does.
• Mistake 6: Assigning the finished mRNA to RNA polymerase II as its direct product. Why it is wrong: polymerase II produces hnRNA, the primary transcript; mRNA exists only after splicing, capping and tailing. Correct principle: polymerase II makes the precursor; processing makes the messenger.
• Mistake 7: Putting the cap on the 3 prime end and the poly-A tail on the 5 prime end. Why it is wrong: the methyl guanosine cap belongs to the 5 prime end and the 200 to 300 adenylate tail to the 3 prime end. Correct principle: cap at five, tail at three — front marked, back padded.
• Mistake 8: Saying the genetic code is ambiguous because several codons can name one amino acid. Why it is wrong: that property is degeneracy; the code is simultaneously unambiguous because a single codon never names two different amino acids. Correct principle: many codons per amino acid is allowed; many amino acids per codon never happens.
• Mistake 9: Swapping the lac operon genes z and y, or calling the operator a gene product. Why it is wrong: z codes for beta-galactosidase, which splits lactose, while y codes for permease, which imports it; the operator is a segment of DNA that the repressor binds, not something translated. Correct principle: z splits, y imports, a acetylates; i represses, o is bound, p is started from.
• Mistake 10: Quoting 100,000 as the human gene count or giving chromosome Y the most genes. Why it is wrong: the Human Genome Project settled the count near 30,000, and the gene-density extremes run the other way. Correct principle: about 30,000 genes; chromosome 1 carries the most with 2968; Y carries the fewest with 231.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A double-stranded DNA molecule contains 26 percent adenine. Calculate the percentages of thymine, guanine and cytosine.
• Given: Double-stranded DNA, so base pairing forces A equals T and G equals C; all four percentages sum to 100.
• Principle: Partner bases are present in equal amounts — Chargaff's rule — so one given percentage determines the whole composition.
• Steps: Adenine 26 percent forces thymine to 26 percent; the pair takes 52 percent. The remaining 48 percent is guanine plus cytosine, split equally: guanine 24 percent, cytosine 24 percent.
• Answer: Thymine 26 percent, guanine 24 percent, cytosine 24 percent.
• Reasoning: The drill has three moves — match the partner, subtract the pair, halve the rest. It works for any starting base and any starting number, which is why examiners return to it every year.
• Mistake to avoid: Giving guanine 26 percent as well. Equality runs within pairs only; guanine's partner is cytosine, not adenine.
• Example 2 — Problem: How many complete turns does a DNA molecule of 3400 base pairs contain, and what is its length?
• Given: 10 base pairs per turn; rise of 0.34 nanometres per base pair; 3400 base pairs in total.
• Principle: Turns equal base pairs divided by pairs per turn; length equals base pairs times rise per pair.
• Steps: Turns: 3400 divided by 10 equals 340 turns. Length: 3400 times 0.34 nanometres equals 1156 nanometres, about 1.16 micrometres.
• Answer: 340 complete turns; length about 1156 nanometres.
• Reasoning: Both answers come from the same dimension trio (3.4 nm, 10 bp, 0.34 nm). Any DNA measurement question is one of these two multiplications wearing a costume.
• Mistake to avoid: Multiplying 3400 by 3.4 — that uses the whole pitch as if it were the rise of one base pair and inflates the length tenfold.
• Example 3 — Problem: DNA from bacteria grown for generations in heavy nitrogen medium bands at one low position in a CsCl gradient. After transfer to light medium, state the band pattern after one and after three generations, assuming semiconservative replication.
• Given: Generation 0 DNA is all heavy; each generation in light medium doubles the molecules.
• Principle: Parental heavy strands are conserved, each in a hybrid molecule; new strands are all light.
• Steps: Generation 1: every molecule pairs one heavy strand with one new light strand — a single hybrid band. Generation 2: half hybrid, half light. Generation 3: the two hybrid molecules persist among eight total — one quarter hybrid, three quarters light.
• Answer: After one generation, one hybrid band. After three generations, two bands: hybrid one quarter, light three quarters.
• Reasoning: The hybrid fraction follows one divided by two raised to (generations minus one); the heavy band never returns because no new heavy strands are ever built.
• Mistake to avoid: Predicting an all-light single band by generation three. The two parental heavy strands survive indefinitely as hybrid molecules; hybrid DNA declines but never vanishes.
• Example 4 — Problem: A template strand of DNA reads 3 prime-TACGGAATT-5 prime. Write the mRNA transcribed from it, mark its direction, and identify the first codon and the amino acid it names.
• Given: Transcription reads the template 3 prime to 5 prime and builds RNA 5 prime to 3 prime with complementary bases, U replacing T.
• Principle: Pairing for transcription: template A calls U, T calls A, C calls G, G calls C.
• Steps: Base by base — T pairs A, A pairs U, C pairs G, G pairs C, G pairs C, A pairs U, A pairs U, T pairs A, T pairs A. The RNA reads 5 prime-AUGCCUUAA-3 prime. Its first codon is AUG.
• Answer: mRNA is 5 prime-AUGCCUUAA-3 prime; the first codon is AUG, which is the start codon and codes for methionine.
• Reasoning: The drill combines the three fixed points of transcription: which strand, which direction, which base swap (U for T in the product). Set those before touching the sequence and the transcription writes itself.
• Mistake to avoid: Copying the template sequence directly, or writing the RNA 3 prime to 5 prime. The product is complementary, antiparallel and contains uracil — all three changes, every time.
• Example 5 — Problem: A eukaryotic gene's primary transcript contains 5 exons totalling 1500 nucleotides and 4 introns totalling 2500 nucleotides. How long is the mature mRNA, and how many codons does its translated region contain at most?
• Given: hnRNA equals exons plus introns; splicing removes all introns; codons are triplets within the translated region.
• Principle: Mature mRNA length equals the sum of exon lengths; the number of codons is at most the mRNA length divided by three, since UTRs are untranslated.
• Steps: Mature mRNA: 1500 nucleotides. Codons: 1500 divided by 3 equals 500 codons at the absolute most; in practice fewer, because the untranslated regions at both ends consume part of the length and one codon is a stop.
• Answer: Mature mRNA is 1500 nucleotides; at most 500 codons, realistically fewer once UTRs and the stop codon are counted.
• Reasoning: The question tests two sections at once — splicing (what survives) and the code (how survivors are read). The honest upper bound, then the correction, is the pattern strong answers follow.
• Mistake to avoid: Including introns in the codon count, or forgetting that the stop codon names no amino acid when converting codons into protein length.
• Example 6 — Problem: An Escherichia coli culture is offered lactose but no glucose. Describe the state of the lac repressor, the operator and the three structural genes, and what changes the moment lactose is exhausted.
• Given: The i gene continuously produces repressor; lactose acts as inducer; the structural genes are z, y and a.
• Principle: Inducer binds the repressor and inactivates it, freeing the operator for RNA polymerase; without inducer, the repressor rebinds the operator.
• Steps: With lactose present, allolactose (the inducer form of lactose) binds the repressor, the repressor leaves the operator, polymerase transcribes z, y and a, and beta-galactosidase, permease and transacetylase are made. When lactose runs out, no inducer remains, newly made repressor binds the operator again, and transcription stops.
• Answer: Lactose present means repressor off, operon on, three enzymes produced; lactose exhausted means repressor back on the operator, operon off.
• Reasoning: Every operon scenario is the same toggle: the variable is never repressor production (always on) but repressor activity (controlled by the inducer). Locate the inducer and the state follows.
• Mistake to avoid: Saying lactose switches off the i gene. The i gene is never switched off; its product is switched off, after it is made, by binding the inducer.
• Example 7 — Problem: A child's DNA fingerprint is made at one VNTR locus. The mother shows bands at positions corresponding to fragments of 4 and 6 units; the child shows bands at 6 and 9 units; the tested man shows bands at 5 and 9 units. Is the tested man excluded as the father? What if his bands were 5 and 7?
• Given: A child inherits one allele at each locus from each parent, so each of the child's bands must match a band of the mother or of the father.
• Principle: The child's band that the mother cannot supply must come from the biological father; if the tested man lacks it, he is excluded.
• Steps: The child's 6-unit band matches the mother's 6. The child's 9-unit band matches neither maternal band, so it must be paternal. The tested man carries a 9-unit band — consistent with paternity at this locus. If his bands were 5 and 7 instead, he would carry no 9, and he would be excluded outright.
• Answer: With bands 5 and 9, the man is not excluded — his 9-unit band can account for the child's second band. With bands 5 and 7, he is excluded as the father.
• Reasoning: Fingerprinting logic is accounting: every band in the child needs a parent to pay for it. One unpayable band excludes; full payment across many loci identifies.
• Mistake to avoid: Claiming one matching locus proves paternity. A single locus can only exclude or stay consistent; proof is built from many loci together.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. Thymine 20 percent means adenine is 20 percent; the pair takes 40 percent, leaving 60 percent for guanine plus cytosine, so cytosine is 30 percent. The licence is Chargaff's rule, which itself follows from complementary base pairing in double-stranded DNA. Misconception watch: the calculation is invalid for single-stranded RNA — the rule needs two paired strands.
• A2. Phosphorus-32, labelling DNA, was found inside the bacteria (the pellet). Sulphur-35, labelling the protein coat, stayed outside in the supernatant after blending. Misconception watch: phosphorus labels DNA because DNA contains phosphorus and protein essentially does not; sulphur labels protein because DNA contains no sulphur at all.
• A3. Two bands in equal amounts: hybrid DNA of intermediate density and light DNA. Misconception watch: no heavy band reappears — after two generations the parental strands sit inside just two hybrid molecules among four, and heavy-only molecules no longer exist.
• A4. The lagging strand, built as Okazaki fragments, sealed by DNA ligase. Each fragment was synthesised 5 prime to 3 prime, like every new DNA strand, because DNA polymerase polymerises in that direction only. Misconception watch: the lagging strand is not synthesised 3 prime to 5 prime overall — it only grows in short 5 prime to 3 prime bursts away from the fork.
• A5. (a) RNA polymerase II makes hnRNA, the mRNA precursor. (b) RNA polymerase III makes tRNA and 5S rRNA (and snRNAs). (c) RNA polymerase I makes 28S and 18S rRNA (and 5.8S). Misconception watch: 5S rRNA belongs to polymerase III, not polymerase I — the one misfit in the ribosomal family and a favourite trap.
• A6. The cap goes on the 5 prime end; the tail of adenylates goes on the 3 prime end. After splicing as well, the processed molecule is mRNA. Misconception watch: the poly-A tail is added enzymatically after transcription; it is not copied from a matching run of bases in the DNA.
• A7. UUU and UUC both code for phenylalanine, so the protein is unchanged — the code's degeneracy absorbed the mutation. UAA would be a stop codon, ending the chain early. Misconception watch: degeneracy is not ambiguity; UUU's silence comes from two codons sharing one meaning, not from one codon having two meanings.
• A8. Gene z produces beta-galactosidase, which splits lactose into glucose and galactose; gene y produces permease, which brings lactose into the cell. The operon shuts off because the i gene's repressor is made continuously, and once lactose is gone there is no inducer to inactivate it, so it rebinds the operator. Misconception watch: the i gene is never switched off — it is the repressor protein that is switched off, temporarily, by the inducer.

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel, complementary strands — pitch 3.4 nanometres, 10 base pairs per turn, 0.34 nanometre rise, A-T held by two hydrogen bonds and G-C by three. Chargaff's equalities turn one base percentage into the whole composition, and the same dimensions fix the great lengths: E. coli 1.36 millimetres from 4.6 x 10^6 base pairs, human 2.2 metres from 6.6 x 10^9. In eukaryotes the tape is packed as nucleosomes, about 200 base pairs around a histone octamer, loosening into active euchromatin or locking into dark heterochromatin.
• Summary: The evidence chain is three experiments deep: Griffith's transforming principle showed heredity could pass chemically between cells; Avery's enzyme digestions identified that principle as DNA; Hershey and Chase watched labelled DNA — and only DNA — enter the infected cell. DNA earned the archive role through stability: no reactive 2 prime hydroxyl, thymine in place of uracil, and a repairable double strand. RNA, the older molecule of the RNA world, kept the catalytic and messenger jobs, and Meselson and Stahl proved the archive is copied semiconservatively.
• Summary: Expression runs on Crick's central dogma. Replication copies the whole tape at about 2000 base pairs per second, leading strand continuous and lagging strand in ligase-sealed fragments. Transcription reads one template strand into RNA — one polymerase in bacteria, three specialised ones in eukaryotes — and eukaryotic transcripts are spliced, capped and tailed into mRNA. The triplet code, degenerate yet unambiguous, is read by ribosomes with tRNA adaptors, while regulation, modelled by the lac operon's repressor and inducer, decides which pages are read at all. The Human Genome Project read the whole book — about 30,000 genes, under 2 percent coding — and DNA fingerprinting learned to tell one reader from another by VNTR lengths.
