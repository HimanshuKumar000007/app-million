Introduction — The River Inside You
• Pump about five litres of blood through roughly a hundred thousand kilometres of vessels, seventy-odd times a minute, for an entire lifetime, without a single pause for repairs — that is the job this chapter describes. Blood is the transport river, the heart is its pump, and the vessels are its channels. Almost every number in this chapter is a classic exam trap, so we will learn each one chained to its reason.
• DEFINITION — Body fluids are the liquid media of the body: blood inside the vessels, and lymph, the fluid that drains from the tissues. Circulation is the continuous movement of these fluids, carrying oxygen, nutrients, hormones and wastes between the lungs, the gut and every living cell.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — the IAT and NEET-style papers recycle a small, fixed cast: the blood counts (5-5.5 million RBCs, 6000-8000 WBCs, 150000-350000 platelets per mm3), the ABO antigen-antibody table, the clotting cascade, the 0.4-0.1-0.3 split of the cardiac cycle, and the three ECG waves. Learn the cast once, with reasons, and the questions become free marks.
• EXAMPLE — Hold two facts side by side and the chapter already makes sense: your heart beats about 72 times a minute and pushes about 70 mL each beat, so it moves your entire blood volume — all five litres — every single minute. The river completes a full round faster than you can boil an egg.
• WORKED PATTERN — The master key for this chapter is to ask of every structure: which blood does it carry, oxygenated or deoxygenated, and in which direction? Right side of the heart: deoxygenated, body to lungs. Left side: oxygenated, lungs to body. That one question answers valve questions, vessel questions and circulation questions alike.
• YOUR TURN — A vessel carries oxygenated blood into the left atrium. Vein or artery, and which vessel? Answer: a vein — a pulmonary vein; vessels are named by direction (veins bring blood to the heart, arteries take it away), not by the oxygen content of the blood they carry.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The whole chapter in one breath: blood is plasma plus three cell teams; groups are decided by antigens on RBCs and antibodies in plasma; clotting is a three-step relay ending in fibrin; the heart is two pumps in one box, fired by its own pacemaker in a 0.8 second cycle, and double circulation keeps the two bloods forever apart.

Blood — Plasma and Formed Elements in One View
• DEFINITION — Blood is a specialised connective tissue: a fluid matrix called plasma, in which cellular components — the formed elements — float. Plasma forms about 55 percent of blood volume and the formed elements about 45 percent. Spin a tube of blood in a centrifuge and the two fractions separate exactly in that proportion, with a thin pale buffy coat of white cells and platelets at the boundary.
• DEFINITION — Plasma is a straw-coloured, slightly alkaline fluid. Water makes up 90-92 percent of it; dissolved in that water are proteins (6-8 percent), minerals such as sodium, calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate ions, glucose, amino acids, lipids, dissolved gases and traces of many other substances, plus factors for clotting held in inactive form.
• WHY plasma must be mostly water — blood is a delivery service, and water is the vehicle: nutrients, hormones and wastes travel dissolved or suspended in it. The dissolved salts and proteins also hold the pH and osmotic balance steady, so cells bathed by tissue fluid drawn from plasma neither swell nor shrivel.
• EXAMPLE — The three plasma proteins divide the labour memorably. Fibrinogen is the clotting raw material. Globulins run defence — many antibodies are globulins — and transport. Albumins, the most abundant, hold the osmotic balance that keeps fluid from leaking out of the blood into the tissues.
• WORKED PATTERN — Any composition question is a matching exercise between a component and its one job: fibrinogen to clotting, globulins to defence, albumins to osmotic balance, calcium ions to clotting steps, plasma without clotting factors to serum. Learn the pairs as pairs; the options will always offer the right names in the wrong pairings.
• YOUR TURN — After blood clots in a test tube, the clear fluid left above the clot is not plasma. What is it, and what is missing from it? Answer: serum — it is plasma minus the clotting factors, which were consumed in building the clot.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Plasma is 55 percent of blood, not of the body, and its 90-92 percent water figure refers to plasma alone. Examiners swap the two percentages (55 and 90-92) into each other's sentences; anchor them: 55 belongs to the tube, 90-92 belongs to the plasma inside it.

Plasma Proteins and the Three Cell Teams
• DEFINITION — Erythrocytes, the red blood cells (RBCs), are the most abundant formed elements: 5 to 5.5 million per mm3 of blood in a healthy adult. They are biconcave discs packed with haemoglobin, the red iron-containing pigment that carries oxygen. In mammals they lose their nucleus, and they are produced in the red bone marrow.
• DEFINITION — Leucocytes, the white blood cells (WBCs), number only 6000 to 8000 per mm3. They are colourless (no haemoglobin), nucleated, larger and far fewer than RBCs, and they are the defence team: some engulf microbes, others produce antibodies. NCERT names five kinds — neutrophils (the most abundant), eosinophils, basophils, lymphocytes and monocytes.
• DEFINITION — Platelets, or thrombocytes, are not whole cells but fragments pinched off from giant megakaryocyte cells of the bone marrow. They number 150000 to 350000 per mm3, and their job is to start and support clotting at an injury — a severe fall in their count leads to clotting disorders and excessive blood loss.
✔ KEY POINT — The three counts in one line: RBCs in millions (5-5.5 million per mm3), WBCs in thousands (6000-8000 per mm3), platelets in lakhs (150000-350000 per mm3). The orders of magnitude are the memory hook — million, thousand, lakh — and examiners test exactly by shuffling them.
• WHY the RBC is built the way it is — the biconcave shape gives a huge surface area for gas exchange, losing the nucleus frees room for more haemoglobin, and being small and flexible lets it squeeze through capillaries narrower than itself. Every feature of the RBC is a transport adaptation; structure questions in this chapter are answered by the job.
• EXAMPLE — An RBC lives about 120 days, worn out by millions of capillary squeezes, and is finally broken down mainly in the spleen — sometimes called the graveyard of RBCs. Your marrow replaces them continuously, which is why blood donation is safe: the factory never stops.
• WORKED PATTERN — Cell-identification questions give you two clues: a count and a feature. Count in millions plus no nucleus: RBC. Count in thousands plus nucleus plus defence: WBC. Count in lakhs plus cell fragment: platelet. If a stem says "cell fragments formed from megakaryocytes", the answer is platelets before you even see the options.
• YOUR TURN — A patient's report shows a platelet count far below 150000 per mm3 after a viral fever. Which body process fails first? Answer: clotting — fewer platelets means less thrombokinase released at injuries, slower fibrin formation, and a risk of excessive bleeding from small wounds.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Platelets are fragments, not cells, and WBCs are the nucleated ones. The swapped statement "RBCs are nucleated and platelets are true cells" appears in assertion questions every year. In mammals: RBC no nucleus, WBC nucleus present, platelet not a cell at all.

Blood Groups — The ABO System
• DEFINITION — Blood grouping rests on surface molecules. In the ABO system, the RBC surface may carry antigen A, antigen B, both, or neither, and the plasma carries antibodies against whichever antigens the person's own RBCs lack. Group A: antigen A on RBCs, anti-B antibody in plasma. Group B: antigen B, anti-A. Group AB: both antigens, no anti-A or anti-B antibodies. Group O: no antigens, both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
• WHY the antibody pattern is forced — your plasma can never carry antibodies against your own antigens, or your blood would attack itself. So the antibody is always the mirror image of the antigen: have A, carry anti-B; have neither antigen, carry both antibodies. The whole table is one rule, not four facts.
• DEFINITION — Transfusion safety follows from one event: if donor RBCs meet matching antibodies in the recipient's plasma, the donor cells clump together — agglutination — and can block vessels fatally. So the check is always: donor's antigens versus recipient's antibodies. The donor's plasma antibodies are diluted in the recipient and are not the deciding factor.
✔ KEY POINT — Group O (Rh negative) is the universal donor: its RBCs carry no A or B antigen, so no recipient's anti-A or anti-B can attack them. Group AB (Rh positive) is the universal recipient: its plasma carries no anti-A or anti-B, so it can receive RBCs of any ABO group. Donor logic looks at antigens; recipient logic looks at antibodies.
• EXAMPLE — A group B patient needs blood. Group B blood: antigen B meets a plasma with anti-A only — safe. Group O blood: no antigens at all — safe. Group A blood: antigen A meets the patient's anti-A — agglutination, dangerous. Group AB: antigens A and B, the A alone is enough to be dangerous. So a B patient can receive from B and O only.
• WORKED PATTERN — To solve any donor question, write two columns: the donor's RBC antigens, and the recipient's plasma antibodies. If any antigen appears in both columns, the transfusion is unsafe; if the columns share nothing, it is safe. This two-column test never fails, including for the universal donor and recipient questions.
• YOUR TURN — Can group AB blood be donated to a group A patient? Answer: no — AB RBCs carry antigen B, and the A patient's plasma carries anti-B; the B antigen would be attacked. AB can donate only to AB.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Universal donor" is O negative and "universal recipient" is AB positive — students swap the two labels constantly, and the Rh sign is part of the answer. Fix it by logic, not memory: the donor must bring nothing attackable (no antigens, Rh negative), the recipient must attack nothing (no antibodies, and Rh positive so Rh positive blood is also acceptable).

Rh Grouping — The Plus-Minus That Matters in Pregnancy
• DEFINITION — The Rh system is a second, independent grouping based on the Rh antigen (first discovered in the Rhesus monkey). A person whose RBCs carry the Rh antigen is Rh positive; one who lacks it is Rh negative. Unlike ABO, there are no ready-made anti-Rh antibodies — an Rh negative person makes them only after exposure to Rh positive blood.
• WHY that delay creates a pregnancy problem — consider an Rh negative mother carrying an Rh positive child (the child inheriting Rh positive from the father). During the first delivery, some of the baby's Rh positive blood can leak into the mother's circulation, and her immune system responds by producing anti-Rh antibodies. The first child escapes, because the antibodies arrive too late — but the mother is now sensitised for life.
• DEFINITION — In a later pregnancy with another Rh positive baby, the mother's anti-Rh antibodies cross the placenta, attack the baby's RBCs and destroy them. This condition — erythroblastosis fetalis — can cause severe anaemia and jaundice in the newborn and can be fatal.
✔ KEY POINT — Erythroblastosis fetalis needs a precise cast: Rh negative mother, Rh positive father (the source of the baby's antigen), a first Rh positive pregnancy that sensitises, and a second Rh positive baby who is attacked. Change any one member — Rh positive mother, or Rh negative babies — and the disease cannot occur.
• EXAMPLE — Prevention exploits the timing. Immediately after the delivery of her first Rh positive baby, the Rh negative mother is given anti-Rh antibodies from outside. These destroy any baby RBCs that entered her blood before her own immune system can learn from them — she never becomes sensitised, and later pregnancies stay safe.
• WORKED PATTERN — Rh questions are story questions: identify the mother's group, the baby's group, and which pregnancy it is. First Rh positive baby plus Rh negative mother: sensitisation, baby safe. Second Rh positive baby, untreated: erythroblastosis fetalis. Rh positive mother anywhere in the story: no problem at all, whatever the baby.
• YOUR TURN — An Rh negative woman and an Rh positive man have a first child who is Rh negative. Is the second child at risk of erythroblastosis fetalis? Answer: the first child was Rh negative, so no Rh positive cells ever entered the mother — she was never sensitised. A later Rh positive child would be like a first exposure: that child is safe, but she must be protected after its delivery.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Erythroblastosis fetalis is not caused by ABO mismatch, and it does not threaten the first Rh positive child — the sensitisation happens at that child's delivery, after it is safely born. Questions that make the first baby the victim are testing whether you know the sequence, not just the names.

Coagulation of Blood — Building the Emergency Seal
• DEFINITION — Coagulation (clotting) is the mechanism that seals injured vessels: liquid blood converts at the wound into a soft jelly-like mass, the clot or coagulum, which plugs the leak. It is a cascade — a chain of inactive-to-active conversions, each step switching on the next, so a small injury signal is amplified into a solid seal within minutes.
• DEFINITION — The cascade in words. Step 1: injured tissues and platelets at the site release an enzyme complex, thrombokinase. Step 2: thrombokinase, in the presence of calcium ions, converts prothrombin — an inactive protein always present in plasma — into thrombin, an active enzyme. Step 3: thrombin converts fibrinogen — a soluble plasma protein — into fibrin, insoluble threads. Step 4: the fibrin threads form a mesh that traps blood cells; the mesh contracts and becomes the clot.
✔ KEY POINT — Only two conversions carry names in the NCERT cascade, and questions live on them: prothrombin to thrombin (by thrombokinase, needing calcium ions), and fibrinogen to fibrin (by thrombin). Calcium ions are essential for the conversion steps — blood that cannot supply calcium ions cannot clot on schedule.
• WHY the cascade uses inactive precursors — prothrombin and fibrinogen circulate harmlessly all the time because they are switched off. If fibrin formed inside uninjured vessels, the vessels themselves would plug — a clot in the wrong place (as in a coronary artery) is as dangerous as bleeding. Local release of thrombokinase confines the reaction to the injury site.
• EXAMPLE — A shaving cut stops bleeding in a few minutes with no help from you: platelets stick at the cut, thrombokinase appears, the two conversions run, and a fibrin net closes the vessel while you are still reaching for a bandage. The dark red crust you see later is the dried clot — fibrin mesh plus trapped cells.
• WORKED PATTERN — Order questions give four events shuffled. Rebuild the chain by following the substance: thrombokinase first (it starts everything), then thrombin appears (from prothrombin), then fibrin appears (from fibrinogen), then the mesh and clot. If an option has fibrin before thrombin, it is wrong — fibrin cannot exist until thrombin makes it.
• YOUR TURN — A sample of blood is placed in a tube from which calcium ions have been removed. It fails to clot normally. Which step is blocked? Answer: the conversion of prothrombin to thrombin (and the cascade steps that need calcium ions) — thrombokinase cannot activate prothrombin without calcium ions, so no thrombin, no fibrin, no clot.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Fibrinogen is the soluble inactive protein dissolved in plasma; fibrin is the insoluble thread that forms the mesh. Thrombin converts the first into the second. Options that say "fibrinogen forms the mesh" or "thrombokinase converts fibrinogen" swap the actors — the enzyme chain is thrombokinase starts, thrombin converts, fibrin builds.

Lymph — The Second River
• DEFINITION — As blood flows through capillaries, some water and small dissolved substances leak out into the spaces between cells, forming tissue fluid (interstitial fluid). The tissue fluid that is collected back by the lymphatic system — a network of lymph vessels, lymph nodes and lymphoid organs — is called lymph. Lymph is a colourless fluid containing lymphocytes, with essentially the same mineral distribution as plasma but far less protein and no RBCs.
• WHY a second drainage system must exist — capillary leakage is continuous, and the blood capillaries do not reclaim everything. Without the lymph vessels returning the excess fluid and escaped proteins to the blood, tissues would swell. Lymph is the return pipeline: it drains into lymphatic vessels, passes through lymph nodes, and finally empties into the large veins near the heart.
✔ KEY POINT — Lymph does three jobs the exam asks about separately: it returns tissue fluid and proteins to the blood, it carries digested fats absorbed from the intestine (the intestinal lymph vessels, lacteals, give lymph a milky look after a fatty meal), and it is an immune highway — its lymphocytes and the filters in lymph nodes defend the body.
• EXAMPLE — After a fatty meal, the lymph leaving the intestine looks milky because it is loaded with absorbed fat droplets — fat enters lymph first, not blood, which is why the lacteals are named in absorption questions. And when you have a throat infection, the swollen glands in your neck are lymph nodes busy filtering lymph and multiplying lymphocytes.
• WORKED PATTERN — Blood-versus-lymph comparisons resolve on four points: colour (red versus colourless), RBCs (present versus absent), protein (more in plasma, less in lymph), and flow (a pumped circuit versus a one-way drainage back into veins). Any "which of these is true for lymph" question is one of these four cells of the table.
• YOUR TURN — Why does lymph contain no RBCs, even though it is derived from blood? Answer: lymph is made from the fluid that leaks out of capillaries, and capillary walls let water and small solutes pass but not cells — RBCs never leave the blood in the first place, so the drained fluid is cell-poor, carrying mainly the lymphocytes it picks up in lymphoid tissue.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Lymph is not "blood without RBCs" in composition: its protein content is much lower than plasma's, and it flows only towards the heart, never in a circuit. Also, fats are absorbed into lymph (lacteals) while sugars and amino acids enter the blood capillaries of the villus — a favourite swapped pair.

Circulatory Pathways — Open, Closed, Single and Double
• DEFINITION — In an open circulatory system, blood pumped by the heart pours out of open-ended vessels into body cavities (sinuses) and bathes the tissues directly, as in arthropods and most molluscs. In a closed circulatory system, blood stays inside vessels of varying diameter — arteries, capillaries and veins — as in annelids and all chordates, including us. Closed systems can regulate flow precisely and sustain higher pressures.
• DEFINITION — Single circulation means blood passes through the heart once per complete circuit: in fishes, the two-chambered heart pumps deoxygenated blood to the gills, oxygenated blood flows straight to the body and returns — heart, gills, body, heart. Double circulation means blood passes through the heart twice per circuit: once in the pulmonary loop and once in the systemic loop, as in birds and mammals.
• WHY double circulation wins for warm-blooded animals — separating the loops means the oxygenated blood returning from the lungs is pumped again before reaching the body, so tissues receive blood at high pressure and fully loaded with oxygen, with zero mixing. Birds and mammals, which burn energy continuously to hold body temperature, cannot afford the diluted supply of a mixing system.
• EXAMPLE — Amphibians and most reptiles run a middle design: a three-chambered heart (two atria, one ventricle) with incomplete double circulation — some mixing occurs in the single ventricle, tolerable for animals with lower energy demands. Crocodiles break the reptile pattern with a fully four-chambered heart, a detail examiners love.
✔ KEY POINT — The chamber ladder: fishes 2 chambers and single circulation; amphibians and reptiles (except crocodiles) 3 chambers and incomplete double circulation; crocodiles, birds and mammals 4 chambers and complete double circulation. Chamber count, mixing, and circulation type always travel together.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any animal named in a question, run the ladder in order: find the group, read off its chambers, then its circulation type, then whether mixing occurs. A question that describes "blood passes through the heart twice, but mixing occurs in the ventricle" is asking for the three-chambered pattern even if no animal is named.
• YOUR TURN — A frog's heart receives oxygenated blood in the left atrium and deoxygenated blood in the right atrium, yet the body still gets partly mixed blood. Why? Answer: both atria empty into one common ventricle, where the two bloods mix before being pumped out — the frog has three chambers, not four, so its double circulation is incomplete.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Double circulation" alone does not mean "no mixing": amphibians have double circulation with mixing. The no-mixing guarantee needs the extra word complete, which belongs only to the four-chambered hearts. And fishes are the single-circulation example — never describe fish blood as passing through the heart twice.

The Human Heart — Position, Sac and Chambers
• DEFINITION — The heart is a muscular, fist-sized organ lying in the thoracic cavity between the two lungs, slightly tilted to the left. It is protected by the pericardium, a double-walled membranous sac enclosing pericardial fluid, which cushions the heart and lets it beat without friction against surrounding tissues.
• DEFINITION — The heart has four chambers: two smaller, thin-walled upper atria (right and left) that receive blood, and two larger, thick-walled lower ventricles (right and left) that pump blood out. A wall called the septum divides the heart into right and left halves: the inter-atrial septum between the atria and the inter-ventricular septum between the ventricles.
• WHY the walls differ in thickness — wall thickness is a pressure map. Atria only push blood a few centimetres down into the ventricles, so their walls are thin. The right ventricle pushes blood only to the nearby lungs, so its wall is thicker. The left ventricle pushes blood against the resistance of the entire body circuit, so its wall is the thickest of all four chambers — structure follows workload, every time.
✔ KEY POINT — The right side of the heart handles only deoxygenated blood (body to lungs); the left side handles only oxygenated blood (lungs to body). The septum is what makes this separation absolute — there is no normal opening between the two sides after birth.
• EXAMPLE — Feel your own pulse logic: the heartbeat you sense at your chest is mostly the left ventricle's contraction, the strongest squeeze of the four, and the heart's leftward tilt is why that beat is felt most clearly on the left side of the chest.
• WORKED PATTERN — Chamber questions reduce to receive-versus-pump plus which blood. Receives deoxygenated blood from the body: right atrium. Pumps to the lungs: right ventricle. Receives oxygenated blood from the lungs: left atrium. Pumps to the body: left ventricle. Four chambers, four one-line addresses — write them until they are reflexes.
• YOUR TURN — A defect leaves a hole in the inter-ventricular septum of a newborn. Predict the immediate consequence for the blood reaching the body. Answer: oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix in the ventricles, so the aorta receives partly deoxygenated blood — the tissues get less oxygen per litre, exactly the inefficiency the septum exists to prevent.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Atria are the receiving chambers and ventricles the pumping chambers, and the atria are smaller with thinner walls. Statements like "the left atrium has the thickest wall" invert the map — the thickest wall belongs to the left ventricle, the chamber with the longest circuit to drive.

Valves and Great Vessels — One-Way Traffic
• DEFINITION — Valves are flaps that let blood pass in one direction only. The tricuspid valve (three flaps) guards the opening between the right atrium and right ventricle. The bicuspid valve, also called the mitral valve (two flaps), guards the left atrium-to-ventricle opening. Together they are the atrioventricular (AV) valves. Semilunar valves guard the exits where the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and the aorta leaves the left ventricle.
• DEFINITION — The great vessels: the superior and inferior vena cava deliver deoxygenated blood from the body into the right atrium. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs. The pulmonary veins bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium. The aorta carries oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the entire body.
✔ KEY POINT — The naming rule that dissolves the chapter's most famous trap: arteries are vessels that carry blood away from the heart and veins carry blood towards the heart — direction, not oxygen content. That is why the pulmonary artery can carry deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary veins oxygenated blood without breaking any rule.
• WHY valves are non-negotiable — the heart squeezes in pulses; without one-way valves, each squeeze would push blood backwards as well as forwards and the circulation would slosh instead of flow. Every valve in the heart exists because the chamber behind it contracts hard enough to reverse the flow it just received.
• EXAMPLE — Trace one drop of blood aloud and every valve and vessel falls into place: body to vena cava to right atrium, through the tricuspid valve to the right ventricle, through the semilunar valve into the pulmonary artery to the lungs, back by pulmonary veins to the left atrium, through the bicuspid valve to the left ventricle, and out through the semilunar valve into the aorta to the body. That single sentence is half the chapter.
• WORKED PATTERN — In any flow-order question, the sequence must alternate chamber, valve, vessel, chamber — and blood can never pass from a ventricle back to an atrium, nor from an artery into a ventricle. Scan each option for a reversed step; usually exactly one option keeps every arrow pointing the legal way.
• YOUR TURN — During ventricular contraction, which two sets of valves are closed and which are open? Answer: the AV valves (tricuspid and bicuspid) are closed — that is what stops blood surging back into the atria — and the semilunar valves are open, letting blood enter the pulmonary artery and aorta.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Tricuspid is on the right, bicuspid (mitral) on the left — remember "right has three, left has two" by the letters: tRicuspid, Right. And the pulmonary artery is the only artery in the chapter carrying deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary vein is the only vein carrying oxygenated blood. Both traps are the same trap: naming by direction, not by cargo.

Nodal Tissue — The Heart's Own Wiring
• DEFINITION — The heart's rhythm is generated inside the heart itself by nodal tissue — patches of specialised cardiac muscle that can fire impulses without any nerve signal. The sinoatrial node (SAN) sits in the right upper corner of the right atrium. The atrioventricular node (AVN) sits in the lower left corner of the right atrium, close to the atrio-ventricular septum. From the AVN, a bundle of fibres — the AV bundle or bundle of His — runs through the septum and branches into Purkinje fibres spread through the ventricular walls.
• DEFINITION — The SAN can generate the maximum number of action potentials of any nodal tissue — about 70 to 75 per minute — and therefore it sets the rhythm for the whole heart. That is why the SAN is called the pacemaker: the fastest natural oscillator wins, and every other tissue simply follows its beat.
✔ KEY POINT — The impulse route is a fixed four-step chain: SAN fires, both atria contract; the impulse reaches the AVN; the AV bundle (bundle of His) carries it down the septum; Purkinje fibres spread it through both ventricles, which contract together. Questions asking "the correct sequence of conduction" are this chain with its steps shuffled.
• WHY the AVN delay is a feature, not slowness — the AVN holds the impulse briefly before releasing it to the ventricles. That pause lets the atria finish squeezing their blood into the ventricles before the ventricles contract. Without it, atria and ventricles would contract almost together and the ventricles would eject only part of their possible load.
• EXAMPLE — Because the heartbeat is generated within the heart (myogenic), a heart removed for transplant continues to beat for a while outside the body — nerves and hormones can only speed it up or slow it down, not start it. The brain manages the pacemaker; it does not replace it.
• WORKED PATTERN — Pacemaker questions hinge on the firing rates: whichever nodal tissue generates impulses fastest controls the rhythm. SAN at 70-75 per minute outruns the AVN and the rest, so SAN answers every "why is it the pacemaker" and "which structure initiates the heartbeat" question in this chapter.
• YOUR TURN — If the SAN is damaged and falls silent, the heart does not stop — the AVN takes over as a backup pacemaker. Will the resting heart rate rise or fall, and why? Answer: it falls, because the AVN's natural firing rate is lower than the SAN's 70-75 per minute — the backup oscillator is slower, so the whole heart settles to its slower rhythm.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node, not the AVN, and it sits in the right atrium — options placing it in the left atrium or calling the AVN the pacemaker are the standard wrong choices. Also, the bundle of His and Purkinje fibres conduct the impulse; they do not generate the normal rhythm.

The Cardiac Cycle — Three Acts in 0.8 Seconds
• DEFINITION — The cardiac cycle is the sequence of events of one heartbeat, from the start of one beat to the start of the next. At a heart rate of 72 beats per minute, one cycle lasts 0.8 seconds. It has three phases: joint diastole (0.4 seconds), atrial systole (0.1 seconds), and ventricular systole (0.3 seconds). Diastole means relaxation and filling; systole means contraction and ejection.
• DEFINITION — In joint diastole, all four chambers are relaxed. The AV valves are open, the semilunar valves are closed, and blood flows passively from the atria into the ventricles — most ventricular filling happens here, without any contraction at all. In atrial systole, the atria contract (following the SAN impulse) and top up the ventricles. In ventricular systole, the ventricles contract, the AV valves slam shut, the semilunar valves are forced open, and blood is ejected into the pulmonary artery and aorta; the atria are already relaxing during this phase.
✔ KEY POINT — The timings add up and the exam checks the sum: 0.4 plus 0.1 plus 0.3 equals 0.8 seconds. Joint diastole is the longest phase — the heart rests and fills for half of every cycle, which is why a faster heart rate steals time mainly from filling, not from ejection.
• DEFINITION — The two heart sounds are valve sounds. Lub, the first sound, is produced by the closure of the tricuspid and bicuspid (AV) valves at the start of ventricular systole. Dub, the second sound, is produced by the closure of the semilunar valves at the start of ventricular diastole. A stethoscope hears valve doors closing, not muscle squeezing.
• WHY blood never flows backwards during the changeovers — pressure does the work and valves make it permanent. The instant ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure, the AV valves are pushed shut; the instant it rises above arterial pressure, the semilunar valves open. The valves themselves have no muscles and no nerves — they are passive flaps moved entirely by pressure differences.
• EXAMPLE — Run the cycle with your own pulse: the beat you feel is ventricular systole ejecting blood into the aorta; between beats, during the long joint diastole, the heart is relaxed and refilling. When you sprint, the cycle shortens — and it is diastole that shrinks most, which is why extreme heart rates eventually reduce filling and output per beat.
• WORKED PATTERN — Phase questions give a valve state and ask for the phase: AV open plus semilunar closed plus atria relaxed means joint diastole. AV open with atria contracting means atrial systole. AV closed plus semilunar open means ventricular systole. Valve states and sounds identify phases more reliably than chamber descriptions — scan for them first.
• YOUR TURN — At the moment the dub sound is heard, which phase is just beginning and which valves have just closed? Answer: ventricular diastole is beginning; the semilunar valves have just closed as the ventricles relax and arterial pressure pushes blood back against them.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Lub goes with the AV valves (tricuspid and bicuspid) and dub with the semilunar valves — swapping them is the single most repeated cardiac-cycle error. And atrial systole is only 0.1 seconds while joint diastole is 0.4: options that hand the 0.4 to systole are wrong on arithmetic alone.

ECG — Reading the Heart's Electricity
• DEFINITION — An electrocardiogram (ECG) is a graphical recording of the electrical activity of the heart during one cardiac cycle, taken by an electrocardiograph through electrodes on the skin. Each peak on the trace corresponds to a specific electrical event, and the muscle contraction follows the electrical signal — electricity first, squeeze second.
• DEFINITION — The three waves of a standard ECG: the P wave is a small upward wave representing the electrical excitation (depolarisation) of the atria, which leads to contraction of both atria. The QRS complex represents the depolarisation of the ventricles, which initiates ventricular contraction — the contraction starts just after the Q peak. The T wave represents the return of the ventricles from the excited to the normal state (repolarisation), and its end marks the end of ventricular systole.
✔ KEY POINT — Depolarisation means the electrical wave that triggers contraction; repolarisation means electrical recovery. P and QRS are depolarisation waves (atria, then ventricles); T is the ventricles' repolarisation wave. The atria also repolarise, but that small signal is buried inside the much larger QRS complex and never seen as its own wave.
• WHY the QRS complex dwarfs the P wave — the trace measures the total electrical activity of the muscle mass involved. The ventricles are far more massive than the atria, so their depolarisation writes a tall, sharp spike, while the atria write a small bump. Wave size follows muscle mass — a principle that also explains why ventricular problems dominate ECG interpretation.
• EXAMPLE — A doctor counts the QRS complexes on a one-minute ECG strip to get the heart rate, and checks that every QRS is preceded by a P wave — proof that the beat began at the SAN and travelled the correct route. The ECG is a route map of the impulse, which is why conduction faults are diagnosed from it.
• WORKED PATTERN — Match wave to chamber to event in one pass: P, atria, depolarise, contract. QRS, ventricles, depolarise, contract. T, ventricles, repolarise, relax. Every ECG question in this syllabus is a permutation of that table — usually asking which wave is missing, misplaced, or wrongly described.
• YOUR TURN — On a patient's ECG, the P waves are present but some are not followed by a QRS complex. Which part of the conduction pathway is failing? Answer: the AVN or AV bundle — the atria are being fired by the SAN (P waves exist), but some impulses are not reaching the ventricles, so the block lies at the atrio-ventricular junction.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The QRS complex is ventricular depolarisation, not contraction itself and not repolarisation. Likewise the T wave is repolarisation of the ventricles — options describing T as "depolarisation of ventricles" or QRS as "repolarisation" invert the two events the whole trace is built on.

Cardiac Output — Stroke Volume Times Rate
• DEFINITION — Stroke volume is the volume of blood pumped out by each ventricle with every beat — about 70 mL in a resting adult. Cardiac output is the volume pumped out by each ventricle per minute: cardiac output equals stroke volume multiplied by heart rate. With 70 mL and 72 beats per minute, cardiac output is about 5000 mL, or 5 litres, per minute.
✔ KEY POINT — The arithmetic is the memory: 70 mL times 72 per minute equals 5040 mL, roughly 5 litres — exactly your total blood volume. So the entire blood supply makes a full circuit about once every minute at rest. Examiners test the formula, the two inputs, and that striking conclusion.
• WHY athletes break the pattern beautifully — a trained athlete's heart ejects a much larger stroke volume, so the same cardiac output is achieved at a far lower resting heart rate, and during exercise the athlete's cardiac output can rise far above an untrained person's. Same formula, different inputs: fitness hides inside the stroke volume term.
• EXAMPLE — Compare two resting people. Ordinary adult: 70 mL times 72 equals about 5040 mL per minute. Trained athlete: 100 mL times 50 equals 5000 mL per minute — the same output from 22 fewer beats every minute. The athlete's heart is not faster; it is stronger per beat.
• WORKED PATTERN — Every cardiac-output problem is one multiplication or one division: output equals volume per beat times beats per minute. Given any two of the three quantities, the third falls out. Check units before calculating — stroke volume in mL, output asked in litres: divide by 1000 at the end, the step most often forgotten.
• YOUR TURN — During exercise a student's heart rate rises to 120 per minute and stroke volume to 90 mL. What is the cardiac output, and how does it compare with the resting value of about 5 litres per minute? Answer: 90 times 120 equals 10800 mL, about 10.8 litres per minute — more than double the resting output, achieved by raising both factors of the product.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Cardiac output is defined per ventricle, yet both ventricles must pump the same average volume — otherwise blood would pile up in one circuit. Options claiming the left ventricle has a permanently higher output than the right misunderstand the definition: the 5 litres is the flow through each side, in series.

Double Circulation — Two Loops, Zero Mixing
• DEFINITION — In humans, blood passes through the heart twice during every complete circuit — this is double circulation. It consists of two loops. Pulmonary circulation: deoxygenated blood flows from the right ventricle through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, and oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium. Systemic circulation: oxygenated blood flows from the left ventricle through the aorta to all body tissues, and deoxygenated blood returns through the vena cavae to the right atrium.
✔ KEY POINT — The two loops share one pump but never share their blood: the septum keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood completely separate, so every drop reaching the body is fully oxygenated and is driven at full systemic pressure. That efficiency is what sustains the high, constant energy demand of warm-blooded animals.
• WHY the heart is visited twice — the pulmonary loop is a low-pressure detour (the lung capillaries are delicate and close by), while the systemic loop needs high pressure for a long journey. One visit collects the blood and sends it to the lungs; the second visit repressurises the oxygenated blood for the body. Two loops, two pressure levels, one four-chambered pump.
• EXAMPLE — Follow one RBC through a full circuit and count the heart visits: start in the right atrium (visit one, right side), out to the lungs, back to the left atrium (visit two, left side), out to a toe, and back to the right atrium. Two passes through the heart, one complete circuit of the body — that sentence is the definition of double circulation in action.
• WORKED PATTERN — Loop questions ask "which loop" using a vessel or chamber as the clue. Pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, right ventricle as the exit, left atrium as the entry: pulmonary loop. Aorta, vena cava, body tissues: systemic loop. If a pathway includes the lungs it is pulmonary; if it includes everything else, systemic — there is no third option.
• YOUR TURN — A drop of blood is in the pulmonary artery. Name the chamber it came from, the chamber it will next enter after the lungs, and the valve it passes through on returning to a ventricle's side of the heart. Answer: it came from the right ventricle; after the lungs it enters the left atrium via the pulmonary veins; it then passes through the bicuspid (mitral) valve into the left ventricle.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — In double circulation, blood passes through the heart twice but through the lungs once per circuit. Stating that the heart "pumps the same blood twice to the lungs" garbles the loops. And the right side of the heart belongs to the pulmonary loop's start — right ventricle pumps into pulmonary, not systemic, circulation.

Regulation of Cardiac Activity
• DEFINITION — Although the heartbeat is generated inside the heart by the SAN, its rate and force are tuned from outside. The neural control centre sits in the medulla oblongata of the brain. Through the autonomic nervous system, sympathetic signals increase the rate of heartbeat and the strength of ventricular contraction, while parasympathetic signals decrease the rate and slow conduction.
• DEFINITION — Hormones reinforce the same tuning: the adrenal medulla secretes adrenaline and noradrenaline, which increase the heartbeat rate and cardiac output — the chemistry of the fight-or-flight response. The heart therefore has three layers of control: its own pacemaker sets the base rhythm, nerves adjust it moment to moment, and hormones sustain the adjustment.
• WHY a myogenic heart still needs outside control — the pacemaker alone would hold one steady tempo, but the body's demand is not steady: sleep, digestion, sprinting and fear each need a different output. The medulla reads the situation and instructs the pacemaker, through nerves and hormones, to speed up or slow down — an autopilot with a supervisor.
• EXAMPLE — You stand up suddenly for a sprint: within seconds the sympathetic system fires, the adrenal medulla releases adrenaline, and heart rate and stroke volume climb together, multiplying cardiac output before your muscles have even begun to ache. Afterwards, parasympathetic signals ease the rate back down — the same system that sped the heart up now applies the brake.
• WORKED PATTERN — Regulation questions name a signal and ask its direction. Sympathetic nerves and adrenal medullary hormones: rate up, output up. Parasympathetic (vagus) signals: rate down, output down. The medulla is the centre for both. Any option that makes the parasympathetic system accelerate the heart has the two divisions swapped.
• YOUR TURN — A drug blocks parasympathetic signals to the heart. Predict the effect on resting heart rate. Answer: it rises — with the brake removed, the SAN's rhythm runs unopposed by the slowing signals, so the heart settles at a faster resting rate.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The heart is myogenic, not neurogenic: the beat originates in the SAN, and nerves only modulate it. Earthworm-style neurogenic hearts (rhythm from a nerve ganglion) are the contrast case. Also, adrenaline comes from the adrenal medulla in this chapter's regulation story — do not place its source in the thyroid or pituitary.

Disorders of the Circulatory System
• DEFINITION — Blood pressure is the force blood exerts on vessel walls; the normal resting value is 120 over 80 — 120 mm Hg systolic (during ventricular contraction) and 80 mm Hg diastolic (during relaxation). Hypertension is persistently high blood pressure, with 140 over 90 or higher taken as the danger threshold; repeated readings at that level require attention because chronic hypertension silently damages the heart, kidneys, brain and eyes.
• DEFINITION — Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the narrowing of the coronary arteries that supply the heart muscle itself, most often by atherosclerosis — deposits of calcium, fat, cholesterol and fibrous tissue building up as plaques inside the vessel wall, shrinking the lumen and starving the muscle downstream.
• DEFINITION — Angina pectoris is acute chest pain that appears when the heart muscle gets too little oxygen — a warning symptom of narrowed coronary arteries, typically triggered by exertion and relieved by rest. Heart failure is a state in which the heart cannot pump rigorously enough to meet the body's needs, so blood congests — most visibly in the lungs.
✔ KEY POINT — Three emergencies that students merge into one, kept apart: heart failure means the pump is too weak (congestion, but the heart still beats); a heart attack means part of the heart muscle is suddenly damaged by inadequate blood supply; cardiac arrest means the heart stops beating altogether. Failure is weakness, attack is damage, arrest is stoppage.
• WHY hypertension is called silent — it usually has no symptoms while it does its damage: the heart must push against higher resistance for years, its left ventricle thickens and tires, and vessel walls stiffen and narrow. The first noticeable event may be the failure or the attack, which is why the 120 over 80 and 140 over 90 numbers are worth memorising cold.
• EXAMPLE — One patient's story separates all four disorders: years of untreated hypertension (150 over 95) strain his heart; atherosclerosis narrows his coronary arteries; climbing stairs now triggers angina, the oxygen-shortage warning pain; a plaque finally blocks the vessel and the starved muscle is damaged — a heart attack; over the following years the weakened heart slides into failure, pumping too feebly and congesting his lungs.
• WORKED PATTERN — Disorder questions describe a mechanism, not a name: deposits in the wall and a narrowed lumen — atherosclerosis and CAD. Chest pain on exertion from oxygen shortage — angina. Pump too weak, blood backing up into the lungs — heart failure. Muscle suddenly damaged — heart attack. Match the mechanism in the stem, and the correct name follows even if the options use unfamiliar wording.
• YOUR TURN — A man's repeated readings are 142 over 92. Is he hypertensive by the chapter's criterion, and by how much does he exceed each threshold? Answer: yes — hypertension begins at 140 over 90; he exceeds the systolic threshold by 2 mm Hg and the diastolic threshold by 2 mm Hg. Small numbers, real diagnosis: the threshold, not the margin, decides.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Heart failure is not the same as cardiac arrest — NCERT says so explicitly. A failing heart is still beating; an arrested heart has stopped. Also note which pressure is which: the larger number (120) is systolic, recorded during ventricular contraction; the smaller (80) is diastolic. Reversed pairs are planted in options.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Blood is about 55 percent plasma and 45 percent formed elements; plasma is 90-92 percent water with 6-8 percent proteins — fibrinogen for clotting, globulins for defence, albumins for osmotic balance. Serum is plasma minus the clotting factors.
✔ KEY POINT — The counts: RBCs 5-5.5 million per mm3 (biconcave, no nucleus in mammals, lifespan about 120 days), WBCs 6000-8000 per mm3 (nucleated, defence), platelets 150000-350000 per mm3 (fragments of megakaryocytes, start clotting).
✔ KEY POINT — ABO table: A has antigen A and anti-B; B has antigen B and anti-A; AB has both antigens and no antibodies; O has no antigens and both antibodies. O negative is the universal donor; AB positive is the universal recipient.
✔ KEY POINT — Rh: an Rh negative mother sensitised by a first Rh positive baby's delivery can attack a later Rh positive baby — erythroblastosis fetalis — prevented by giving her anti-Rh antibodies right after the first delivery.
✔ KEY POINT — Clotting cascade: injury releases thrombokinase; thrombokinase plus calcium ions converts prothrombin into thrombin; thrombin converts fibrinogen into fibrin; fibrin threads mesh, trap cells and form the clot.
✔ KEY POINT — Lymph: colourless, no RBCs, low protein, same mineral distribution as plasma; drains tissue fluid back to the veins, carries absorbed fats from the intestine, and moves lymphocytes for defence.
✔ KEY POINT — Circulation ladder: fishes 2 chambers, single circulation; amphibians and reptiles 3 chambers, incomplete double; crocodiles, birds and mammals 4 chambers, complete double circulation with no mixing.
✔ KEY POINT — Heart wiring: SAN (right upper corner of right atrium) is the pacemaker, firing 70-75 impulses per minute; then AVN, bundle of His, Purkinje fibres. Atria contract first, ventricles after the AVN delay.
✔ KEY POINT — Cardiac cycle at 72 beats per minute lasts 0.8 seconds: joint diastole 0.4, atrial systole 0.1, ventricular systole 0.3. Lub is the AV valves closing; dub is the semilunar valves closing.
✔ KEY POINT — ECG: P wave is atrial depolarisation, QRS complex is ventricular depolarisation (contraction starts just after Q), T wave is ventricular repolarisation and its end marks the end of ventricular systole.
✔ KEY POINT — Cardiac output equals stroke volume (about 70 mL) times heart rate (72) equals about 5000 mL per minute — the whole blood volume every minute; athletes achieve it with a larger stroke volume and lower rate.
✔ KEY POINT — Double circulation: pulmonary loop (right ventricle to lungs to left atrium) plus systemic loop (left ventricle to body to right atrium); the septum keeps the two bloods completely separate.
✔ KEY POINT — Regulation: the medulla oblongata controls the heart through autonomic nerves — sympathetic speeds it up, parasympathetic slows it down — and adrenal medullary hormones (adrenaline, noradrenaline) increase rate and output.
✔ KEY POINT — Disorders: normal pressure 120 over 80, hypertension at 140 over 90 and above; atherosclerosis narrows coronary arteries (CAD); angina is oxygen-shortage chest pain; heart failure is a weak pump with congestion — not a heart attack (muscle damage) and not cardiac arrest (stoppage).

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Swapping the universal donor and recipient. Why it is wrong: the donor's RBCs must carry nothing attackable — group O negative (no A or B antigens, no Rh antigen) — while the recipient's plasma must attack nothing — group AB positive (no anti-A or anti-B antibodies). Correct principle: donor is judged by antigens on the RBCs; recipient is judged by antibodies in the plasma.
• Mistake 2: Saying the pulmonary artery carries oxygenated blood because it is an artery. Why it is wrong: arteries and veins are named by direction of flow relative to the heart, not by oxygen content; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood away from the heart to the lungs. Correct principle: artery means away from the heart, vein means towards the heart — then check the cargo separately.
• Mistake 3: Making fibrinogen form the clot mesh directly. Why it is wrong: fibrinogen is the soluble plasma protein; it must first be converted by thrombin into fibrin, the insoluble thread that actually builds the mesh. Correct principle: thrombokinase activates prothrombin to thrombin, thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin, fibrin forms the clot.
• Mistake 4: Attaching the lub sound to the semilunar valves. Why it is wrong: lub is the first sound, made when the AV valves (tricuspid and bicuspid) close at the start of ventricular systole; dub follows when the semilunar valves close. Correct principle: lub-AV, dub-semilunar — first sound, first valves the blood met.
• Mistake 5: Reading the QRS complex as ventricular repolarisation. Why it is wrong: QRS is the depolarisation of the ventricles that triggers their contraction; repolarisation of the ventricles is the T wave. Correct principle: P and QRS depolarise (atria, ventricles); T repolarises (ventricles).
• Mistake 6: Claiming the first Rh positive baby of an Rh negative mother suffers erythroblastosis fetalis. Why it is wrong: sensitisation happens at that baby's delivery, when fetal blood leaks into the mother — the antibodies form afterwards, so the danger is to later Rh positive babies. Correct principle: first pregnancy sensitises, second pregnancy suffers; prevention is anti-Rh antibodies given just after the first delivery.
• Mistake 7: Giving joint diastole 0.1 seconds and atrial systole 0.4. Why it is wrong: the three phases are joint diastole 0.4, atrial systole 0.1 and ventricular systole 0.3, summing to the 0.8 second cycle. Correct principle: the resting, filling phase is the longest; contractions are brief. If your three numbers do not sum to 0.8, they are wrong.
• Mistake 8: Equating heart failure with cardiac arrest. Why it is wrong: in heart failure the heart still beats but pumps too weakly, causing congestion; in cardiac arrest the heart stops beating altogether; a heart attack is sudden damage to heart muscle from inadequate blood supply. Correct principle: failure is weakness, attack is damage, arrest is stoppage — three different mechanisms, three different names.
• Mistake 9: Calling platelets nucleated cells and RBCs nucleated too. Why it is wrong: platelets are cell fragments shed by megakaryocytes, and mammalian RBCs lose their nucleus; among the formed elements, only WBCs are true nucleated cells. Correct principle: RBC no nucleus, WBC nucleus, platelet not a cell at all.
• Mistake 10: Assuming the left ventricle's cardiac output exceeds the right ventricle's. Why it is wrong: the two ventricles pump in series in one circuit, so their average outputs must be equal — about 5 litres per minute each — or blood would accumulate in one loop. Correct principle: cardiac output is the flow through each ventricle per minute, and in a closed series circuit the flows match.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A patient with blood group B negative needs an urgent transfusion. Blood of groups A positive, B negative, O negative and AB negative is available. Which units are safe?
• Given: Recipient is group B (RBC antigen B, plasma anti-A) and Rh negative (must not receive Rh positive blood, which would sensitise).
• Principle: A unit is safe only if the donor's RBC antigens (A, B, Rh) find no matching antibodies in the recipient, and an Rh negative recipient must receive Rh negative blood.
• Steps: A positive: antigen A meets anti-A — unsafe, and Rh positive too. B negative: antigen B only; recipient has no anti-B — safe. O negative: no A, no B, no Rh antigen — safe. AB negative: antigen A present — unsafe.
• Answer: B negative and O negative units only.
• Reasoning: Run the two-column test (donor antigens versus recipient antibodies) for every unit, then apply the Rh rule on top — two filters, applied in that order, solve every transfusion problem in this chapter.
• Mistake to avoid: Choosing O positive "because O is the universal donor" — the universal donor is O negative; the Rh sign is part of the answer, not a footnote.
• Example 2 — Problem: An Rh negative woman's first baby, born to an Rh positive father, is Rh positive and healthy. During her second pregnancy the baby is again Rh positive. Explain the risk and how it could have been prevented.
• Given: Mother Rh negative (no anti-Rh antibodies initially); first Rh positive delivery; second Rh positive fetus; no preventive injection was given.
• Principle: At the first delivery, fetal Rh positive RBCs entering the mother's blood sensitise her immune system, which then produces anti-Rh antibodies permanently. In later pregnancies those antibodies cross the placenta and destroy fetal RBCs — erythroblastosis fetalis.
• Steps: First baby: exposure at delivery, mother sensitised, baby unharmed. Interval: mother now carries anti-Rh antibodies. Second baby: antibodies cross the placenta, fetal RBCs are destroyed, causing severe anaemia and jaundice in the newborn.
• Answer: The second baby risks erythroblastosis fetalis; it was preventable by injecting the mother with anti-Rh antibodies immediately after the first delivery, destroying the leaked fetal RBCs before her own immune system could respond to them.
• Reasoning: The disease is a timing story: harm needs prior sensitisation, and prevention works by erasing the sensitising event before the mother's immune system learns from it.
• Mistake to avoid: Saying the first baby was the one at risk, or blaming an ABO mismatch — the mechanism is Rh sensitisation across two pregnancies, nothing else.
• Example 3 — Problem: Calculate the cardiac output of a resting adult with stroke volume 70 mL and heart rate 72 beats per minute, in litres per minute.
• Given: Stroke volume 70 mL per beat; heart rate 72 beats per minute.
• Principle: Cardiac output equals stroke volume multiplied by heart rate.
• Steps: 70 multiplied by 72 equals 5040. Units: mL per minute. Convert: 5040 divided by 1000 equals 5.04 litres per minute.
• Answer: About 5.04 litres per minute — roughly 5 litres, equal to the entire blood volume each minute.
• Reasoning: Check the product digit by digit: 70 times 70 is 4900, plus 70 times 2 is 140, total 5040 — the standard NCERT value, and the reason the whole blood volume circulates about once a minute.
• Mistake to avoid: Forgetting the mL-to-litre conversion and reporting 5040 litres, or multiplying stroke volume by the cardiac cycle duration instead of the heart rate.
• Example 4 — Problem: A trained athlete has a resting stroke volume of 100 mL and a resting heart rate of 50 beats per minute. Compare the athlete's cardiac output and daily number of heartbeats with the adult of Example 3.
• Given: Athlete: 100 mL, 50 per minute. Ordinary adult: 70 mL, 72 per minute, output 5040 mL per minute.
• Principle: Same formula, cardiac output equals stroke volume times heart rate; daily beats equal heart rate times 1440 minutes.
• Steps: Athlete output: 100 times 50 equals 5000 mL per minute — essentially the same as 5040. Athlete daily beats: 50 times 1440 equals 72000. Ordinary daily beats: 72 times 1440 equals 103680. Difference: 103680 minus 72000 equals 31680 fewer beats per day.
• Answer: Nearly identical cardiac output (about 5 litres per minute), but the athlete's heart beats about 31680 fewer times every day.
• Reasoning: Output is a product: the athlete trades rate for volume per beat. The comparison shows why a low resting heart rate in a trained person signals a stronger, not a weaker, heart.
• Mistake to avoid: Concluding the athlete's output must be lower because the heart rate is lower — never judge a product by one factor alone.
• Example 5 — Problem: An adult has 5 litres of blood. (a) How many litres are plasma? (b) Taking the RBC count as 5 million per mm3, estimate the total number of RBCs in the body. (1 litre equals 1000000 mm3.)
• Given: Blood volume 5 litres; plasma is 55 percent; RBC density 5 million per mm3; 5 litres equals 5000000 mm3.
• Principle: Part (a) is a percentage of a volume. Part (b) is density multiplied by total volume.
• Steps: (a) 55 percent of 5 litres equals 0.55 times 5 equals 2.75 litres of plasma (and 2.25 litres of formed elements). (b) 5 million times 5000000 equals 5 times 5 followed by 6 plus 6 zeros: 25 times 10 to the power 12, that is 25000000000000, about 25 trillion RBCs.
• Answer: (a) 2.75 litres of plasma. (b) About 25 trillion (2.5 times 10 to the power 13) red blood cells.
• Reasoning: Both answers are the chapter's percentages and counts used as tools: the same numbers the exam asks you to recall are the numbers that make the body's scale computable.
• Mistake to avoid: Using 45 percent for plasma (that is the formed elements' share), or dropping powers of ten in part (b) — count the zeros in groups: million is 6 zeros, twice over is 12, times 25 gives 25 trillion.
• Example 6 — Problem: An ECG strip shows, in order: a small rounded wave, then a sharp tall spike with small dips on either side, then a moderate rounded wave, repeating regularly. Identify the waves and state the event each one announces.
• Given: Three repeating deflections per beat: small wave, spiked complex, moderate wave.
• Principle: P wave (small) is atrial depolarisation; QRS complex (sharp spike) is ventricular depolarisation; T wave (moderate) is ventricular repolarisation. Contraction follows each depolarisation wave.
• Steps: First deflection, small and rounded: P — the atria are depolarising and are about to contract. Second, the tall spike with dips: QRS — the ventricles are depolarising; ventricular contraction begins just after its Q dip. Third, moderate and rounded: T — the ventricles are repolarising; its end marks the end of ventricular systole.
• Answer: P announces atrial contraction; QRS announces ventricular contraction; T announces ventricular recovery (repolarisation), ending ventricular systole.
• Reasoning: The strip is read by shape first (small, spiked, moderate) and translated by the wave table — shape identifies the wave, the wave identifies the chamber and the event.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling the tall spike the T wave because it is the most prominent — prominence follows ventricular muscle mass, and the spike is the QRS complex; the T wave is the modest dome after it.
• Example 7 — Problem: During hard exercise a student's heart rate rises to 100 beats per minute. (a) What is the duration of one cardiac cycle now? (b) Which phase of the cycle loses the most time, and why does that matter?
• Given: Heart rate 100 per minute; resting cycle 0.8 seconds at 72 per minute with joint diastole 0.4, atrial systole 0.1, ventricular systole 0.3.
• Principle: Cycle duration equals 60 divided by heart rate in seconds. Systole durations change little with rate; diastole absorbs most of the shortening.
• Steps: (a) 60 divided by 100 equals 0.6 seconds per cycle. (b) The cycle has lost 0.2 seconds; since the ventricles must still eject (about 0.3 seconds) and the atria still contract (about 0.1), the loss falls on joint diastole, which shrinks from 0.4 towards 0.2 seconds.
• Answer: (a) 0.6 seconds. (b) Joint diastole shrinks most — and because the heart fills during diastole (and its own coronary blood flow occurs mainly then), very high rates eventually cut filling and output per beat.
• Reasoning: The numbers explain the physiology: there is a ceiling on useful heart rate precisely because the cycle's flexible phase is the filling phase.
• Mistake to avoid: Dividing 100 by 60 instead of 60 by 100 — the cycle in seconds is always 60 over the rate, and at any rate above 60 it must come out below one second.

• Q1. Which plasma protein is converted into fibrin during clotting, which enzyme converts it, and which earlier conversion produces that enzyme?
• Q2. A person's RBCs carry antigen B and the Rh antigen. State the blood group with its Rh sign, the antibodies in the plasma, and one group this person can safely receive blood from other than their own.
• Q3. Why is group O negative called the universal donor while group AB positive is the universal recipient? Answer in terms of antigens and antibodies.
• Q4. Write the conduction pathway of the heartbeat impulse from its origin to the ventricular walls, naming all four structures in order.
• Q5. State the durations of joint diastole, atrial systole and ventricular systole in a 0.8 second cardiac cycle, and name the valve closures that produce the lub and dub sounds.
• Q6. In an ECG, what does the T wave represent, and what does the end of the T wave mark? Which wave would you count to measure heart rate?
• Q7. A ventricle ejects 80 mL per beat at 75 beats per minute. Calculate the cardiac output in litres per minute.
• Q8. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood, yet their names are not mistakes. Explain why, using the naming rule for vessels.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. Fibrinogen is converted to fibrin by thrombin; thrombin itself is produced when thrombokinase (with calcium ions) converts prothrombin into thrombin. Misconception watch: thrombokinase does not act on fibrinogen directly — the cascade has two conversions, and collapsing them into one loses the thrombin step the exam is checking.
• A2. Blood group B positive. Plasma antibody: anti-A. Safe additional donor group: O (negative or positive is not acceptable here — the person is Rh positive, so both O negative and O positive are safe on Rh grounds; the classic safe answer is O). Misconception watch: a B person carries anti-A, not anti-B — the antibody always targets the antigen the person lacks.
• A3. O negative RBCs carry no A, no B and no Rh antigen, so no recipient's antibodies can agglutinate them — it can give to everyone. AB positive plasma carries no anti-A or anti-B antibodies, and being Rh positive it accepts Rh positive blood too — it can receive from everyone. Misconception watch: the labels are not interchangeable courtesies; donor status is earned by having no antigens, recipient status by having no antibodies.
• A4. Sinoatrial node (SAN) to atrioventricular node (AVN) to AV bundle (bundle of His) to Purkinje fibres in the ventricular walls. Misconception watch: the impulse does not spread directly from atria to ventricles through the ordinary muscle — the AV bundle is the only bridge, which is why the AVN delay can exist.
• A5. Joint diastole 0.4 seconds, atrial systole 0.1 seconds, ventricular systole 0.3 seconds. Lub: closure of the tricuspid and bicuspid (AV) valves. Dub: closure of the semilunar valves. Misconception watch: the sounds are valve closures, not the noise of muscle contraction or of blood hitting vessel walls.
• A6. The T wave represents repolarisation of the ventricles; its end marks the end of ventricular systole. Count the QRS complexes per minute to get the heart rate — one complex per beat. Misconception watch: the T wave is not a second depolarisation, and heart rate is not counted from P waves in conduction disorders — the QRS is the beat that ejects blood.
• A7. Cardiac output equals 80 times 75 equals 6000 mL per minute, which is 6 litres per minute. Misconception watch: reporting 6000 litres, or averaging the two numbers instead of multiplying — output is a product of volume per beat and beats per minute.
• A8. Vessels are named by direction relative to the heart: arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins carry blood towards it. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle (away — an artery), and the pulmonary veins enter the left atrium (towards — veins); their oxygen content is simply unusual because the lungs sit between the two sides of the heart. Misconception watch: "arteries always carry oxygenated blood" is false as a rule — it is true only of the systemic circuit, and the pulmonary pair is the standing counterexample.

Plasma proteins divide into fibrinogen (clotting), globulins (defence) and albumins (osmotic balance), and serum is what remains when the clotting factors are spent.
• Summary: Groups are logic, not lists: antigens sit on RBCs, antibodies in plasma mirror the missing antigens, O negative donates to all and AB positive receives from all, and the Rh story adds sensitisation — first Rh positive baby sensitises an Rh negative mother, later ones risk erythroblastosis fetalis unless anti-Rh antibodies are given after the first delivery.
• Summary: The heart is two pumps sharing one box and one wiring system: the SAN fires 70-75 times a minute, the AVN delays, the bundle of His and Purkinje fibres fire the ventricles, and the 0.8 second cycle (0.4 joint diastole, 0.1 atrial systole, 0.3 ventricular systole) repeats with lub and dub marking the valve closures — while the ECG writes the same events as P, QRS and T.
• Summary: Output and loops complete the picture: cardiac output of about 5 litres per minute pushes the whole blood volume through a double circulation — pulmonary and systemic — with the septum guaranteeing zero mixing, the medulla and adrenal hormones tuning the rate, and hypertension, atherosclerosis, angina and heart failure as the failure modes to distinguish precisely.
