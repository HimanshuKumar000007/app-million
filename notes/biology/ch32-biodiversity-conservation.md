Introduction — The Wealth of Life Is Life Itself
• Stand anywhere on Earth and you are surrounded by a census you cannot finish: beetles under the bark, fungi threading the soil, birds you can hear but not see. This chapter is about that variety — how much there is, why it follows patterns, why it is collapsing, and what we can still do.
• DEFINITION — Biodiversity is the combined diversity at all the levels of biological organisation — from genes inside a single species, through the species themselves, up to whole ecosystems. The term was popularised by the sociobiologist Edward Wilson.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — it is built from a short list of stories and numbers examiners recycle: Robert May's estimate, the species pie, the Amazon roll-call, Humboldt's species-area equation, Tilman's plots, the rivet popper hypothesis, the 784-extinction ledger, the Evil Quartet, hotspots and sacred groves. Learn the stories with their numbers attached.
• EXAMPLE — One country, India, holds 8.1 per cent of all the species diversity on the planet on just 2.4 per cent of its land area. That single ratio explains why this chapter keeps returning to India.
• WORKED PATTERN — The reading method for this chapter: every topic answers one of three questions — (1) How much diversity is there? (2) Why should we care? (3) How is it being lost, and how can it be saved? Tag each fact with its question as you read, and revision becomes sorting, not re-learning.
• YOUR TURN — A question asks: which scientist popularised the term biodiversity? Answer: the sociobiologist Edward Wilson — a one-line gift examiners repeat.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The chapter in one breath: life is staggeringly varied and unevenly spread, that variety keeps ecosystems steady and useful, humans are erasing it 100 to 1000 times faster than the background rate, and conservation — in the wild and outside it — is the reply.

Biodiversity — One Word for All the Variety of Life
• DEFINITION — Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety and variability of life — NCERT compresses it into the combined diversity at all levels of biological organisation. It is not just a count of species; it is variety measured at three nested levels.
• The three levels stack like boxes inside boxes: genetic diversity is variety within a species; species diversity is variety among species in a region; ecological diversity is variety among the ecosystems themselves.
• EXAMPLE — A single medicinal plant species differing chemically from valley to valley shows genetic diversity; the Western Ghats holding more kinds of frogs than the Eastern Ghats shows species diversity; India holding deserts, rain forests and coral reefs in one country shows ecological diversity.
• WHY the levels must not be blurred — exam options are built by swapping them: variation inside one species will be offered as "species diversity", the tempting wrong label. Decide which level is changing before reading the options.
• WORKED PATTERN — The level test: ask "what is being counted?" If the answer is gene versions or strains, it is genetic; if it is kinds of organisms, it is species; if it is kinds of habitats or ecosystems, it is ecological. Three questions, three levels — the whole section in one routine.
• YOUR TURN — Classify: a forest contains 40 tree species, 25 bird species and 60 insect species. Which level is being described? Answer: species diversity — you are counting kinds of organisms, not genes and not habitats.
✔ KEY POINT — Biodiversity = genetic diversity + species diversity + ecological diversity, and Edward Wilson is the name attached to the term.

Genetic Diversity — Variety Written Inside a Species
• DEFINITION — Genetic diversity is the variety of genes and their alleles within a single species — the differences between populations and individuals of the same kind.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT's flagship example: the medicinal plant Rauwolfia vomitoria growing in different Himalayan ranges shows genetic variation in the potency and concentration of the active chemical, reserpine, that the plant produces. Same species, same mountain belt — different chemical strength, written in the genes.
• EXAMPLE — India shows the scale of this level at home: more than 50,000 genetically different strains of rice and 1,000 varieties of mango have been recorded from India alone. Every strain is a slightly different genetic edition of the same species.
• WHY genetic diversity matters — a deep gene pool holds spare solutions: some individuals may resist a new disease, a hotter season or a drier soil. A genetically uniform species can fail all at once, because every individual fails the same test the same way.
• WORKED PATTERN — When a question mentions strains, varieties, potency or concentration differences within one named species, file it as genetic diversity without hesitation. The trigger words are inside-species words: strain, variety, breed, potency, concentration.
• YOUR TURN — Two populations of the same fish species differ in a gene that controls salt tolerance. Which diversity level is this, and why does it matter? Answer: genetic diversity — if salinity rises, the tolerant population's genes give the species a survival route that a uniform species would not have.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Rauwolfia vomitoria is the genetic-diversity example; do not upgrade it to species diversity just because different ranges are mentioned. The species stays one — only its chemistry varies.

Species and Ecological Diversity — Kinds and Their Homes
• DEFINITION — Species diversity is the diversity at the species level — the variety of species in a region, often judged by how many species an area holds and how evenly they are represented.
• EXAMPLE — The Western Ghats have a greater amphibian species diversity than the Eastern Ghats. Two neighbouring hill ranges, one country, measurably different counts of frog and toad species — that comparison is NCERT's standard illustration of species diversity.
• DEFINITION — Ecological diversity is the diversity at the ecosystem level — the variety of ecosystems, habitats and ecological processes across a landscape or a country.
• EXAMPLE — Compare India with a Scandinavian country like Norway: India packs deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows into one map, so its ecological diversity is far greater.
• WHY the two levels answer different questions — species diversity tells you how crowded the guest list is; ecological diversity tells you how many different kinds of rooms the guests live in. Conservation must protect both — species in one habitat type is not the same as many habitat types.
• WORKED PATTERN — The counting test again, one level up: if the sentence lists types of organisms (frogs, trees, birds), it is species diversity; if it lists types of places (desert, reef, estuary), it is ecological diversity. Places versus organisms — the fastest discriminator in this chapter.
• YOUR TURN — A survey reports that a district holds grasslands, two lakes, a mangrove patch and a dry forest. Which level is being described? Answer: ecological diversity — the items counted are ecosystems, not species.
✔ KEY POINT — Western Ghats versus Eastern Ghats (amphibians) is the species-diversity pair; India versus Norway (Scandinavia) is the ecological-diversity pair. Learn them as welded pairs.

How Many Species Are There on Earth
• DEFINITION — Species richness described so far: according to the IUCN (2004), the total number of plant and animal species described so far is slightly more than 1.5 million — but that counts only species science has formally named, not the number that exist.
• WHY the true total is unknown — estimates of the undescribed majority range wildly, from about 20 to 50 million, because a huge share of species lives in the tropics, where taxonomic work is still incomplete.
• ★ HIGHLIGHT — Robert May's estimate: the conservative, scientifically sound figure NCERT adopts is Robert May's — he places the global species diversity at about 7 million. When a question asks for the accepted estimate, 7 million is the answer; 1.5 million is only the described count.
• EXAMPLE — Most undescribed species are likely to be small, tropical and short-generationed — insects, fungi and other invertebrates that no taxonomist has yet sorted.
• WORKED PATTERN — The two-number drill: described = a little over 1.5 million (IUCN 2004); estimated total = about 7 million (Robert May). Questions swap these deliberately — one asks "have been described", the other asks "are estimated to exist". Read the verb before choosing the number.
• YOUR TURN — If only about 22 per cent of all species have been recorded so far (the proportion you get from May's estimate), roughly how many described species does that imply out of 7 million? Answer: about 1.5 million — matching the IUCN described count, which is why May's figure is treated as the consistent one.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Never answer "7 million" to "how many species have been described so far" and never answer "1.5 million" to "how many species are estimated to exist". The chapter's favourite swap lives here.

Reading the Species Pie — Who Owns the Planet
• [diagram: Fig. 32.1 — the species pie plus the India panel; read it alongside this section]
• DEFINITION — The species pie is the proportional breakdown of all described species by group: more than 70 per cent of all the species recorded are animals, while plants (including algae, bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms and angiosperms) comprise no more than 22 per cent of the total.
• ★ HIGHLIGHT — Insects rule the pie: among animals, insects are the most species-rich taxonomic group, making up more than 70 per cent of all animal species. In plain words, out of every 10 animals on this planet, 7 are insects.
• EXAMPLE — The surprise guest is fungi: the number of fungi species in the world is more than the combined total of the species of fishes, amphibians, reptiles and mammals. The silent decomposers out-number the entire backboned headline cast put together, fishes included.
• WHY insects dominate — NCERT poses the question itself: what makes insects so successful? Small size, short life cycles and huge reproductive rates let them specialise on tiny niches — one tree can be a whole world to a beetle lineage — and specialisation multiplies species.
• WORKED PATTERN — Pie arithmetic the examiners love: in a random sample of 100 described species, expect roughly 70 animals and about 22 plants; of the 70 animals, roughly 49 or more are insects. If an option claims plants outnumber animals, eliminate it on sight.
• YOUR TURN — A catalogue lists 1,000 described species in the global proportions of this chapter. About how many are insects? Answer: about 70 per cent of species are animals (about 700), and over 70 per cent of animals are insects — so roughly 490 to 500, about half the entire catalogue.
✔ KEY POINT — Animals above 70 per cent, plants no more than 22 per cent, insects above 70 per cent of animals, fungi above fishes plus amphibians plus reptiles plus mammals combined.

India — 8.1 Per Cent of Life on 2.4 Per Cent of Land
• DEFINITION — A mega diversity country is one of a small set of nations holding an exceptionally large share of global species diversity. India qualifies: it has only 2.4 per cent of the world's land area, its share of the global species diversity is an impressive 8.1 per cent — that is what makes India one of the 12 mega diversity countries of the world.
• EXAMPLE — The home census so far: nearly 45,000 species of plants and twice as many of animals — roughly 90,000 animal species — have been recorded from India.
• WHY the undiscovered Indian list is still huge — if only about 22 per cent of species have been recorded so far (Robert May's global proportion), India probably holds more than 1,00,000 plant species and more than 3,00,000 animal species yet to be discovered and described.
• WORKED PATTERN — The India ratio as a one-liner: land share 2.4, species share 8.1 — species density more than three times the world average per unit land. Any option reversing the two percentages (8.1 land, 2.4 species) is the classic trap and can be rejected instantly.
• YOUR TURN — India has recorded about 45,000 plant species but the estimated true total exceeds 1,00,000. What fraction of India's plant diversity is still undescribed, roughly? Answer: over half — about 55,000 or more of the estimated total.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The India numbers as a welded set: 2.4 per cent land, 8.1 per cent species, 12 mega diversity countries, 45,000 plants recorded, about 90,000 animals recorded, over 1,00,000 plants and 3,00,000 animals still waiting.

The Amazon — The Greatest Biodiversity on Earth
• DEFINITION — The Amazon rain forest of South America is the largest tropical rain forest and, in NCERT's words, has the greatest biodiversity on earth.
• EXAMPLE — The Amazon roll-call, worth memorising as a chant: at least 40,000 species of plants, 3,000 species of fishes, 1,300 species of birds, 427 species of mammals, 427 species of amphibians, 378 species of reptiles and more than 1,25,000 species of invertebrates.
• WHY these numbers keep growing — scientists estimate that the Amazon may be home to about two million insect species that are yet to be discovered and named.
• WORKED PATTERN — Order-of-magnitude check: invertebrates (over 1,25,000) dwarf every vertebrate group in the Amazon list, exactly as the species pie predicts; if an option puts mammals on top, Section 5 exposes the error.
• YOUR TURN — Which single Amazon group outnumbers all its vertebrate groups combined? Answer: the invertebrates, at more than 1,25,000 species — against roughly 5,400 vertebrates in total across fishes, birds, mammals, amphibians and reptiles.
✔ KEY POINT — Amazon chant: 40,000 plants; 3,000 fishes; 1,300 birds; 427 mammals; 427 amphibians; 378 reptiles; over 1,25,000 invertebrates; about two million insects still unnamed.

Latitudinal Gradients — Rich at the Equator, Poor at the Poles
• [diagram: Fig. 32.2 — diversity falling from equator to poles; keep this picture open while reading]
• DEFINITION — The latitudinal gradient is the global pattern in which species diversity decreases as we move away from the equator towards the poles. The tropics — the band roughly between 23.5 degrees North and 23.5 degrees South — harbour more species than temperate or polar regions.
• EXAMPLE — The bird ladder makes the gradient concrete: Colombia, located near the equator, has nearly 1,400 species of birds, while New York at 41 degrees North has only 105 species and Greenland at 71 degrees North has just 56 species. Same planet, same group of animals — latitude alone explains most of the fall.
• EXAMPLE — A tropical country like India, falling largely in the tropics, has more than 1,200 species of birds. And a forest in Ecuador, a tropical country, has up to 10 times as many species of vascular plants as a forest of equal area in the temperate mid-west of the USA.
• WHY the pattern is a gradient and not a cliff — diversity drains steadily with latitude because the drivers change steadily: less solar energy, harsher seasons and a history of repeated glaciations as you leave the tropics.
• WORKED PATTERN — The latitude drill: any question giving you a place, your first move is to ask "how far from the equator?" Equatorial Colombia 1,400; mid-latitude New York 105; polar Greenland 56. If options rank a polar region richer than a tropical one, the gradient rejects them.
• YOUR TURN — A survey finds 10 times more vascular plant species in an Ecuadorian forest than in an equal area of temperate American forest. Is this surprising? Answer: no — it is NCERT's own comparison, and it is the latitudinal gradient expressed in plants instead of birds.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The bird numbers are welded to their places: Colombia 1,400, New York 105, Greenland 56. Examiners swap Greenland and New York freely; learn the ladder in order, richest at the equator.

Why the Tropics Win — Time, Stability and Sunshine
• DEFINITION — The latitudinal gradient needs a mechanism, and NCERT gives three reasons the tropics out-produce the rest of the world in species. Learn them as a numbered trio, because questions ask for "which of the following is NOT a reason" and plant one outsider.
• Reason one, evolutionary time — tropical environments, unlike temperate ones, are relatively undisturbed for millions of years, so tropics had a long evolutionary time for species diversification. Temperate regions suffered frequent glaciations, repeatedly resetting their communities.
• Reason two, stability — tropical environments are less seasonal, more constant and more predictable than temperate ones. Such constant environments promote niche specialisation, and specialisation packs more species into the same space — that leads to a greater species diversity.
• Reason three, energy — there is more solar energy available in the tropics, which contributes to higher productivity, and higher productivity can in turn contribute indirectly to greater diversity: more energy supports more individuals, and more individuals can be divided among more species.
• WHY all three are needed — time alone would fill a museum, not a living forest; stability lets specialists survive side by side; energy feeds the crowd. Remove any one and the tropical advantage shrinks.
• EXAMPLE — The glacier test makes reason one visible: temperate forests were scraped and reset by repeated ice ages, while an equatorial forest kept accumulating specialist species through the same period, undisturbed.
• WORKED PATTERN — Reason matching: "undisturbed for millions of years" means time; "less seasonal, constant, predictable" means stability; "more solar energy, higher productivity" means energy. Three phrases, three reasons — match the phrase, win the mark.
• YOUR TURN — A question claims the tropics are richer because they have harsher winters that force adaptation. Verdict? Answer: false — the NCERT mechanism is the opposite; freedom from repeated glaciations and harsh seasonality is what the tropics enjoyed.
✔ KEY POINT — Tropics win on time (undisturbed millions of years), stability (less seasonal, niche specialisation) and energy (more solar input, higher productivity).

Species-Area Relationships — Humboldt's Curve
• DEFINITION — The species-area relationship says that within a region, species richness increases with increasing explored area, but only up to a limit. Sample a bigger piece of a region and you keep meeting new species — at first quickly, then ever more slowly.
• EXAMPLE — The observer is Alexander von Humboldt, the German naturalist and geographer who noticed this pattern during his explorations of South American jungles.
• The curve's shape: plotted for groups as different as angiosperms, birds, bats and freshwater fishes, richness against area gives a rectangular hyperbola — steep at first, bending over as the region's species pool is gradually exhausted.
• WHY the curve bends — the first hectares of a region are full of unseen species; later hectares mostly re-show species already counted. New area adds new species only while it adds new habitats and new corners of the pool.
• WORKED PATTERN — Reading any species-area graph: the bend is the message — richness rises with area but saturates. If an option says richness grows in a straight line forever, the hyperbola rejects it.
• YOUR TURN — Two surveys sample the same forest, one over 1 hectare and one over 100 hectares. Which finds more species, and will it find 100 times more? Answer: the 100-hectare survey finds more, but far fewer than 100 times more — the hyperbola bends, because most of the pool is already met in the first hectares.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Humboldt, South American jungles, richness rises with area up to a limit, curve is a rectangular hyperbola — four welds on one fact.

The Species-Area Equation — Slopes, Z Values and Meaning
• [diagram: Fig. 32.3 — the hyperbola straightened on a log scale, with the two Z bands marked]
• DEFINITION — On a logarithmic scale, the species-area relationship becomes a straight line described by the equation: log S = log C + Z log A. Here S is species richness, A is area, Z is the slope of the line (the regression coefficient) and C is the Y-intercept.
• ★ HIGHLIGHT — The ordinary Z band: analyses across a wide variety of taxa show the slope Z lies in a narrow range of 0.1 to 0.2, regardless of the taxonomic group or the region — whether it is plants in Britain, birds in California or molluscs in New York State, the slopes are amazingly similar.
• The continental Z band: when the explored area is very large — like entire continents — the slope is much steeper, with Z values in the range of 0.6 to 1.2. For frugivorous (fruit-eating) birds and mammals in the tropical forests of different continents, the slope is found to be 1.15.
• EXAMPLE — Molluscs in New York State, birds in California and plants in Britain all return slopes inside the same 0.1 to 0.2 band, although the groups and regions could hardly differ more.
• WHY big areas steepen the line — crossing into a new continent means crossing into a new evolutionary theatre: whole pools of endemic species that share nothing with the last area. Small areas resample one pool (flat slope); continents replace the pool (steep slope).
• WORKED PATTERN — A clean computation: suppose Z is 0.2 and area increases 100 times. On the log scale, log S rises by Z times log A increase, that is 0.2 times 2, which is 0.4, so S rises by 10 raised to 0.4 — about 2.5 times. A hundredfold area buys only about a two-and-a-half-fold richness — the bend expressed in numbers.
• YOUR TURN — A survey of birds across whole continents returns Z = 1.15. Which band does it belong to, and does that fit? Answer: the continental band, 0.6 to 1.2 — and 1.15 is NCERT's exact value for frugivorous birds and mammals across continents, so it fits perfectly.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — C is the Y-intercept and Z is the slope; options swap the two letters. And the log equation is a straight line only on the log scale — on ordinary axes the same relationship is the rectangular hyperbola of the previous section.

Why Species Diversity Keeps an Ecosystem Steady
• DEFINITION — A stable community is one that holds together through time and trouble. Ecologists have long believed that communities with more species generally tend to be more stable than those with fewer species.
• The three marks of a stable community: first, it should not show too much variation in productivity from year to year. Second, it must be resistant or resilient to occasional disturbances, whether natural or man-made. Third, it must be resistant to invasions by alien species.
• WHY variety steadies the system — with many species, the failure of one is buffered by others doing overlapping jobs: if one pollinator declines, another still visits the flowers. With few species, every loss is structural, and productivity swings wildly.
• EXAMPLE — Two meadows, one drought year: the species-rich meadow loses its thirstiest plants but its deep-rooted species keep producing, so total biomass dips gently. The species-poor meadow, built on one thirsty grass, crashes — same drought, different stability, decided by the species count.
• WORKED PATTERN — Stability questions are definition questions in disguise: the three marks (steady productivity, resistance or resilience to disturbance, resistance to alien invasion) are the answer template. Any option defining stability as "species never change" or "no species ever goes extinct locally" is overclaiming.
• YOUR TURN — A community's productivity swings violently from year to year and an introduced weed takes over large patches. How many of the three stability marks is it failing? Answer: two — it varies too much in productivity and it is not resistant to invasion by an alien species.
✔ KEY POINT — Stability means steady year-to-year productivity, resistance or resilience to disturbance, and resistance to alien invasions — and richer communities tend to score better on all three.

Tilman's Plots and the Rivet Popper Hypothesis
• DEFINITION — David Tilman's long-term ecosystem experiments, run using outdoor plots, are NCERT's evidence that diversity drives stability: he found that plots with more species showed less year-to-year variation in total biomass, and he also showed that increased diversity contributed to higher productivity.
• WHY outdoor plots convince — Tilman did not argue from a model; he watched real planted communities through many seasons, and the diverse plots' totals stayed steadier — and higher — even as individual species rose and fell.
• EXAMPLE — Picture two of Tilman's plots through one drought: the species-poor plot halves its biomass when its dominant grass wilts, while the species-rich plot leans on its drought-tolerant members and barely dips.
• DEFINITION — The rivet popper hypothesis, proposed by Stanford ecologist Paul Ehrlich, compares an aeroplane to an ecosystem and species to the rivets that hold the plane together — thousands of rivets joining every part.
• The story in motion: if every passenger travelling in the plane starts popping a rivet to take home — that is, causes a species to become extinct — it may not affect flight safety (ecosystem functioning) initially, but as more and more rivets are removed, the plane becomes dangerously weak over a period of time.
• ★ HIGHLIGHT — Not all rivets are equal: loss of rivets on the wings — key species that drive major ecosystem functions — is obviously a more serious threat to flight safety than loss of a few rivets on the seats or windows inside the plane. Extinction of a keystone species is the wing-rivet case.
• WORKED PATTERN — Analogy mapping is the whole game in rivet questions: plane = ecosystem, rivets = species, popping a rivet = extinction, weakening plane = loss of ecosystem function, wing rivets = key species. Map first, then answer — options scramble the pairs deliberately.
• YOUR TURN — In Ehrlich's analogy, what does removing one rivet from a window frame represent, and why is it less alarming than a wing rivet? Answer: the extinction of an ordinary species with a modest functional role; the plane flies on, whereas wing rivets are key species whose loss threatens the whole system's working.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Tilman goes with plots, biomass and stability; Ehrlich goes with rivets, aeroplane and key species. Swapping the two names is the single most repeated error in this chapter — weld each name to his experiment.

The Loss Ledger — Extinctions Already on Record
• [diagram: Fig. 32.5 — the extinction ledger: 784 gone in 500 years, the famous names, the threatened queue]
• DEFINITION — The IUCN Red List (2004) is the world register of threatened and extinct species, and its ledger for our era is grim: it documents the extinction of 784 species in the last 500 years — including 338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates and 87 plants.
• EXAMPLE — The famous names on the ledger: the dodo from Mauritius, the quagga from Africa, the thylacine from Australia, Steller's sea cow from Russia, and three subspecies of tiger — Bali, Javan and Caspian. The last twenty years alone have witnessed the disappearance of 27 species.
• EXAMPLE — The ledger's earlier pages are worse: the colonisation of tropical Pacific Islands by humans is said to have led to the extinction of more than 2,000 species of native birds. Humanity was erasing species long before it kept records.
• WHY the ledger undercounts — extinction is documented only when the species was described and someone was watching. Most lost species were probably never named, so recorded numbers are the visible tip of a much larger loss.
• WORKED PATTERN — Ledger arithmetic: 338 plus 359 plus 87 equals 784 — if an option offers a split that does not add to the stated total, it is false regardless of how familiar the numbers look. And note the shape: invertebrates, not vertebrates, are the largest block of the recorded extinctions.
• YOUR TURN — A species vanishes from an unexplored tropical forest before any scientist describes it. Does it appear in the IUCN ledger, and what does that imply? Answer: no — the ledger records described species, so true extinction totals must exceed the documented 784.
✔ KEY POINT — 784 species in 500 years (338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates, 87 plants), 27 gone in the last twenty years, over 2,000 Pacific island birds lost earlier — the recorded ledger, and a floor, not a ceiling.

The Sixth Extinction — A Faster Extinction Than Any Before
• DEFINITION — The fossil record shows that life on earth has seen five episodes of mass extinction of species. The current, human-driven wave is the sixth extinction — different from the five before it not in kind, but in speed.
• ★ HIGHLIGHT — The speed gap: current species extinction rates are estimated to be 100 to 1,000 times faster than in pre-human times, and our activities are responsible for this faster rate. Five natural extinctions were geological events; the sixth is a biological one with a return address.
• The warning that follows: ecologists warn that if the present trends continue, nearly half of all the species on earth might be wiped out within the next 100 years.
• The threatened queue: more than 15,500 species worldwide are presently facing the threat of extinction. Presently 12 per cent of all bird species, 23 per cent of all mammal species, 32 per cent of all amphibian species and 31 per cent of all gymnosperm species in the world face the threat of extinction.
• WHY amphibians lead the danger table — they live double lives in water and on land, with permeable skin and unshelled eggs, so pollution, habitat change and disease strike from every side. Their 32 per cent is a warning light for the whole system.
• EXAMPLE — The dodo's recorded arc fits inside the modern era: a species unknown to science before sailors reached Mauritius was gone within about a century of contact — background extinction never works at that speed.
• WORKED PATTERN — Rate questions are ratio questions: background rate versus 100 to 1,000 times faster. If an option says current rates are "about ten times" or "comparable to" pre-human rates, it understates NCERT by orders of magnitude — reject it.
• YOUR TURN — Why is the sixth extinction called different from the previous five, when all six involve mass species loss? Answer: its speed and its cause — it is running 100 to 1,000 times faster than the pre-human background rate, driven by one species' activities rather than planetary geology.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Five mass extinctions have already happened; the present crisis is the sixth, and it is not over. Options that call the current event "the fifth extinction" are miscounting by design.

The Evil Quartet — The Four Causes of Loss
• [diagram: Fig. 32.4 — the Evil Quartet with its flagship examples; use it as the cause-spotting flowchart]
• DEFINITION — Accelerated rates of species extinction that the world is facing now are largely due to human activities, and NCERT groups the causes into four major ones, nicknamed The Evil Quartet: habitat loss and fragmentation, over-exploitation, alien species invasion and co-extinction.
• WHY a quartet and not a list of twenty — almost every documented extinction can be filed under one of these four mechanisms, so the examiners test them as a set: name all four, match a scenario to its cause, or spot the outsider planted among them (pollution, however real, is not a member of NCERT's named quartet).
• EXAMPLE — One line per member, to be expanded next: forests cut and splintered (habitat loss and fragmentation); the passenger pigeon hunted out of existence (over-exploitation); the Nile perch devouring Lake Victoria's cichlids (alien species invasion); a host fish dying and taking its unique parasites with it (co-extinction).
• WORKED PATTERN — Cause-spotting routine: ask "what exactly killed them?" If the home was destroyed or split, cause one. If humans harvested them directly, cause two. If a newcomer species did the damage, cause three. If they died because a partner species died first, cause four. Every scenario resolves with that question.
• YOUR TURN — A medicinal plant is collected from the wild far faster than it can regrow, and its wild population crashes. Quartet member? Answer: over-exploitation — the killing mechanism is direct human harvest, not habitat change or an invader.
✔ KEY POINT — The Evil Quartet, in order: habitat loss and fragmentation; over-exploitation; alien species invasion; co-extinction. Four causes, four mechanisms, one question — what exactly killed them.

Habitat Loss and Fragmentation — The Biggest Killer
• DEFINITION — Habitat loss and fragmentation is the destruction of natural habitat, and the breaking of large habitats into small fragments by human activities. NCERT names it plainly: this is the most important cause driving animals and plants to extinction.
• EXAMPLE — The shrinking green blanket: tropical rain forests once covered more than 14 per cent of the earth's land surface and now cover no more than 6 per cent, and they are being destroyed fast. More than half the richest habitat type on earth has gone within the modern era.
• EXAMPLE — The Amazon again, now as a warning: the huge Amazonian rain forest — called the lungs of the planet because of the oxygen it releases — is being cut and cleared for cultivating soya beans, or for conversion to grasslands for raising beef cattle.
• DEFINITION — Fragmentation is the second blade of the same scissors: when large habitats are broken up into small fragments, mammals and birds requiring large territories, and certain animals with migratory habits, are badly affected — leading to population declines even in surviving fragments.
• WHY fragments fail — a fragment is too small for a wide-ranging animal's breeding population, too edged to keep its interior climate, and too isolated for migrants to move between patches. Species can be doomed in a fragment before the last tree falls.
• WORKED PATTERN — Scenario drill: "a highway splits a forest; tiger numbers fall though no tiger is hunted and half the forest still stands." Answer: habitat loss and fragmentation — territory broken up, not harvesting or invasion. Hunt for keywords: cut, cleared, converted, fragmented, large territories, migratory.
• YOUR TURN — Rain forest cover fell from over 14 per cent to about 6 per cent of land surface. Express the loss: roughly what fraction of the original rain forest is gone? Answer: more than half — about eight of every fourteen original units, leaving under half standing.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Lungs of the planet" belongs to the Amazon forest in NCERT. And note the pair 14 per cent to 6 per cent — options reverse them to claim forests are growing; the arrow runs one way only, downward.

Over-exploitation and Alien Species Invasion
• DEFINITION — Over-exploitation is harvesting a species faster than it can replenish itself. NCERT frames it morally and precisely: humans have always depended on nature for food and shelter, but when need turns to greed, it leads to over-exploitation of natural resources.
• EXAMPLE — The over-hunted: many species extinctions in the last 500 years, like Steller's sea cow and the passenger pigeon, were due to over-exploitation. The pattern continues underwater — presently many marine fish populations around the world are over-harvested, endangering the continued existence of some commercially important species.
• DEFINITION — Alien species invasion: when alien species are introduced unintentionally or deliberately for whatever purpose, some of them turn invasive and cause the decline or extinction of indigenous species — species that evolved without any defence against the newcomer.
• WHY invaders win — indigenous species evolved alongside their own predators and competitors, not against the newcomer, so they often have no defence, no escape behaviour and no resistance ready when an alien arrives.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT's flagship invasion: the Nile perch, introduced into Lake Victoria in East Africa, eventually led to the extinction of an ecologically unique assemblage of more than 200 species of cichlid fish in the lake. One introduced predator, an entire evolutionary radiation erased.
• EXAMPLE — The plant invaders of India: the carrot grass Parthenium, Lantana and water hyacinth (Eichhornia) are invasive weeds that threaten native species. And the African catfish, Clarias gariepinus, recently introduced for aquaculture purposes, is posing a threat to the indigenous catfishes in our rivers.
• WORKED PATTERN — Keep the two mechanisms apart with one question: did humans take the victims themselves (over-exploitation) or did humans release the thing that took them (invasion)? Steller's sea cow was shot and eaten — over-exploitation. The cichlids were eaten by an introduced fish — invasion.
• YOUR TURN — A foreign ornamental plant escapes from gardens and smothers a lake's native vegetation. Quartet member, and which NCERT weed behaves this way? Answer: alien species invasion — the water hyacinth, Eichhornia, is the chapter's lake-smothering example.
✔ KEY POINT — Need turning to greed is over-exploitation (Steller's sea cow, passenger pigeon); introduced species turning invasive is the Nile perch (over 200 cichlids), Parthenium, Lantana, Eichhornia and Clarias gariepinus.

Co-extinction and What a Region Loses With Its Species
• DEFINITION — Co-extinction is the quartet's quietest member: when a species becomes extinct, the plant and animal species associated with it in an obligatory way also become extinct. The victim never meets a human; it dies because its partner died first.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT's two clean cases: when a host fish species becomes extinct, its unique assemblage of parasites also meets the same fate. And in a co-evolved plant-pollinator mutualism, the extinction of one invariably leads to the extinction of the other.
• WHY co-extinction multiplies the ledger — the recorded 784 count only the named victims. Every obligate partner, parasite and specialist that depended on them is an unrecorded second wave, so one extinction is usually several, stacked.
• DEFINITION — The consequences of regional loss: loss of biodiversity in a region may lead to a decline in plant production, lowered resistance to environmental perturbations such as drought, and increased variability in certain ecosystem processes such as plant productivity, water use and pest and disease cycles.
• WORKED PATTERN — Read those three consequences as Tilman's stability marks played in reverse: production falls, disturbance resistance falls, variability rises. Loss questions and stability questions are the same coin.
• YOUR TURN — A fig species disappears from a forest; its species-specific pollinator wasp dies out soon after, though nobody harmed the wasp. Which quartet member is this? Answer: co-extinction — the wasp was in an obligatory association with the fig, so the fig's extinction became the wasp's.
✔ KEY POINT — Co-extinction: obligatory partners share one fate — host fish and its parasites, plant and its co-evolved pollinator. And regional loss shows up as falling production, drought vulnerability and wilder swings in ecosystem processes.

Why Conserve — Utilitarian and Ethical Arguments
• DEFINITION — The reasons to conserve biodiversity are grouped by NCERT into three categories: narrowly utilitarian, broadly utilitarian and ethical. The first two are about what nature does for us; the third is about what we owe nature.
• DEFINITION — Narrowly utilitarian arguments are the direct economic benefits from nature: humans derive countless direct benefits — food (cereals, pulses, fruits), firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins and perfumes) and products of medicinal importance.
• EXAMPLE — The pharmacy in the forest: more than 25 per cent of the drugs currently sold in the market worldwide are derived from plants, and 25,000 species of plants contribute to the traditional medicines used by native peoples. Exploring molecular, genetic and species-level diversity for valuable products is called bioprospecting — and nobody knows what cures sit unread in undescribed species.
• DEFINITION — Broadly utilitarian arguments rest on ecosystem services. Biodiversity plays a major role in many ecosystem services that nature provides; lose the species and the service fails silently.
• EXAMPLE — The Amazon forest is estimated to produce, through photosynthesis, 20 per cent of the total oxygen in the earth's atmosphere — a broadly utilitarian service you cannot buy. Pollination is the twin example: bees, bumblebees, birds and bats pollinate crops and wild plants, work we would otherwise have to do, and pay for, ourselves.
• DEFINITION — The ethical argument: every species has an intrinsic value, even if it is currently of no economic value to us. We have a moral duty to care for the well-being of every species and pass on our biological legacy in good order to future generations.
• WHY three categories and not one — some benefits can be priced (products), some are noticed only when they fail (services), and some are not benefits at all but duties; questions test all three registers, so keep them separate.
• WORKED PATTERN — Category sorting: "yields a new drug" is narrowly utilitarian (direct, marketable); "produces oxygen" or "pollinates crops" is broadly utilitarian (a service of the whole system); "has a right to exist" is ethical. Sort by whether money changes hands, whether a service flows, or whether duty speaks.
• YOUR TURN — A tribe uses a bark extract as its traditional fever medicine. Which argument category does conserving that tree fall under, and what makes it different from oxygen production? Answer: narrowly utilitarian — a direct product of known use; oxygen is a broadly utilitarian ecosystem service, not a product taken from one species.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Oxygen production by the Amazon is broadly utilitarian, not narrowly — it is a service, not a harvested product. And 25 per cent refers to drugs derived from plants; 25,000 refers to plant species in traditional medicine. Two similar numbers, two different facts.

In Situ Conservation — Protecting Life Where It Lives
• [diagram: Fig. 32.6 — the conservation map: in situ strongholds on one side, ex situ safety net on the other]
• DEFINITION — In situ conservation means protecting species in their natural habitats — on site, inside the ecosystems where they evolved. The whole community is guarded, not one species at a time, so the web of interactions is conserved along with its members.
• The problem that shapes the strategy: threatened species far outnumber the facilities available to protect them one by one. The practical answer is to identify regions of maximum diversity and endemism and protect them hardest — the hotspot approach.
• DEFINITION — Biodiversity hotspots are regions characterised by very high levels of species richness and a high degree of endemism (species confined to that region and found nowhere else), and they are also regions of accelerated habitat loss.
• WHY hotspots first — conservation money and land are limited, so triage applies: protecting the places with the most unique species, disappearing fastest, saves the most diversity per unit of effort.
• ★ HIGHLIGHT — The hotspot ledger: initially 25 biodiversity hotspots were identified, but subsequently nine more were added, bringing the total number in the world to 34. Together they cover less than 2 per cent of the earth's land area, yet strict protection of them could reduce the ongoing mass extinctions by almost 30 per cent.
• EXAMPLE — Three of these hotspots cover India's exceptionally high biodiversity regions: the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma and the Himalaya. If a question asks how many hotspots touch India, the answer is three — and the names are the follow-up.
• EXAMPLE — India's protected-area network beyond hotspots: India now has 14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks and 448 wildlife sanctuaries.
• EXAMPLE — Sacred groves are India's oldest in situ device: a tract of forest preserved wholly because it is sacred to a local deity — not a branch cut, not a leaf taken. The Khasi and Jaintia Hills of Meghalaya are the famous examples; sacred groves also survive in the Aravalli Hills of Rajasthan, the Western Ghat regions of Karnataka and Maharashtra, and the Sarguja, Chanda and Bastar areas of Madhya Pradesh. They are the last refuges for many rare and threatened plants.
• WORKED PATTERN — In situ or not? Ask one question: does the species stay in its natural habitat? National park, sanctuary, biosphere reserve, hotspot, sacred grove — yes, in situ. If the species has been moved into human care, it has crossed to ex situ, whatever the signboard says.
• YOUR TURN — A temple forest in Meghalaya has remained untouched for centuries because felling there is taboo. Which conservation approach is this, and which hills made it famous in NCERT? Answer: in situ conservation through a sacred grove — the Khasi and Jaintia Hills of Meghalaya.
✔ KEY POINT — In situ = protect in place: 34 hotspots (25 plus 9), three covering India (Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, Himalaya); 14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks, 448 sanctuaries; sacred groves guard the rarest plants.

Ex Situ Conservation — The Safety Net Outside the Wild
• DEFINITION — Ex situ conservation means protecting threatened animals and plants outside their natural habitat — off site, in special settings where they can be protected and given special care by humans.
• EXAMPLE — The classic addresses: zoological parks, botanical gardens and wildlife safari parks all serve as ex situ conservation. The species has left home, but it is alive, guarded and breeding.
• The modern upgrades: advanced methods now let us preserve gametes of threatened species in viable and fertile condition for long periods using cryopreservation techniques; eggs can be fertilised in vitro; and plants can be propagated using tissue culture methods.
• EXAMPLE — The seed vault: seeds of different genetic strains of commercially important plants can be kept for long periods in seed banks — a frozen library of genetic diversity — Section 2's rice strains and mango varieties, stored against catastrophe.
• WHY both nets are needed — in situ protects whole ecosystems but cannot save a species whose numbers have already crashed or whose habitat is gone. Ex situ rescues exactly those species, and can later feed individuals back into the wild.
• WORKED PATTERN — The ex situ checklist: zoo, botanical garden, safari park, seed bank, cryopreservation, tissue culture, in vitro fertilisation of eggs. Every item shares one trait — the organism or its material is in human care, outside the natural habitat. Spot that trait and the classification is done.
• YOUR TURN — Sperms and eggs of a critically endangered rhino are frozen in liquid nitrogen for future use. In situ or ex situ, and by which technique? Answer: ex situ, by cryopreservation of gametes — NCERT's textbook example of the newest conservation method.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Wildlife safari parks are ex situ, although the animals look free-ranging; botanical gardens are ex situ, although the plants stand in soil. The test is not how natural it looks but whether it is the natural habitat — in every listed garden, zoo and park, it is not.

The World Joins Hands — Rio 1992 and Johannesburg 2002
• DEFINITION — Biodiversity knows no political boundaries, and its conservation is therefore the collective responsibility of all nations. That principle produced this chapter's two closing summits.
• EXAMPLE — The Earth Summit: the historic Convention on Biological Diversity, held in Rio de Janeiro in 1992, called upon all nations to take appropriate measures for conservation of biodiversity and for sustainable utilisation of its benefits.
• EXAMPLE — The follow-up: the World Summit on Sustainable Development, held in 2002 in Johannesburg, South Africa, where 190 countries pledged their commitment to achieve, by 2010, a significant reduction in the current rate of biodiversity loss at global, regional and local levels.
• WHY the pair is always asked together — Rio made the promise; Johannesburg set the deadline. Examiners weld year to city to event: 1992 with Rio and the Convention, 2002 with Johannesburg, 190 countries and the 2010 target.
• WORKED PATTERN — Summit sorting: if the stem mentions "Convention on Biological Diversity" or "called upon all nations", it is Rio 1992. If it mentions a pledge, a reduction target and the year 2010, it is Johannesburg 2002. The verb is the discriminator — convening versus pledging.
• YOUR TURN — Which summit saw 190 countries pledge to cut the rate of biodiversity loss by 2010? Answer: the World Summit on Sustainable Development, Johannesburg, 2002 — a decade after Rio's Earth Summit wrote the Convention.
✔ KEY POINT — Rio de Janeiro 1992 = Earth Summit, Convention on Biological Diversity. Johannesburg 2002 = World Summit on Sustainable Development, 190 countries pledged, target year 2010.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Biodiversity is the combined diversity at all levels of biological organisation — genetic, species and ecological — and the term was popularised by sociobiologist Edward Wilson.
✔ KEY POINT — Genetic diversity: variation within a species. Rauwolfia vomitoria from different Himalayan ranges varies in potency and concentration of reserpine; India holds over 50,000 strains of rice and 1,000 varieties of mango.
✔ KEY POINT — Species diversity is counted in kinds of organisms (Western Ghats have greater amphibian diversity than Eastern Ghats); ecological diversity is counted in kinds of ecosystems (India's deserts, rain forests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows far exceed Norway's).
✔ KEY POINT — Described versus estimated: IUCN (2004) records slightly more than 1.5 million species described; Robert May's conservative estimate of the true global total is about 7 million.
✔ KEY POINT — The species pie: animals form more than 70 per cent of recorded species, plants no more than 22 per cent; insects are over 70 per cent of animals — 7 of every 10 animals; fungi species outnumber fishes plus amphibians plus reptiles plus mammals combined.
✔ KEY POINT — India: only 2.4 per cent of world land, but 8.1 per cent of global species diversity — one of the 12 mega diversity countries; nearly 45,000 plant and about 90,000 animal species recorded, with over 1,00,000 plant and 3,00,000 animal species probably still undescribed.
✔ KEY POINT — The Amazon has the greatest biodiversity on earth: at least 40,000 plants, 3,000 fishes, 1,300 birds, 427 mammals, 427 amphibians, 378 reptiles and over 1,25,000 invertebrates, with about two million insect species still unnamed.
✔ KEY POINT — Latitudinal gradient: diversity falls from equator to poles — Colombia about 1,400 bird species, New York (41 degrees North) 105, Greenland (71 degrees North) 56. The tropics win because of long undisturbed evolutionary time, a less seasonal and more constant environment promoting niche specialisation, and more solar energy driving higher productivity.
✔ KEY POINT — Species-area relation (Humboldt): richness rises with area up to a limit, forming a rectangular hyperbola; on a log scale, log S = log C + Z log A, with Z between 0.1 and 0.2 for ordinary areas and 0.6 to 1.2 for continents — 1.15 for frugivorous birds and mammals across tropical forest continents.
✔ KEY POINT — Stability and diversity: stable communities keep productivity steady, resist or recover from disturbances, and resist alien invasions. Tilman's outdoor plots: more species meant less year-to-year variation in total biomass and higher productivity. Ehrlich's rivet popper hypothesis: species are rivets of the aeroplane-ecosystem, and wing rivets are key species.
✔ KEY POINT — The loss ledger: IUCN Red List (2004) records 784 species extinct in the last 500 years — 338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates, 87 plants — including the dodo (Mauritius), quagga (Africa), thylacine (Australia), Steller's sea cow (Russia) and the Bali, Javan and Caspian tiger subspecies; 27 species vanished in twenty years, and over 2,000 native Pacific island birds were lost earlier.
✔ KEY POINT — The sixth extinction is running 100 to 1,000 times faster than pre-human rates, driven by human activity; over 15,500 species face the threat of extinction (12 per cent of birds, 23 per cent of mammals, 32 per cent of amphibians, 31 per cent of gymnosperms), and half of all species could be wiped out within 100 years if trends continue.
✔ KEY POINT — The Evil Quartet: habitat loss and fragmentation (the most important cause; rain forests down from over 14 to about 6 per cent of land), over-exploitation (Steller's sea cow, passenger pigeon), alien species invasion (Nile perch and over 200 Lake Victoria cichlids; Parthenium, Lantana, Eichhornia; Clarias gariepinus), and co-extinction (host fish and its parasites; plant and its co-evolved pollinator).
✔ KEY POINT — Why conserve: narrowly utilitarian (direct products — food, firewood, fibre, tannins, dyes, perfumes; over 25 per cent of marketed drugs come from plants, 25,000 plant species serve traditional medicine; bioprospecting), broadly utilitarian (ecosystem services — the Amazon produces about 20 per cent of the earth's atmospheric oxygen; pollination by bees, birds and bats), and ethical (every species has intrinsic value; our legacy must pass to future generations).
✔ KEY POINT — Conservation practice: in situ protects life in place — 34 hotspots (25 plus 9; Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma and Himalaya cover India; all hotspots cover under 2 per cent of land yet strict protection could cut mass extinctions by almost 30 per cent), plus 14 biosphere reserves, 90 national parks, 448 wildlife sanctuaries and sacred groves (the Khasi and Jaintia Hills of Meghalaya head the list). Ex situ protects life in care — zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks, seed banks, tissue culture and cryopreservation of gametes.
✔ KEY POINT — The world response: Earth Summit (Convention on Biological Diversity), Rio de Janeiro, 1992; World Summit on Sustainable Development, Johannesburg, 2002 — 190 countries pledged to significantly reduce the rate of biodiversity loss by 2010 at global, regional and local levels.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Answering 7 million when asked how many species have been described. Why it is wrong: about 7 million is Robert May's estimate of species that exist; the described count is slightly more than 1.5 million (IUCN 2004). Correct principle: described 1.5 million, estimated 7 million — match the number to the verb in the question.
• Mistake 2: Calling the Rauwolfia example species diversity. Why it is wrong: Rauwolfia vomitoria remains one species; only the potency and concentration of reserpine vary between Himalayan populations. Correct principle: variation inside a species is genetic diversity, however many locations are named.
• Mistake 3: Reversing India's percentages into "8.1 per cent land, 2.4 per cent species". Why it is wrong: India is land-poor and species-rich — 2.4 per cent of the world's land carries 8.1 per cent of its species. Correct principle: the small number is land, the big number is diversity; that imbalance is the whole point.
• Mistake 4: Putting Z between 0.6 and 1.2 for small study areas. Why it is wrong: 0.1 to 0.2 is the band for ordinary areas — plants in Britain, birds in California, molluscs in New York; 0.6 to 1.2 belongs to whole continents. Correct principle: small areas flat slopes, continents steep slopes, frugivores at 1.15.
• Mistake 5: Saying the species-area curve is a straight line, full stop. Why it is wrong: on ordinary axes it is a rectangular hyperbola; it straightens only on a logarithmic scale, where log S = log C + Z log A applies. Correct principle: hyperbola on paper, straight line on logs.
• Mistake 6: Crediting the rivet popper hypothesis to Tilman or the plot experiments to Ehrlich. Why it is wrong: Tilman ran outdoor plots and measured biomass variation; Paul Ehrlich built the aeroplane-and-rivets analogy. Correct principle: Tilman means plots; Ehrlich means rivets.
• Mistake 7: Filing the Nile perch disaster under over-exploitation. Why it is wrong: nobody over-harvested the cichlids; an introduced predator ate them. Correct principle: an introduced species doing the damage is alien species invasion, one of the Evil Quartet's distinct members.
• Mistake 8: Listing botanical gardens and wildlife safari parks as in situ conservation. Why it is wrong: both hold species away from their natural habitats under human care. Correct principle: in situ keeps species at home — parks, sanctuaries, reserves, hotspots, sacred groves; everything moved into care is ex situ.
• Mistake 9: Claiming four hotspots cover India by adding Sundaland, or quoting 36 hotspots from a newer list. Why it is wrong: the textbook count is 34 hotspots worldwide, three covering India: Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma and Himalaya. Correct principle: answer from the textbook's numbers, not a website's updated tally.
• Mistake 10: Putting the Amazon's oxygen under narrowly utilitarian benefits. Why it is wrong: oxygen is an ecosystem service of the whole forest system, not a product harvested from it; narrowly utilitarian covers direct products like food, fibre, tannins and drugs. Correct principle: products are narrow, services are broad, intrinsic value is ethical.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A region follows the species-area relation with Z equal to 0.2. Surveyors enlarge the explored area 100 times. By what factor does species richness rise?
• Given: log S = log C + Z log A, with Z = 0.2 and the area multiplied by 100.
• Principle: On the log scale, the rise in log S equals Z times the rise in log A.
• Steps: The rise in log A is log of 100, which is 2. Multiply by Z: 0.2 times 2 equals 0.4. So log S rises by 0.4, and S itself is multiplied by 10 raised to 0.4, which is about 2.5.
• Answer: Richness rises only about 2.5 times for a 100-fold rise in area.
• Reasoning: The shallow small-area slope is the hyperbola's bend expressed as arithmetic — the regional pool is mostly re-sampled, not expanded.
• Mistake to avoid: Multiplying richness by 100, or by Z times 100. The equation is logarithmic; factors multiply on the log scale, not on the raw scale.
• Example 2 — Problem: A botanist reports that a Himalayan valley population of Rauwolfia vomitoria yields far stronger reserpine than a population in another range. An ecologist reports that the Western Ghats hold more frog species than the Eastern Ghats. Name the diversity level in each report.
• Given: Two reports — chemical potency varying within one plant species; species counts varying between two hill ranges.
• Principle: Apply the level test — what is being counted: gene versions, kinds of organisms, or kinds of ecosystems.
• Steps: Report one counts chemical variants inside a single species — genetic diversity. Report two counts kinds of organisms (frog species) between regions — species diversity.
• Answer: Rauwolfia potency is genetic diversity; the Ghats frog comparison is species diversity.
• Reasoning: The species in report one never changes; only its internal chemistry does. Report two changes the cast list itself.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling the Rauwolfia case species diversity merely because two locations are involved — locations do not set the level; what varies does.
• Example 3 — Problem: Sort four conservation actions into in situ and ex situ: (i) declaring a wildlife sanctuary, (ii) storing seeds in a seed bank, (iii) protecting a sacred grove, (iv) freezing gametes of a threatened species.
• Given: Four textbook addresses of conservation practice.
• Principle: The dividing question is whether the species remains in its natural habitat under protection, or is kept outside it under human care.
• Steps: Sanctuary — species stays home: in situ. Seed bank — genetic material stored in a facility: ex situ. Sacred grove — forest protected in place by tradition: in situ. Frozen gametes — cryopreservation in a laboratory: ex situ.
• Answer: In situ: (i) and (iii). Ex situ: (ii) and (iv).
• Reasoning: Both ex situ cases involve material physically held by humans; both in situ cases leave the community where it evolved.
• Mistake to avoid: Filing the seed bank as in situ because seeds "belong" to a place. Location of care, not origin, decides the category.
• Example 4 — Problem: In a sample drawn in the chapter's global proportions, a museum holds 2,000 described species. Estimate how many are animals, how many are plants, and how many are insects.
• Given: Animals form more than 70 per cent of recorded species; plants no more than 22 per cent; insects form more than 70 per cent of animals.
• Principle: Apply the pie percentages in sequence — first split the sample into animals and plants, then split the animals again for insects.
• Steps: Animals: about 70 per cent of 2,000, roughly 1,400. Plants: no more than 22 per cent, roughly 440 or fewer. Insects: over 70 per cent of the 1,400 animals, roughly 980 or more — about half the entire collection.
• Answer: About 1,400 animals, at most about 440 plants, and nearly 1,000 insects.
• Reasoning: 0.7 times 0.7 is about 0.49 — insects are roughly half the whole pie.
• Mistake to avoid: Applying the insect percentage to the full 2,000 rather than to the animal subset — insects are 70 per cent of animals, not of all species.
• Example 5 — Problem: A lake's native fish fauna collapses within years of a large predatory fish being released into it for food. A second lake's fish are netted out commercially until the prized species disappears. Assign each collapse to its Evil Quartet member.
• Given: Two fish disasters with different killing mechanisms.
• Principle: Ask what exactly killed them — an introduced species, or direct human harvest.
• Steps: In lake one, the newcomer predator did the eating — the Nile perch and Lake Victoria pattern: alien species invasion. In lake two, humans removed the victims themselves for trade — over-exploitation.
• Answer: Lake one: alien species invasion. Lake two: over-exploitation.
• Reasoning: Both are human-caused, but the quartet sorts by mechanism, not by blame — who or what did the killing decides the box.
• Mistake to avoid: Labelling any fishery collapse over-exploitation automatically; an introduced predator is invasion even when humans released it.
• Example 6 — Problem: Tilman-style plots are sown with either 2 or 16 grassland species and followed for a decade of wet and dry years. Predict which set of plots shows (a) steadier total biomass across years and (b) higher average productivity, and justify both.
• Given: Tilman's long-term outdoor plot findings as the model.
• Principle: Diverse communities buffer individual species' swings — some species thrive in dry years, others in wet ones — so totals stabilise and average higher.
• Steps: For (a), the 16-species plots varied less in total biomass from year to year in Tilman's experiments. For (b), increased diversity contributed to higher productivity, so the 16-species plots also average more biomass.
• Answer: The species-rich plots win on both counts — steadier and more productive.
• Reasoning: Species substitute for one another across years; a two-species plot has almost no substitutes, so each bad year is shared by the whole plot.
• Mistake to avoid: Claiming diverse plots win because each individual species grows better — the measured effect is on the community's total and its stability, not on single-species performance.
• Example 7 — Problem: From the IUCN ledger in this chapter: what fraction of the 784 recorded extinctions of the last 500 years were invertebrates, and what does the size of that fraction tell you?
• Given: 784 total extinctions — 338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates, 87 plants.
• Principle: Compute the share and compare it with the public image of extinction, which is dominated by large vertebrates.
• Steps: 359 divided by 784 is about 0.46 — roughly 46 per cent, the largest block, ahead of vertebrates (43 per cent) and plants (11 per cent). Check: 338 plus 359 plus 87 equals 784.
• Answer: About 46 per cent were invertebrates — the biggest block of recorded extinctions.
• Reasoning: Extinction is mostly a small-animal story; the famous vertebrate names are the visible minority of a largely invertebrate ledger.
• Mistake to avoid: Assuming vertebrates dominate the extinction list because they dominate the examples — the dodo and thylacine are memorable, not majority.

• A1. The sociobiologist Edward Wilson. The three levels are genetic diversity (within a species), species diversity (among species) and ecological diversity (among ecosystems). Misconception watch: Wilson popularised the term; the three levels are the standard framework NCERT presents under it.
• A2. Robert May estimates about 7 million species in total; the IUCN (2004) records slightly more than 1.5 million species as described so far. Misconception watch: swapping these two numbers is the chapter's most planted error — one is an estimate of existence, the other a count of descriptions.

• A4. Z is the slope of the line (regression coefficient); C is the Y-intercept. For frugivorous birds and mammals in tropical forests of different continents, Z is 1.15, inside the continental band of 0.6 to 1.2. Misconception watch: C is not the slope and Z is not the intercept — the letters are assigned by the equation's form, log S equals log C plus Z times log A.
• A5. Habitat loss and fragmentation; over-exploitation; alien species invasion; co-extinction. The Lake Victoria cichlids were destroyed by alien species invasion — the introduced Nile perch. Misconception watch: students file the perch under exploitation because humans introduced it; the quartet sorts by mechanism, and the perch, not the net, did the killing.
• A6. 784 species — 338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates and 87 plants. Misconception watch: the split must add to 784; any option whose three numbers sum to a different total is false even if one number looks familiar.
• A7. In situ: national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves (also hotspots and sacred groves). Ex situ: zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks (also seed banks, tissue culture and cryopreservation of gametes). Misconception watch: safari parks are ex situ in NCERT despite the open landscape — not the natural habitat.
• A8. The Earth Summit at Rio de Janeiro, 1992, produced the Convention on Biological Diversity. Misconception watch: Rio wrote the Convention; the numbered pledge with the 2010 deadline is Johannesburg's, ten years later.

• Summary: Biodiversity is life's variety at three levels — genetic (Rauwolfia's reserpine, India's 50,000 rice strains), species (Western Ghats amphibians over Eastern) and ecological (India's seven habitat types over Norway's few) — popularised as one word by Edward Wilson.
• Summary: The census is unfinished: slightly more than 1.5 million species described, about 7 million estimated by Robert May. Animals take over 70 per cent of the pie and insects over 70 per cent of animals; India holds 8.1 per cent of global diversity on 2.4 per cent of land, and the Amazon holds the richest list on earth.
• Summary: Patterns rule the map — diversity falls from equator to poles for reasons of time, stability and sunshine, and rises with area along Humboldt's hyperbola, log S = log C + Z log A, flat (0.1 to 0.2) within regions and steep (0.6 to 1.2) across continents.
• Summary: Diversity is not decoration: Tilman's plots show it steadies and raises production, and Ehrlich's rivets show what weakens as species vanish. Against that stands the ledger — 784 recorded extinctions, a sixth extinction running 100 to 1,000 times too fast, and four causes in the Evil Quartet doing the damage.
• Summary: The reply is layered — utilitarian and ethical reasons to care, in situ protection of hotspots, parks, sanctuaries and sacred groves, ex situ rescue in zoos, gardens, seed banks and freezers, and two world summits, Rio 1992 and Johannesburg 2002, binding nations to the task.
