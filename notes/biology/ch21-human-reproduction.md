Introduction — One Chapter, One New Life
• Every human reading this began as a single cell smaller than a full stop, built by two cells meeting in a tube about ten centimetres long. This chapter follows that whole arc: how the male and female systems are built, how sperms and ova are manufactured on two opposite schedules, how the menstrual cycle rehearses a pregnancy every month, and how a fertilised egg becomes a baby in nine months. Almost every fact here is a favourite exam trap, so we will learn each one chained to its reason.
• DEFINITION — Human reproduction is sexual reproduction: a male gamete (the sperm, haploid) and a female gamete (the ovum, haploid) fuse at fertilisation to form a diploid zygote, which develops inside the mother's uterus until birth. Humans are viviparous — the young one is born alive, not hatched from an egg laid outside.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — the examiners recycle a small fixed cast: the duct order (rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, vas deferens), the two tubule cell teams (Sertoli feeds, Leydig secretes androgens), the follicle ladder, the ploidy bookkeeping (four sperms per primary spermatocyte, one ovum per primary oocyte), the LH surge on day 14, and the placenta hormone list. Learn the cast once, with reasons, and the questions become free marks.
• EXAMPLE — Hold the two production schedules side by side and the chapter already makes sense: a man manufactures sperms continuously from puberty, roughly 200-300 million in a single ejaculate, while a woman is born with her entire stock of primary oocytes, releases about one per month, and releases only about 400-500 across her whole reproductive life. Quantity versus conservation — every difference between the two systems flows from that contrast.
• WORKED PATTERN — The master key for this chapter is to ask of every structure: is it a duct, a gland or a gonad? Gonads make gametes and hormones (testis, ovary). Ducts transport and store (epididymis, fallopian tube). Glands add secretions (prostate, seminal vesicles). Sort any named part into its box first and most match-the-following questions solve themselves.
• YOUR TURN — The bulbourethral glands are small and paired. Duct, gland or gonad — and what is their one job? Answer: glands; their secretion lubricates the penis (and the male urethra) during copulation. They make no gametes and carry nothing — the box decides.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The whole chapter in one breath: testes outside the body make sperms non-stop, ovaries inside release one pre-made oocyte a month, the cycle is driven by FSH, LH, estrogen and progesterone, fertilisation happens in the ampulla of the fallopian tube, and the placenta then runs a nine-month supply line ending in a positive-feedback birth.

The Male System — A Factory Outside the Body
• DEFINITION — The male reproductive system lies in the pelvis region. It consists of a pair of testes (the primary sex organs or gonads), the accessory ducts (rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis and vas deferens), the accessory glands (seminal vesicles, prostate and bulbourethral glands) and the external genitalia, the penis.
• DEFINITION — The testes are oval organs about 4-5 cm long and 2-3 cm wide, and they sit outside the abdominal cavity in a pouch called the scrotum. The scrotum is not packaging; it is a climate-control device — it holds the testes at a temperature 2-2.5 degrees Celsius lower than the normal body temperature, and that cooler temperature is necessary for spermatogenesis.
• WHY the factory is kept outside — sperm production is heat-sensitive in a way most body chemistry is not. Spermatogonia divide and mature correctly only in the cooler scrotal environment; a testis that fails to descend into the scrotum and stays at core body temperature typically fails to produce fertile sperms. The anatomy is the physiology: the position exists because of the temperature requirement.
• DEFINITION — Trace the sperm highway in order. Sperms made in the seminiferous tubules pass into the rete testis, a network of tubules inside the testis, then out through the vasa efferentia into the epididymis, a coiled tube along the posterior surface of the testis where sperms are stored and mature. From the epididymis the vas deferens ascends into the abdomen and loops over the urinary bladder; it receives a duct from the seminal vesicle and becomes the ejaculatory duct through the prostate. The urethra runs from the urinary bladder through the penis to the urethral meatus — the shared final road for urine and semen.
✔ KEY POINT — The duct order is a fixed chain the exam loves to shuffle: seminiferous tubules, rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, urethra. Learn it as one sentence — the tubules hand over to the rete, the rete to the vasa, the vasa to the epididymis, and from there it is one long vas to the exit.
• DEFINITION — The accessory glands pour in the transport medium. The paired seminal vesicles and the single prostate secrete most of the seminal plasma, which is rich in fructose (fuel for the sperms), calcium and certain enzymes. The paired bulbourethral glands secrete a fluid that lubricates the penis. Semen is the final mixture: sperms plus seminal plasma.
• EXAMPLE — Fructose is the clue that the plasma is a supply fluid, not just a carrier: sperms are stripped-down cells with almost no food reserves, so the semen carries their sugar with them, the way a rocket carries its fuel.
• WORKED PATTERN — Accessory gland questions are pairing questions: seminal vesicles and prostate to seminal plasma (fructose, calcium, enzymes), bulbourethral glands to lubrication. If an option credits the bulbourethral glands with fructose, or the epididymis with secreting plasma, the pairing is broken and the option is wrong.
• YOUR TURN — During a vasectomy, the vas deferens is cut and tied on both sides. Will the man's semen volume collapse, and will his hormone levels change? Answer: neither — the glands keep secreting seminal plasma (the bulk of semen volume) and the testes keep making androgens; only the sperms are blocked from reaching the urethra, so the ejaculate is sperm-free.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The epididymis is a duct (storage and maturation), not a gland, and the urethra — not the vas deferens — is the common passage for urine and semen in males. Also fix the numbers: about 250 lobules per testis come next section, but the temperature gap is 2-2.5 degrees, not 5 or 10; options inflate it.

Seminiferous Tubules — Where Sperms Are Made
• DEFINITION — Each testis contains about 250 compartments called testicular lobules. Each lobule holds one to three highly coiled seminiferous tubules, and the seminiferous tubules are the actual site of sperm production. Zoom into one tubule and its wall is lined by two cell types: the male germ cells (spermatogonia), which divide and mature into sperms, and the Sertoli cells, which provide nutrition to the developing germ cells.
• DEFINITION — Outside the tubules lie the interstitial spaces, containing the Leydig cells (also called interstitial cells). Leydig cells synthesise and secrete the male hormones, the androgens (testosterone is the chief one), under the signal of LH from the pituitary. The interstitial spaces also hold blood vessels and other immunologically competent cells.
✔ KEY POINT — The two cell teams divide the labour absolutely: Sertoli cells (inside the tubule) feed and support the germ cells — food; Leydig cells (outside the tubule, in the interstitial spaces) make androgens — hormones. FSH acts on Sertoli cells; LH acts on Leydig cells. Inside-feeds, outside-secretes: that one line answers a whole family of questions.
• WHY the germ cells need a nurse — developing spermatids are busy discarding cytoplasm and rebuilding themselves into streamlined swimmers; they cannot feed themselves efficiently through the long remodelling. Sertoli cells bridge the gap: they nourish the germ cells, and at the end of spermiogenesis the finished sperm heads stay embedded in Sertoli cells until release (spermiation).
• EXAMPLE — Think of the tubule as a boarding school: the students (germ cells) progress as they mature, the warden (Sertoli cell) feeds them and lets them out at graduation (spermiation), while the office outside the building (Leydig cells) issues the hormone circulars that keep the school running.
• WORKED PATTERN — Every tubule question is a location question in disguise. Inside the tubule wall: spermatogonia, developing stages, Sertoli cells. In the lumen: sperms ready for release. Outside the tubule: Leydig cells, blood vessels. Place the named item on that map first; the correct option is then the only one standing in the right zone.
• YOUR TURN — A drug destroys only the Leydig cells. Predict two consequences. Answer: androgen secretion falls, so spermatogenesis loses its hormonal drive and sperms are not produced properly; androgen-maintained secondary sexual characters also suffer — while the Sertoli cells, a different team in a different zone, are untouched directly.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Androgens come from Leydig cells, never from Sertoli cells, and Sertoli cells never make hormones in this chapter's account — they nourish. Options swapping the two (androgens by Sertoli, nutrition by Leydig) are the single most repeated error in this section. Also: each lobule has one to three tubules, and each testis about 250 lobules — do not merge the two numbers.

The Female System — The Meeting Place and the Nursery
• DEFINITION — The female reproductive system consists of a pair of ovaries (the primary sex organs), the accessory ducts — a pair of oviducts or fallopian tubes, the uterus and the vagina — the external genitalia, and a pair of mammary glands. The ovaries produce the female gamete, the ovum, and also several steroid hormones.
• DEFINITION — Each ovary is about 2-4 cm long and sits on each side of the lower abdomen, connected to the pelvic wall and to the uterus by ligaments. Each ovary is covered by a thin epithelium and encloses the ovarian stroma, which has two zones: a peripheral cortex and an inner medulla.
• DEFINITION — Each fallopian tube is about 10-12 cm long, running from the ovary's periphery to the uterus, with three named regions in order: the infundibulum, a funnel close to the ovary whose edges carry finger-like fimbriae that collect the ovum after ovulation; the ampulla, the wider middle part where fertilisation normally occurs; and the isthmus, the narrow last part joining the uterus.
✔ KEY POINT — Tube geography is destiny in this chapter: the ovum is collected at the infundibulum, fertilised in the ampulla, and the dividing embryo travels through the isthmus into the uterus. Fimbriae collect, ampulla hosts the meeting, isthmus is the corridor — three regions, three jobs, in that order.
• DEFINITION — The uterus (womb) is single, shaped like an inverted pear, and supported by ligaments attached to the pelvic wall. It opens into the vagina through a narrow neck, the cervix, whose cavity is the cervical canal; the cervical canal together with the vagina forms the birth canal. The uterus wall has three layers: the thin outer perimetrium, the thick middle myometrium of smooth muscle, and the inner glandular endometrium that lines the uterine cavity.
• WHY the wall needs three different layers — each layer has a different contract. The endometrium is the receptive soil: it changes cyclically and receives the implanting embryo. The myometrium is the engine: it contracts strongly during parturition to expel the baby. The perimetrium is the protective outer coat. Layer, cycle, contraction — match them and the anatomy explains itself.
• DEFINITION — The external genitalia include the mons pubis (a cushion of fatty tissue covered by skin and pubic hair), the labia majora (fleshy folds extending down from it), the labia minora (paired folds beneath them), the hymen (a membrane often partially covering the vaginal opening) and the clitoris (a tiny finger-like structure above the urethral opening, at the upper junction of the labia minora).
• EXAMPLE — The mammary glands complete the reproductive job after birth. Each breast contains glandular tissue divided into 15-20 mammary lobes, each holding clusters of milk-secreting alveoli. The sequence outwards is fixed: alveoli open into mammary tubules, tubules join into a mammary duct, ducts converge into a wider mammary ampulla, and the ampulla connects to the lactiferous duct through which milk is sucked out.
• WORKED PATTERN — Female-system questions sort by the same three boxes as the male side. Ovary: gonad (makes ovum and steroid hormones). Tube and uterus and vagina: ducts (transport, meeting, nursery, birth canal). Mammary glands: glands (milk). A named structure that makes no gametes cannot be the primary sex organ, whatever its size.
• YOUR TURN — The hymen is sometimes torn during the very first intercourse — but can its absence prove a woman has had intercourse? Answer: no. NCERT is explicit that the hymen can also be torn by sudden falls, sports, cycling or horse riding, and it is present in some women even after intercourse — it is not a reliable indicator of virginity or sexual experience.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The uterus is single but the ovaries and fallopian tubes are paired — and the fallopian tube is one duct with three regions, not three separate tubes. Also, the birth canal is cervical canal plus vagina together; options naming the vagina alone are incomplete.

The Ovary and Its Follicles — A Monthly Countdown
• DEFINITION — Inside the ovarian cortex, each primary oocyte begins life wrapped in a layer of granulosa cells — together they form a primary follicle. As more layers of granulosa cells and a new layer, the theca, are added, it becomes a secondary follicle. The follicle then develops a fluid-filled cavity called the antrum and is called a tertiary follicle; the fully mature tertiary follicle is the Graafian follicle, the one that ruptures at ovulation.
✔ KEY POINT — The follicle ladder has four rungs with one feature added per rung: primary follicle (oocyte plus granulosa layer), secondary follicle (more granulosa layers plus theca), tertiary follicle (antrum appears), Graafian follicle (mature tertiary, ready to rupture). Questions that shuffle the rungs are solved by asking one thing: has the antrum appeared yet?
• DEFINITION — The numbers are stark. A baby girl is born with her lifetime stock already inside her ovaries: a large number of primary follicles — figures of 60000 to 80000 in each ovary at puberty are the ones usually quoted after most have degenerated during childhood — and from puberty onwards, only one follicle matures in one ovary in each monthly cycle. Across an entire reproductive life, only about 400-500 ova are ever released; the overwhelming majority of follicles simply degenerate.
• WHY only one follicle wins each month — the growing follicles compete for the pituitary signal FSH, and the most sensitive follicle outgrows the rest, whose development stalls and which degenerate. One winner per cycle keeps the system matched to its design load: the uterus prepares one lining, for one ovum, for one possible embryo.
• EXAMPLE — Run the ledger: millions of follicles exist before birth, 60000-80000 remain per ovary at puberty, and only about 400-500 are ever ovulated. The ovary is not a factory that runs out of raw material — it is a vault opened once a month, with almost everything inside destined to be discarded unused.
• WORKED PATTERN — Follicle questions give a feature and ask for the stage, or give a stage and ask for the feature. Antrum present: tertiary or later. Theca present but no antrum: secondary. Ruptured at ovulation: Graafian only. After rupture, the same follicle is no longer a follicle at all — it becomes the corpus luteum, which is next section's territory in the cycle story.
• YOUR TURN — A section of ovary shows a large fluid-filled antrum and the primary oocyte still inside, but the follicle has not ruptured. Name the stage, and name what this follicle becomes if fertilisation does NOT occur this cycle. Answer: it is a tertiary (maturing Graafian) follicle; without fertilisation, after ovulation its remains become the corpus luteum, which regresses into the corpus albicans as the cycle resets.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The oocyte does not mature first and get wrapped afterwards: the primary oocyte sits inside the follicle from the very first rung, arrested mid-division, and finishes its first meiotic division only inside the tertiary follicle shortly before ovulation. And ovulation releases a secondary oocyte, not a finished ovum — that detail decides whole questions.

Spermatogenesis — Four Sperms from One Cell
• DEFINITION — Spermatogenesis is the formation of sperms inside the seminiferous tubules, and it runs continuously from puberty onwards. It begins with the spermatogonia, diploid germ cells (2n, 46 chromosomes) sitting on the inner wall of the tubule. Some spermatogonia keep dividing by mitosis to maintain the stem pool; others grow into primary spermatocytes.
• DEFINITION — The division ledger is the heart of the section. A primary spermatocyte (2n = 46) completes the first meiotic division, producing two equal haploid secondary spermatocytes (n = 23 each). Each secondary spermatocyte completes the second meiotic division, producing two equal haploid spermatids — four spermatids in all from one primary spermatocyte. The spermatids then transform into sperms by spermiogenesis, and their release from the Sertoli cells is called spermiation.
✔ KEY POINT — Ploidy bookkeeping: mitosis keeps 2n, meiosis I halves 2n to n, meiosis II keeps n. From ONE primary spermatocyte you get FOUR equal, functional sperms — the arithmetic 1 to 2 to 4 is the question, every single time.
• DEFINITION — A mature sperm is a microscopic, streamlined cell: a head containing the elongated haploid nucleus, capped in front by the acrosome, a sac of enzymes that will help it penetrate the ovum; a neck; a middle piece packed with numerous mitochondria that produce the energy for movement; and a long tail that gives it motility through the female tract.
• WHY each part of the sperm earns its place — the nucleus is the cargo (the father's 23 chromosomes), the acrosome is the breaching tool, the mitochondria are the engine room, and the tail is the propeller. A sperm carries almost no cytoplasm and no food store because it is built for one journey only; anything not serving delivery, penetration or swimming was discarded during spermiogenesis.
• DEFINITION — The hormonal switchboard: at puberty, secretion of GnRH from the hypothalamus increases, which makes the anterior pituitary secrete two gonadotropins. LH acts on the Leydig cells and triggers androgen secretion, and the androgens drive spermatogenesis. FSH acts on the Sertoli cells and stimulates secretion of factors that help spermiogenesis. Two pituitary hormones, two tubule addresses — LH to Leydig outside, FSH to Sertoli inside.
• EXAMPLE — Quality control in numbers: a human male ejaculates about 200-300 million sperms, yet for normal fertility at least 60 percent must have normal shape and size, and at least 40 percent must show vigorous motility. Even with hundreds of millions of starters, the system demands a passing grade in both shape and swimming.
• WORKED PATTERN — Count-the-products questions always use the same branching tree: start with the number of primary spermatocytes given, double it for secondary spermatocytes, double again for spermatids and sperms. Ten primary spermatocytes make twenty secondary spermatocytes and forty sperms. If an option says ten spermatocytes make ten sperms, it has quietly deleted meiosis II.
• YOUR TURN — A man's report shows plenty of spermatids being formed but almost no mature sperms in the semen. Which step is failing — meiosis or spermiogenesis? Answer: spermiogenesis, the transformation of spermatids into sperms. Meiosis has done its job (the spermatids exist); the remodelling into tailed, capped sperms is what is broken.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Spermiogenesis (spermatid becomes sperm) is not spermiation (sperm is released from the Sertoli cell), and neither is a division — no cell number changes in either. Also, secondary spermatocytes are already haploid: the halving happens at meiosis I, not meiosis II. Options that keep the secondary spermatocyte diploid fail the ledger.

Oogenesis — One Ovum from One Cell
• DEFINITION — Oogenesis is the formation of a mature female gamete, and its timetable is the opposite of the male one: it is initiated during embryonic development itself. Several million oogonia are formed inside each fetal ovary, and no more oogonia are formed or added after birth. Each oogonium grows into a primary oocyte, which starts the first meiotic division before birth — and then stops.
• DEFINITION — The primary oocyte begins meiosis I in fetal life and is temporarily arrested in prophase I, wrapped in granulosa cells as a primary follicle. It stays arrested for years — from before birth until after puberty — and completes that first division only when its follicle reaches the tertiary stage in a particular cycle.
✔ KEY POINT — When the primary oocyte finally divides, the division is dramatically unequal: one large haploid secondary oocyte keeps nearly all the cytoplasm, and one tiny first polar body takes the other set of chromosomes and almost nothing else. The secondary oocyte is what the Graafian follicle releases at ovulation — surrounded by its zona pellucida coat — and its second meiotic division begins but arrests again, completing only if a sperm enters.
• WHY the division is unequal — the ovum is not just a chromosome packet; it must carry the entire starting kitchen for the early embryo: cytoplasm, nutrients and mitochondria enough to survive the first days before implantation. Concentrating everything into one cell and discarding the extra chromosomes in disposable polar bodies is the economical design. The male strategy splits evenly because sperms carry nothing but chromosomes.
• EXAMPLE — Timeline of one oocyte: it begins meiosis before its owner is born, waits arrested through childhood, resumes in one chosen month perhaps 15, 25 or 40 years later, and is ovulated as a secondary oocyte that finishes meiosis II within hours if fertilised — or never finishes it at all. Some primary oocytes wait half a century for a division that takes a day.
• WORKED PATTERN — Product-count questions in oogenesis invert the male tree: one primary oocyte gives one secondary oocyte plus one polar body at meiosis I, and one ovum (ootid) plus one more polar body at meiosis II, which completes only after sperm entry. Functional gametes per primary oocyte: exactly one. If an option grants four ova from one primary oocyte, it has imported the male pattern — reject it.
• YOUR TURN — A Graafian follicle ruptures and releases its cell. A student calls it a mature ovum. Correct the statement in one line, naming what was actually released and what event would complete its maturation. Answer: a secondary oocyte arrested in meiosis II was released; it becomes a true ovum only when a sperm enters and triggers completion of the second meiotic division.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Three separate arrest facts get merged into one wrong statement: oogonia are all formed before birth (none added later), the primary oocyte arrests in prophase I, and the secondary oocyte arrests again until fertilisation. "Oogenesis starts at puberty" is false — puberty only resumes a process that started in the womb.

The Menstrual Cycle — Twenty-Eight Days in Four Acts
• DEFINITION — The reproductive cycle in female primates (monkeys, apes and humans) is called the menstrual cycle. It starts at puberty (menarche), repeats at an average interval of about 28/29 days, and ceases around 50 years of age (menopause). One cycle prepares one ovum and one uterine lining for a possible pregnancy — and discards both if pregnancy does not happen.
• DEFINITION — Act one, the menstrual phase, lasts about 3-5 days at the start of the cycle. If the ovum released in the previous cycle was not fertilised, the endometrium and its blood vessels break down and are shed as menstrual flow. Menstruation is therefore the visible signature of a pregnancy that did not occur.
• DEFINITION — Act two, the follicular (proliferative) phase: primary follicles in the ovary grow into a fully mature Graafian follicle under rising levels of the gonadotropins LH and FSH, while simultaneously the endometrium of the uterus regenerates through proliferation. The growing follicles secrete increasing amounts of estrogen, the hormone that drives the rebuilding of the lining.
• DEFINITION — Act three, the ovulatory phase, falls mid-cycle, around the 14th day: both LH and FSH rise to a peak, but it is the LH that spikes dramatically — the LH surge — and this rapid secretion of LH induces the Graafian follicle to rupture and release the secondary oocyte. That release is ovulation.
• DEFINITION — Act four, the luteal (secretory) phase: the remains of the ruptured Graafian follicle transform into the corpus luteum, which secretes large amounts of progesterone. Progesterone maintains the endometrium in the thickened, secretory state needed for implantation. If fertilisation occurs, the lining is kept and pregnancy begins; if not, the corpus luteum degenerates, progesterone collapses, and the endometrium breaks down into the next menstrual phase.
✔ KEY POINT — The hormone-to-event pairings are the exam's favourite chain: FSH grows the follicle, estrogen rebuilds the endometrium, the LH surge triggers ovulation on about day 14, and progesterone from the corpus luteum maintains the endometrium afterwards. Memorise the chain as cause and effect, not as four separate facts — each hormone's fall or rise explains the next event.
• WHY the cycle is a rehearsal, not a waste — every month the body runs the full preparation: grow an egg, rebuild a nursery, release the egg, hold the nursery ready for two weeks. Only the last step (implantation) is conditional. The monthly rehearsal means a fertilised egg never arrives at an unprepared uterus; the cost is the shedding, the benefit is zero waiting time.
• EXAMPLE — The hormone timeline the exam loves: estrogen peaks just before ovulation (late follicular phase), progesterone peaks in the mid-luteal phase (about a week after ovulation), and both fall together just before menstruation. Two peaks, two hormones, two phases — graph questions are nothing but this sentence drawn.
• WORKED PATTERN — Day-counting questions: anchor on ovulation at day 14 of a 28-day cycle and count from the first day of menstrual flow, which is day 1. Fertilisation is possible only in the short window around ovulation, because the ovum survives about a day and sperms a couple of days in the tract. Any "most likely day of conception" question is answered by distance from day 14.
• YOUR TURN — A woman's cycle is a regular 28 days and her period begins on 1 March. On about what date does she ovulate, and what single hormonal event causes it? Answer: about 14 March (day 14); the LH surge — the mid-cycle peak of luteinising hormone — ruptures the Graafian follicle.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Ovulation is caused by the LH surge, not by FSH, estrogen or progesterone — and progesterone is a luteal-phase hormone, high after ovulation, never the trigger for it. Also: during pregnancy there is no menstrual cycle and no menstruation, because the corpus luteum (later supported by placental hormones) keeps progesterone flowing; a question that has a pregnant woman menstruating normally has broken the chain.

Fertilisation — Fusion at the Ampulla
• DEFINITION — Fertilisation is the fusion of a sperm with an ovum, and in humans it can happen only in one place: the ampullary region of the fallopian tube — the wider part, just beyond the infundibulum, on the ovarian side of the isthmus. During copulation, semen is released into the vagina (insemination); the motile sperms swim rapidly through the cervix, enter the uterus and finally reach the ampulla, while the ovulated secondary oocyte is transported to the same region. Fertilisation is possible only if the ovum and sperms arrive there at the same time — which is why not all copulations lead to fertilisation or pregnancy.
• DEFINITION — At the meeting, the sperm first contacts the zona pellucida, the coat surrounding the secondary oocyte. Enzymes from the acrosome help the sperm digest a path through the coats and the plasma membrane; contact with the zona induces changes in the oocyte membrane that block the entry of any additional sperms, so exactly one sperm gets in — polyspermy is prevented.
✔ KEY POINT — The entry of the sperm is the starting gun for the oocyte: it induces the secondary oocyte to complete its arrested second meiotic division. That unequal division produces the large haploid ovum (ootid) and a tiny second polar body. Only now do the haploid sperm nucleus and haploid ovum nucleus fuse, restoring the diploid number — the zygote, 2n = 46, the first cell of the new individual.
• WHY the block to polyspermy matters — one extra sperm would add a whole third set of chromosomes (triploidy), which human embryos cannot survive. The membrane change triggered by the first successful sperm is therefore not a detail; it is the quality-control gate that guarantees the zygote receives exactly two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent.
• EXAMPLE — Think of the scale of the race: of the 200-300 million sperms inseminated, only a few hundred reach the ampulla at all — the female tract is an obstacle course, so arrival itself is a selection filter. The sperm that fertilises is the survivor of the largest elimination race in biology.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sequence questions in this section always run: insemination, sperm transport to ampulla, contact with zona pellucida, acrosomal enzymes and entry of one sperm, block to polyspermy, completion of meiosis II by the oocyte, fusion of the two haploid nuclei, diploid zygote. Any option that fuses the nuclei before meiosis II completes, or lets the second polar body form before sperm entry, has the order wrong.
• YOUR TURN — A sperm reaches the oocyte but its acrosome is defective and releases no enzymes. Predict the outcome. Answer: it cannot digest a path through the zona pellucida and the oocyte coats, so it never enters; no entry means no completion of meiosis II and no fertilisation, however motile the sperm is.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Fertilisation does not occur in the uterus or the vagina — it is the ampulla, always. And the cell that gets fertilised is a secondary oocyte, not a mature ovum: the "ovum" stage exists only after the sperm has already entered. Questions that say "the mature ovum is released at ovulation and then fertilised" contain two errors in one line.

Sex Determination — The Father Holds the Deciding Chromosome
• DEFINITION — Human females carry two X chromosomes (XX) and produce only one kind of ovum, always carrying an X. Human males are XY and make two kinds of sperms in equal numbers: about 50 percent carry the X chromosome and 50 percent carry the Y. The ovum always contributes an X; the sperm contributes either an X or a Y.
✔ KEY POINT — The fusion arithmetic is complete: ovum X plus sperm X gives an XX zygote, a baby girl; ovum X plus sperm Y gives an XY zygote, a baby boy. Since the ovum's contribution never varies, the sex of the baby is determined by the sperm — that is, by the father — at the moment of fertilisation, purely by chance, 50:50 each time.
• WHY this is more than a diagram — it corrects a persistent social superstition. In many families the mother is blamed for the sex of the child, but the biology admits no blame at all: her ova are chromosomally identical, the variable gamete is the sperm, and even the father's contribution is a coin toss he does not control.
• EXAMPLE — A couple has four daughters in a row. What is the probability their fifth child is a boy? Still 50 percent. Each fertilisation is an independent draw from a sperm pool that is half X-bearing and half Y-bearing; previous children do not change the proportions.
• WORKED PATTERN — Sex cross questions are one-cell Punnett squares: mother XX (X, X) against father XY (X, Y) gives XX, XY, XX, XY in the four boxes. Read the ratio straight off: half XX (female), half XY (male), regardless of family history.
• YOUR TURN — A man claims his wife "only produces girl eggs". Dismantle the claim with two facts. Answer: first, all her ova carry an X chromosome, so there are no "girl eggs" versus "boy eggs" — ova do not differ at all for sex; second, the child's sex is set by whether an X-bearing or Y-bearing sperm from the father fertilises the ovum.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Sex is determined at fertilisation, not during development, and the Y chromosome comes only from the father. Any option that has the mother contributing a Y, or the environment deciding the sex later in pregnancy, contradicts the XX-XY mechanism completely.

Cleavage to Blastocyst — Division Without Growth
• DEFINITION — The zygote does not wait to start developing: as it is carried through the isthmus of the fallopian tube towards the uterus, it undergoes rapid mitotic divisions called cleavage. The daughter cells are called blastomeres, and the divisions run 2, 4, 8, 16 — the embryo with 8-16 blastomeres is called a morula.
• DEFINITION — Cleavage has one strange property that defines it: the cells divide, but the embryo does not grow. The zygote's original volume is simply partitioned into smaller and smaller cells, all still inside the original zona pellucida envelope — so a 16-cell morula is about the same size as the one-cell zygote was.
• WHY dividing without growing is the right strategy — the embryo is travelling and cannot feed; there is no food supply line until implantation. Cleavage spends nothing on growth and instead multiplies the cell count, so that by the time the embryo reaches the uterus it has enough cells to organise into two different tissues — one to build the baby, one to build the supply line.
• DEFINITION — The morula keeps dividing and transforms into the blastocyst. In the blastocyst, the blastomeres are arranged into an outer layer called the trophoblast and a cluster of cells attached inside it called the inner cell mass. The trophoblast is the future interface with the mother — it will attach to the endometrium — while the inner cell mass is the future embryo itself.
✔ KEY POINT — Two-layer destiny: trophoblast outside (attachment, placenta, membranes), inner cell mass inside (the baby). Every later structure traces back to one of these two populations, and questions that swap them — "the inner cell mass forms the placenta" — are testing exactly this fork.
• EXAMPLE — The journey on a clock: fertilisation on day 0 in the ampulla; cleavage during the ride down the tube; the morula enters the uterus; the blastocyst forms, hatches from the zona pellucida and implants roughly a week after fertilisation. Meanwhile the uterus, under progesterone from the corpus luteum, has spent the same week preparing the lining — the two timetables meet by design.
• WORKED PATTERN — Stage-identification questions use three clues: cell count, arrangement, and location. 8-16 cells in a solid ball: morula. Hollow ball with an outer layer and an inner cluster: blastocyst. Solid ball versus hollow ball is the visual divide, and the inner cell mass exists only in the blastocyst.
• YOUR TURN — A blastocyst's inner cell mass is removed in an experiment. Can the trophoblast alone produce a baby? Answer: no — the trophoblast builds the attachment and placental structures, but the embryo proper comes only from the inner cell mass. You would get the lifeline without the life.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Cleavage is mitosis, not meiosis: chromosome number stays 2n in every blastomere, and no cell grows between divisions. Also, the morula is a stage of the embryo travelling in the tube/uterus — implantation has not happened yet; implantation is the blastocyst's job, not the morula's or the zygote's.

Implantation and the Placenta — The Lifeline Is Built
• DEFINITION — Implantation is the embedding of the blastocyst in the endometrium of the uterus, and it leads to pregnancy. The trophoblast layer attaches to the endometrium; the uterine cells divide rapidly and cover the blastocyst, so the blastocyst becomes fully embedded in the lining.
• DEFINITION — After implantation, finger-like projections called chorionic villi appear on the trophoblast. The villi are surrounded by uterine tissue and maternal blood, and villi and uterine tissue become interlocked with each other to jointly form the placenta — a structural and functional unit between the developing foetus and the maternal body, connected to the foetus by the umbilical cord.
✔ KEY POINT — The placenta has three jobs the exam lists separately: it facilitates the supply of oxygen and nutrients from the mother to the embryo, it facilitates the removal of carbon dioxide and excretory or waste materials produced by the embryo, and it acts as an endocrine gland. Note the direction of traffic in each — oxygen and nutrients flow mother to foetus, carbon dioxide and wastes flow foetus to mother.
• DEFINITION — The placenta's hormone list is a closed set in NCERT: it secretes human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG), human placental lactogen (hPL), estrogens and progestogens. One related hormone is deliberately NOT placental: relaxin is secreted by the ovary in the later phase of pregnancy. In the mother's blood, levels of estrogens, progestogens, cortisol, prolactin and thyroxine also increase several-fold during pregnancy — the metabolic support cast.
• WHY maternal and foetal blood must never mix directly — the foetus may carry a different blood group from the mother, and direct mixing would be dangerous. The placenta solves it as an exchange surface: the two bloods come extremely close across the villi, close enough for diffusion of gases, nutrients and wastes, but they remain in separate vessels. A border crossing with trade, not an open border.
• EXAMPLE — hCG is the hormone of the pregnancy test: produced by the developing placenta (via the trophoblast) right from implantation and excreted in the mother's urine, its detection is the basis of standard pregnancy tests. It also keeps the corpus luteum active in the early weeks, so progesterone keeps flowing until the placenta secretes enough of its own.
• WORKED PATTERN — "Which of these is secreted by the placenta" questions are list-membership questions: the answer is inside the set hCG, hPL, estrogens, progestogens — or it is outside. Relaxin (ovary) and oxytocin (pituitary, at birth) are the two classic outsiders planted as options. Run the set, not the familiarity: a hormone can sound reproductive and still not be placental.
• YOUR TURN — The umbilical cord is cut at birth and the baby's lungs take over within seconds. Which two placental jobs must the newborn instantly replace with its own organs, and with which organs? Answer: gas exchange (oxygen in, carbon dioxide out — now the lungs) and waste removal (now the baby's own kidneys and liver take over excretion and processing); nutrition switches to milk through the gut.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The placenta is built from BOTH sides: chorionic villi (foetal, from the trophoblast) interlocked with uterine tissue (maternal). Options calling it purely foetal or purely maternal are half-wrong. And hCG comes from the placenta, relaxin from the ovary — the pair is planted together precisely because students merge them.

Embryonic Development — Milestones of Nine Months
• DEFINITION — After implantation, the inner cell mass differentiates into a three-layered germinal disc: an outer ectoderm and an inner endoderm, with a middle mesoderm appearing soon between them. These three germ layers give rise to all tissues and organs of the adult body — a process of organ formation called organogenesis — and the inner cell mass carries the stem cells that have the potency to build every one of those tissues.
• DEFINITION — The milestones, in the order NCERT lists them. After one month, the embryo's heart is formed — the heart sound, heard through a stethoscope, is the first detectable sign of the growing foetus. By the end of the second month, limbs and digits are formed. By the end of 12 weeks (the first trimester), most major organ systems are formed, and external genital organs are well developed. In the fifth month, the first foetal movements and hair on the head are noticed. By the end of 24 weeks, the body is covered with fine hair, the eye-lids separate and eye-lashes form. By nine months, the foetus is fully developed and ready for delivery.
✔ KEY POINT — Anchor the milestone table to four hooks: 1 month = heart; 2 months = limbs and digits; 12 weeks = major organ systems plus external genitalia; 5 months = first movements and hair on the head; 24 weeks = eyelids separate and eyelashes form. Any matching question is solved by pinning the event to its hook, and the 12-week mark (end of first trimester) is tested most of all.
• WHY the order of organ-building cannot be shuffled — development is construction with dependencies: the heart must pump before a large body can be supplied, so it is built in the first month; organ systems must exist before they can be refined, so the first trimester is the great building phase and the rest is mostly growth. That is also why the first trimester is the most sensitive period for damage from infections, drugs or radiation — everything is under construction at once.
• EXAMPLE — The embryo-versus-foetus language follows the building schedule: the developing organism of the early weeks, while organs are forming, is the embryo; the later growth phase is the foetus. A question describing first movements in the fifth month is describing a foetus, not an embryo.
• WORKED PATTERN — Milestone questions present an event and four time slots. Do not memorise the table as a list; memorise it as a story with the four hooks (heart 1, limbs 2, systems 12 weeks, movements 5, eyelids 24 weeks). Then any event locates itself: "external genital organs well developed" can only sit at the 12-week hook.
• YOUR TURN — A scan at 10 weeks shows a normally developing pregnancy. Which of these should NOT yet be expected: heart sounds, recognisable external genital organs, or the beginnings of limbs? Answer: recognisable external genital organs — they are well developed by the end of 12 weeks, not at 10; the heart (1 month) and limb formation (by end of month 2) are already underway or done.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — First foetal movements are a fifth-month event, not a third-month one, and eyelids separate at 24 weeks — they do not open at 12 weeks. The other classic swap: "heart is formed by the end of the second month" — wrong; the heart belongs to the first month, limbs and digits to the second.

Parturition — Birth Runs on Positive Feedback
• DEFINITION — The average duration of human pregnancy is about nine months — the gestation period. At the end of it, vigorous contractions of the uterus cause expulsion of the fully developed foetus: this delivery process is parturition, and it is induced by a complex neuroendocrine mechanism.
• DEFINITION — The signals for parturition originate from the fully developed foetus and the placenta. They induce mild uterine contractions called the foetal ejection reflex. This reflex triggers the release of oxytocin from the maternal pituitary, and oxytocin acts on the uterine muscle (the myometrium) to cause stronger contractions.
✔ KEY POINT — Parturition is the chapter's one positive-feedback loop: contractions release oxytocin, oxytocin strengthens contractions, stronger contractions release still more oxytocin — the loop escalates until the baby is expelled through the birth canal, and soon after, the placenta is expelled too. Contrast it mentally with the negative feedback of everyday hormone control: here the response amplifies the signal instead of cancelling it, which is exactly what an all-or-nothing event like birth needs.
• WHY the baby helps trigger its own birth — a birth that started before the foetus was fully developed would be a disaster, so the starting signal is wired to the one party who knows readiness first-hand: the foetus and its placenta. Maternal hormones alone run on the mother's timetable; the foetal ejection reflex keys the event to the foetus's maturity.
• EXAMPLE — The feedback structure is visible in the delivery room: contractions begin mild and spaced, then grow stronger and closer together in a self-reinforcing climb — not a steady plateau. Doctors can also induce or strengthen labour with oxytocin injections, entering the same loop from outside.
• WORKED PATTERN — Any question on this section reduces to a four-link chain: fully developed foetus and placenta signal, foetal ejection reflex, oxytocin from the maternal pituitary, stronger myometrial contractions — and then back to more oxytocin. If an option inserts progesterone or relaxin as the contraction driver, it has left the chain; oxytocin is the only hormone in the loop.
• YOUR TURN — Why does labour not stop halfway once it has properly begun — what property of the mechanism guarantees completion? Answer: positive feedback. Each contraction increases the oxytocin release that drives the next, stronger contraction, so the process is self-amplifying and runs to completion (delivery) rather than settling at an intermediate level.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Oxytocin comes from the mother's pituitary, not from the placenta and not from the foetus; the foetus supplies the signal (the ejection reflex), the mother supplies the hormone. Also, parturition expels the placenta after the baby — a sequence question that delivers the placenta first has reversed the order.

Lactation — Colostrum, the First Vaccine
• DEFINITION — Lactation is milk production by the mammary glands, which undergo differentiation during pregnancy and start producing milk towards the end of it. Milk flows through the duct chain built in the earlier section: alveoli to mammary tubules to mammary ducts to the mammary ampulla to the lactiferous duct.
• DEFINITION — The milk produced during the initial few days of lactation is called colostrum. It contains several antibodies and is absolutely essential to develop the resistance of the newborn baby — a borrowed immune system, delivered as food, at exactly the age when the baby's own immune system is still immature.
✔ KEY POINT — Colostrum is the exam's favourite lactation fact: first milk, first few days, loaded with antibodies, essential for the newborn's resistance. And the recommendation follows from it: doctors recommend breast-feeding during the initial period of infant growth for bringing up a healthy baby — it delivers nutrition and immunity in one package.
• WHY borrowed antibodies are needed at all — the baby is born with an immune system that has met almost nothing. Colostrum's antibodies cover the gap during the first days and weeks, protecting the gut and body while the infant's own defences are being built — a handover of protection from the mother's experienced immune system to the baby's new one.
• EXAMPLE — Connect the two ends of the chapter: the mammary alveoli that were mapped as anatomy in the female-system section are the same alveoli that now secrete milk; the duct chain (tubules, duct, ampulla, lactiferous duct) is tested once as structure and again as the route of milk. Learn it once, collect both marks.
• WORKED PATTERN — Lactation questions are usually one-fact probes wearing disguises: "first milk rich in antibodies" is colostrum; "hormone for milk ejection (let-down)" points to oxytocin's action on the mammary glands in general physiology; "milk production" is associated with prolactin. Keep the colostrum fact separate and it will never be traded away by an option.
• YOUR TURN — A newborn is fed formula from day one and never receives colostrum. What protection has it missed? Answer: the ready-made maternal antibodies in colostrum; the newborn's own immune system is immature and cannot manufacture equivalent antibodies fast enough, so the borrowed passive immunity of the first days is irreplaceable on that timescale.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Colostrum is not "weak" or "incomplete" milk — it is the antibody-rich first secretion, and calling it low-value is the standard wrong statement. Also fix the timing: mammary glands differentiate during pregnancy and produce milk towards the end of pregnancy and after delivery; they are not switched on by fertilisation.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Crediting Sertoli cells with androgen production. Why it is wrong: Sertoli cells lie inside the seminiferous tubule and nourish the germ cells; androgens are synthesised and secreted by the Leydig (interstitial) cells in the spaces outside the tubules, under LH. Correct principle: inside the tubule feeds (Sertoli, FSH), outside the tubule secretes (Leydig, LH).
• Mistake 2: Treating the epididymis as a gland or placing fructose in its secretion. Why it is wrong: the epididymis is a duct where sperms are stored and mature; fructose-rich seminal plasma comes from the seminal vesicles and prostate, and lubrication comes from the bulbourethral glands. Correct principle: sort every part into gonad, duct or gland before answering what it produces.
• Mistake 3: Saying one primary spermatocyte produces one sperm, or keeping the secondary spermatocyte diploid. Why it is wrong: meiosis I converts one 2n primary spermatocyte into two haploid secondary spermatocytes, and meiosis II doubles them into four spermatids, all of which become sperms. Correct principle: run the tree 1 to 2 to 4, and halve the chromosomes exactly once, at meiosis I.
• Mistake 4: Confusing spermiogenesis with spermiation, or calling either a division. Why it is wrong: spermiogenesis is the transformation of a spermatid into a sperm; spermiation is the release of the finished sperm from the Sertoli cell; neither changes cell number or ploidy. Correct principle: divisions change counts; -genesis changes shape; -ation (spermiation) changes location.
• Mistake 5: Claiming oogenesis begins at puberty and that the ovary keeps making oogonia. Why it is wrong: oogenesis is initiated during embryonic development, several million oogonia form in the fetal ovary, and none are formed or added after birth; puberty merely resumes meiosis in a few follicles. Correct principle: the female stock is fixed before birth; the male stock renews from puberty.
• Mistake 6: Saying ovulation releases a mature ovum. Why it is wrong: the Graafian follicle releases a secondary oocyte arrested in its second meiotic division; it becomes an ovum only after a sperm enters and meiosis II completes. Correct principle: ovulated cell equals secondary oocyte; finished ovum equals post-fertilisation-entry.
• Mistake 7: Naming estrogen or progesterone as the trigger of ovulation. Why it is wrong: ovulation is induced by the LH surge, the mid-cycle peak of luteinising hormone around day 14; estrogen dominates before it and progesterone (from the corpus luteum) after it. Correct principle: FSH grows, estrogen builds, LH releases, progesterone maintains — in that order.
• Mistake 8: Placing fertilisation in the uterus or the isthmus. Why it is wrong: fertilisation occurs in the ampulla, the wider region of the fallopian tube; the embryo then travels through the isthmus while cleaving and implants in the uterus days later. Correct principle: meeting in the ampulla, journey through the isthmus, implantation in the uterus.
• Mistake 9: Assigning the placenta to the inner cell mass, or the embryo to the trophoblast. Why it is wrong: in the blastocyst the outer trophoblast attaches to the endometrium and builds the chorionic villi of the placenta, while the inner cell mass becomes the embryo proper. Correct principle: trophoblast makes the lifeline; inner cell mass makes the life.
• Mistake 10: Listing relaxin among the placental hormones. Why it is wrong: the placenta secretes hCG, hPL, estrogens and progestogens; relaxin is secreted by the ovary in the later phase of pregnancy. Correct principle: the placental set has exactly four members in NCERT — hCG, hPL, estrogens, progestogens.
• Mistake 11: Making oxytocin a placental hormone that starts labour by itself. Why it is wrong: the signals originate from the fully developed foetus and placenta (foetal ejection reflex), but the oxytocin itself is released from the maternal pituitary, and it then drives stronger myometrial contractions in positive feedback. Correct principle: foetus signals, mother's pituitary secretes, uterus obeys — and the loop amplifies.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: Starting from 25 primary spermatocytes in a testis, how many secondary spermatocytes, spermatids and sperms will be produced, assuming all divisions complete normally?
• Given: 25 primary spermatocytes, each diploid (2n); spermatogenesis with meiosis I, meiosis II and spermiogenesis.
• Principle: Each primary spermatocyte gives 2 secondary spermatocytes at meiosis I, 4 spermatids at meiosis II, and 4 sperms after spermiogenesis.
• Steps: Secondary spermatocytes: 25 x 2 = 50. Spermatids: 50 x 2 = 100. Sperms: 100 spermatids transform one-to-one, so 100 sperms.
• Answer: 50 secondary spermatocytes, 100 spermatids and 100 sperms.
• Reasoning: The tree multiplies by two at each meiotic division and by one at spermiogenesis — spermiogenesis changes shape, not number, so the last step adds nothing to the count.
• Mistake to avoid: Doubling again at spermiogenesis to claim 200 sperms, or forgetting meiosis II and reporting 50 sperms.
• Example 2 — Problem: A fertility report counts 250 million sperms in an ejaculate. Using the NCERT fertility criteria, find the minimum number that must have normal shape and size, and the minimum number that must show vigorous motility.
• Given: Total sperms 250 million; criteria: at least 60 percent normal shape and size; at least 40 percent vigorous motility.
• Principle: Apply each percentage to the same total independently — the two criteria grade different qualities of the ejaculate.
• Steps: Normal shape: 60 percent of 250 million = 0.6 x 250 = 150 million. Vigorous motility: 40 percent of 250 million = 0.4 x 250 = 100 million.
• Answer: At least 150 million normally shaped sperms and at least 100 million vigorously motile sperms.
• Reasoning: Both thresholds come straight from the NCERT statement of normal fertility; the arithmetic is only percentage-of-total, and the two sets overlap freely — a sperm can count in both.
• Mistake to avoid: Applying the 40 percent to the 150 million survivors of the first filter. The criteria are parallel, not sequential.
• Example 3 — Problem: Trace, in order, every structure a sperm passes through from its production site to the outside of the body, and name the one duct it shares with urine.
• Given: Production in seminiferous tubules; duct system of rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, urethra.
• Principle: The ducts form a single fixed chain out of the testis; the urethra, running from the urinary bladder through the penis, is the common final passage.
• Steps: Seminiferous tubule to rete testis; rete testis to vasa efferentia; vasa efferentia to epididymis; epididymis to vas deferens; vas deferens (looping over the bladder, joined by the seminal vesicle duct) to ejaculatory duct through the prostate; ejaculatory duct to urethra; urethra to the urethral meatus.
• Answer: Tubules, rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, urethra — and the shared duct is the urethra.
• Reasoning: The chain has no branches for the sperm; glands pour secretions into it but the sperm's own road is this single sequence. Any shuffled option breaks the chain at exactly one link.
• Mistake to avoid: Placing the epididymis before the vasa efferentia, or sending the vas deferens into the bladder instead of over it.
• Example 4 — Problem: In a regular 28-day cycle, menstruation begins on 5 January. Estimate (a) the day of ovulation, (b) the phase and dominant hormone on 24 January, and (c) what happens to the corpus luteum if fertilisation does not occur.
• Given: Cycle length 28 days; day 1 is 5 January; ovulation at about day 14; luteal phase follows ovulation.
• Principle: Count cycle days from the first day of flow: ovulation about day 14; days 15-28 are luteal, dominated by progesterone from the corpus luteum; without fertilisation the corpus luteum degenerates.
• Steps: (a) Day 14 is 5 plus 13 = 18 January. (b) 24 January is day 20, mid-luteal phase; the corpus luteum is secreting large amounts of progesterone and the endometrium is being maintained. (c) The corpus luteum degenerates (to corpus albicans), progesterone falls, the endometrium breaks down and the next menstrual phase begins around 1-2 February.
• Answer: (a) About 18 January. (b) Luteal phase, progesterone dominant. (c) It degenerates, progesterone collapses, menstruation follows.
• Reasoning: Every date answer is a distance from the day-14 anchor; every hormone answer follows from which structure is in charge during that phase (follicle before, corpus luteum after).
• Mistake to avoid: Counting day 14 from the end of menstruation instead of its first day, or assigning the 24 January state to estrogen, whose peak passed before ovulation.
• Example 5 — Problem: A primary oocyte and a primary spermatocyte each complete gametogenesis. Compare their outputs: number of functional gametes, and what happens to the discarded material in each case.
• Given: Male: primary spermatocyte 2n; female: primary oocyte 2n arrested since fetal life; both complete both meiotic divisions.
• Principle: Male meiosis divides cytoplasm equally at both divisions; female meiosis divides cytoplasm unequally, keeping it in one cell.
• Steps: Spermatocyte: meiosis I gives 2 secondary spermatocytes, meiosis II gives 4 spermatids, all 4 become sperms — 4 functional gametes, nothing discarded. Oocyte: meiosis I gives 1 secondary oocyte plus first polar body; meiosis II (on sperm entry) gives 1 ovum plus second polar body — 1 functional gamete; the polar bodies, carrying the discarded chromosome sets with almost no cytoplasm, degenerate.
• Answer: Four functional sperms versus one functional ovum; the female line discards two polar bodies, the male line discards nothing cellular (only cytoplasm shed during spermiogenesis).
• Reasoning: The contrast is the design logic of the whole chapter: sperms are cheap chromosome couriers made in bulk; the ovum is a provisioned ship that must carry the early embryo's entire cytoplasmic supply, so its divisions concentrate rather than share.
• Mistake to avoid: Claiming the polar bodies are small ova that could also be fertilised, or that the oocyte produces four cells of equal size.
• Example 6 — Problem: A blastocyst reaches the uterus, but its trophoblast fails to attach to the endometrium while the inner cell mass remains healthy. Predict the outcome and explain which later structures are affected.
• Given: Blastocyst with functional inner cell mass; trophoblast unable to attach; implantation requires trophoblast attachment to endometrium.
• Principle: Implantation is the trophoblast's job; the placenta is built from the trophoblast's chorionic villi interlocked with uterine tissue.
• Steps: No attachment means no implantation, so the blastocyst is not embedded and cannot draw maternal support. Without implantation there are no chorionic villi anchored in uterine tissue, so no placenta forms. The inner cell mass, though healthy, has no supply line and cannot develop further in the uterus.
• Answer: Pregnancy is not established; the blastocyst is lost with the next menstrual flow, and neither placenta nor continued embryonic development follows.
• Reasoning: The two blastocyst populations are a division of labour, not a duplication — the lifeline must be built before the life it serves can proceed, which is why implantation failure is a failure of pregnancy itself.
• Mistake to avoid: Expecting the inner cell mass to implant directly or to form its own placenta; both belong to the trophoblast lineage.
• Example 7 — Problem: During labour, a monitor shows contractions growing steadily stronger and closer together over two hours rather than holding a constant strength. Using the mechanism of parturition, explain the pattern and name the hormone and gland responsible for the escalation.
• Given: Parturition is driven by the foetal ejection reflex and oxytocin; contractions strengthen progressively.
• Principle: This is positive feedback: uterine contractions stimulate further oxytocin release, and oxytocin intensifies the contractions, so each round is stronger than the last.
• Steps: The fully developed foetus and placenta signal mild contractions (foetal ejection reflex). The reflex triggers oxytocin release from the maternal pituitary. Oxytocin acts on the myometrium, producing stronger contractions. Stronger contractions intensify the reflex and release more oxytocin. The loop repeats and escalates until the baby is expelled, followed by the placenta.
• Answer: The escalating pattern is positive feedback; the hormone is oxytocin, released from the maternal pituitary.
• Reasoning: A negative-feedback system would settle at a steady level; an escalating trace is the fingerprint of a self-amplifying loop, and parturition is the chapter's textbook example of one.
• Mistake to avoid: Attributing the escalation to progesterone (which maintains pregnancy and falls in influence before labour) or placing oxytocin's release in the placenta or foetus.

• A1. Male germ cells (spermatogonia), which divide and mature into sperms — acted on indirectly through the process they run; and Sertoli cells, which provide nutrition to the germ cells — acted on by FSH. Misconception watch: Sertoli cells do not secrete androgens, and LH does not act on Sertoli cells in this account — the pairings are Sertoli-FSH-nutrition and Leydig-LH-androgens.
• A2. Seminiferous tubules, rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, urethra (opening at the urethral meatus). Storage and maturation happen in the epididymis. Misconception watch: the vas deferens is a transport road, not the store; and the vasa efferentia come before the epididymis, not after.
• A3. Meiosis I: two secondary spermatocytes, 23 chromosomes each (already haploid). Meiosis II: four spermatids, 23 chromosomes each. After spermiogenesis: four sperms. Misconception watch: the chromosome number halves at meiosis I only; students who halve again at meiosis II invent sperms with 11 or 12 chromosomes.
• A4. The female divisions are unequal: at each meiotic division almost all cytoplasm goes to one daughter cell, so one primary oocyte yields one large, well-provisioned ovum. The discarded cells are the first and second polar bodies, which carry the spare chromosome sets with minimal cytoplasm and degenerate. Misconception watch: polar bodies are not small ova and are not fertilised; they are the disposal route that lets the ovum keep the cytoplasm.
• A5. Ovulation occurs at about day 14, caused by the LH surge (the mid-cycle peak of luteinising hormone rupturing the Graafian follicle). The endometrium is then maintained by progesterone, secreted in large amounts by the corpus luteum formed from the ruptured follicle. Misconception watch: estrogen peaks before ovulation and builds the lining, but it neither triggers ovulation nor maintains the lining afterwards — those are LH's and progesterone's jobs.
• A6. Fertilisation occurs in the ampulla of the fallopian tube. The acrosome supplies enzymes that help the sperm penetrate the zona pellucida and oocyte membrane. Sperm entry triggers (1) membrane changes that block polyspermy and (2) completion of the oocyte's second meiotic division, producing the ovum and the second polar body, after which the nuclei fuse. Misconception watch: the ovum does not exist before fertilisation begins — it is created by the sperm's entry.
• A7. Placenta secretes hCG, hPL, estrogens and progestogens. The outsider is relaxin, secreted by the ovary in the later phase of pregnancy. Misconception watch: oxytocin is also not placental (it is pituitary), but the classic planted option is relaxin because it sounds like a pregnancy hormone — and it is one, just from the ovary.
• A8. Order: zygote (formed in the ampulla) — morula, 8-16 blastomeres (travelling through the tube into the uterus) — blastocyst (in the uterus, trophoblast outside and inner cell mass inside) — implantation (blastocyst embedded in the endometrium). The trophoblast forms the placenta (via chorionic villi); the inner cell mass forms the baby. Misconception watch: the morula does not implant, and the inner cell mass never forms the placenta — the two layers swap in careless answers and both halves then fail.

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: The male system is a cooled external factory: testes in the scrotum (2-2.5 degrees below body temperature) hold about 250 lobules of seminiferous tubules, where Sertoli cells nurse germ cells into sperms while Leydig cells outside secrete androgens; the product exits through rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct and urethra, mixed with fructose-rich seminal plasma and bulbourethral lubricant.
• Summary: The female system runs on a stock fixed before birth: oogonia become primary oocytes arrested in prophase I inside follicles, one follicle per month climbs the ladder primary, secondary, tertiary, Graafian, and ovulation releases a secondary oocyte into the fimbriae of a 10-12 cm tube whose ampulla is the meeting place and whose partner organ, the uterus, renews its endometrium every cycle under estrogen and progesterone.
• Summary: The two gamete ledgers are mirror opposites: one primary spermatocyte divides evenly into four sperms, continuously from puberty; one primary oocyte divides unevenly into one cytoplasm-rich ovum plus disposable polar bodies, finishing meiosis II only if a sperm enters at fertilisation in the ampulla — where the block to polyspermy, the completion of meiosis and the fusion into a 2n zygote all follow in strict order, with the sperm's X or Y deciding the child's sex.
• Summary: Development then runs on timetables: cleavage without growth gives the morula and then the blastocyst (trophoblast outside, inner cell mass inside); implantation embeds it; the placenta — chorionic villi interlocked with uterine tissue — exchanges gases, nutrients and wastes and secretes hCG, hPL, estrogens and progestogens while the germ layers build a body whose milestones run heart at one month, limbs at two, systems by 12 weeks, movements at five months and eyelids at 24 weeks; birth is an oxytocin positive-feedback loop, and colostrum hands the newborn its first antibodies.
• Revision checklist — I can recite the sperm duct chain (tubules, rete testis, vasa efferentia, epididymis, vas deferens, ejaculatory duct, urethra) and pair each accessory gland with its secretion.
• Revision checklist — I can place Sertoli (inside, nutrition, FSH) and Leydig (outside, androgens, LH) correctly and explain why the testes are kept 2-2.5 degrees cooler than the body.
• Revision checklist — I can run both gametogenesis ledgers with ploidy at every step: 2n to n at meiosis I only, four sperms per primary spermatocyte, one ovum plus two polar bodies per primary oocyte.
• Revision checklist — I can climb the follicle ladder (primary, secondary, tertiary with antrum, Graafian) and state what ovulation actually releases.
• Revision checklist — I can narrate the 28-day cycle as a hormone chain: FSH grows the follicle, estrogen rebuilds the endometrium, the LH surge ovulates at day 14, progesterone from the corpus luteum maintains the lining.
• Revision checklist — I can give the fertilisation script in order: ampulla, zona contact, acrosomal entry, polyspermy block, meiosis II completed, nuclei fuse, 2n zygote — and explain why the father determines sex.
• Revision checklist — I can separate trophoblast from inner cell mass, list the placenta's three jobs and four hormones, and keep relaxin (ovary) and oxytocin (pituitary) outside the placental set.
• Revision checklist — I can pin the milestones to their hooks (heart 1 month, limbs and digits 2 months, major systems 12 weeks, movements 5 months, eyelids 24 weeks) and explain parturition as positive feedback and colostrum as borrowed immunity.
