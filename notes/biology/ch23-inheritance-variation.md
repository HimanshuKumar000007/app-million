Introduction — Inheritance, the Rules Hidden in a Pea Patch
• Why does a tall pea plant sometimes produce dwarf children, why can two brown-eyed parents have a blue-eyed baby, and why does your blood group refuse to be a blend of your parents' groups? Inheritance looks like a lottery until you learn its rules — and the astonishing part of this chapter is that the rules were worked out by a monk counting peas, decades before anyone had seen a chromosome or heard of DNA.
• DEFINITION — Inheritance is the process by which characters are passed from parents to offspring. The basis of heredity is this transmission, and the small differences that appear among individuals of a species — even between parents and offspring — are called variation. Heredity plus variation is what genetics studies.
• WHY Mendel matters more than Mendel's facts — Mendel did not just discover three laws; he invented the method: choose clean contrasting traits, count large numbers of offspring, and let ratios speak. Every genetics problem you will ever solve in an exam is run on his machinery — crosses, gametes, Punnett squares and ratios.
• EXAMPLE — A gardener in the 1850s crossing tall and dwarf peas got only tall plants in the first generation — and then, in the next generation, dwarfs came back in a precise one-in-four pattern. That quiet reappearance is the single most important observation in genetics: a trait can be carried invisibly and still be inherited intact.
• WORKED PATTERN — The chapter's master key is one sentence: the phenotype you see is produced by a genotype you cannot see, and the genotype is made of paired factors (alleles) that separate when gametes form. Everything in this chapter — test crosses, blood groups, linkage, pedigrees, disorders — is that sentence applied in a new costume.
• YOUR TURN — Two tall pea plants are crossed and some offspring are dwarf. What does the appearance of dwarf offspring prove about the parents? Answer: both parents carried the dwarf allele hidden in heterozygous form — a recessive trait can skip a visible generation without being lost.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Read this chapter as a courtroom story: Mendel proposes the rules, Sutton and Boveri find where the factors live, Morgan catches them misbehaving (linkage), and the disorders at the end are what happens when the script itself carries a misprint.

Mendel's Toolkit — Pea Plants and Seven Contrasting Traits
• DEFINITION — A true-breeding line is one that has undergone continuous self-pollination for several generations and shows a stable trait inheritance — every tall plant in the line gives only tall offspring. Mendel first built true-breeding lines for each version of every trait, so that every cross started from known, pure parents.
• DEFINITION — Mendel selected 14 true-breeding pea plant varieties, as pairs showing contrasting traits for seven characters: stem height (tall or dwarf), flower colour (violet or white), flower position (axial or terminal), pod shape (inflated or constricted), pod colour (green or yellow), seed shape (round or wrinkled) and seed colour (yellow or green).
• WHY the pea plant was a genius choice — pea is naturally self-pollinating, so lines stay pure, yet its flowers can be artificially cross-pollinated by hand; it has a short life cycle and produces many offspring per cross, which turns genetics into countable statistics instead of anecdotes. Mendel worked on these experiments for seven years, from 1856 to 1863.
• EXAMPLE — Take seed colour: one true-breeding line always made yellow seeds, its partner always green. Cross them and every hybrid seed was yellow — the green had not mixed or diluted, it had simply gone quiet. Blend-inheritance thinking died right there: inheritance is particulate, passed in discrete units.
• WORKED PATTERN — Mendel's experimental grammar, which every later section uses: P is the parental generation of true-breeding opposites; F1 is the first filial (offspring) generation, all hybrids; selfing or crossing F1 plants gives the F2 generation, where hidden traits resurface in countable ratios. Learn the labels P, F1 and F2 now — every cross in this chapter is written in them.
• YOUR TURN — Why did Mendel count thousands of offspring instead of reporting the few crosses that merely "looked right"? Answer: because the laws of inheritance are ratios — 3:1 or 9:3:3:1 patterns only become visible, and convincing, in large numbers; small samples drown the pattern in chance.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The seven traits are seven characters with two contrasting forms each — do not inflate the count to fourteen characters. And note that Mendel's traits show complete dominance: in every F1 hybrid, only one of the two parental forms appeared. That luck is exactly why his ratios were so clean — later sections show traits where dominance is incomplete.

The Monohybrid Cross — One Trait, One Clean Ratio
• DEFINITION — A monohybrid cross follows one character with two contrasting traits — for example tall (TT) crossed with dwarf (tt). The F1 generation is entirely tall but hybrid (Tt); selfing the F1 gives an F2 with a phenotypic ratio of 3 tall : 1 dwarf and a genotypic ratio of 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.
• WHY the dwarf returns unchanged — in the F1 plant the two factors T and t sit side by side in every cell, yet the dwarf plants of F2 are exactly as dwarf as the original parent. The factors did not contaminate each other in the hybrid: they stayed discrete, separated cleanly when gametes formed, and reassembled at fertilisation. Mendel called the unseen units factors; we call them genes, and their versions alleles.
• EXAMPLE — Run the cross as a Punnett square (Figure 23.1): the F1 Tt plant makes two kinds of gametes, T and t, in equal numbers, from both parents. The four equally likely combinations are TT, Tt, Tt and tt — three boxes carry at least one T and look tall; one box is tt and is dwarf. The 3:1 ratio is not magic; it is the arithmetic of two coin flips.
• WORKED PATTERN — The monohybrid recipe you will reuse all chapter: (1) write each parent's genotype; (2) list the gametes each parent can make — one allele of the pair per gamete; (3) combine them in a Punnett square or by multiplication; (4) read phenotypes off the genotypes using the dominance rule. Any cross question, however dressed up, is these four steps.
• YOUR TURN — In an F2 of 800 pea plants from Tt crossed with Tt, how many do you expect to be dwarf, and how many of the tall ones are pure-breeding? Answer: about 200 dwarf (one quarter tt); of the 600 tall, about 200 are TT and breed true, while 400 are Tt carriers.
✔ KEY POINT — F2 of a monohybrid cross: phenotypic ratio 3:1, genotypic ratio 1:2:1. The gap between the two ratios is the whole insight — appearances hide genotypes, and only breeding tests reveal them.

Dominance and Segregation — Mendel's First Two Conclusions
• DEFINITION — Law of Dominance: characters are controlled by discrete units called factors, which occur in pairs; in a dissimilar pair, one member dominates the other and is expressed in the offspring, while the other, recessive member stays hidden. This explains why the F1 shows only one parental trait and why the hidden trait reappears in about one quarter of the F2.
• DEFINITION — Law of Segregation: the two alleles of a pair do not blend and do not alter each other in the hybrid; during gamete formation they segregate (separate) from each other, so that a gamete receives only one of the two alleles — a gamete is always pure for the trait. The zygote's pair is rebuilt at fertilisation.
• WHY segregation is the deeper law — dominance only describes what a heterozygote looks like; segregation describes what it passes on. Segregation is why recessive traits survive being rare and hidden: the allele is never diluted, never half-strength in the carrier — it waits, intact, in half the carrier's gametes.
• EXAMPLE — A single Tt plant is a walking demonstration: it looks completely tall (dominance), yet half its pollen and half its eggs carry t (segregation). Self it, and the tt combination — two t gametes meeting — rebuilds the dwarf in one quarter of the offspring.
• WORKED PATTERN — Translate every "law" question into gametes. If a question asks why a ratio appears, do not recite the law's name first — list the gametes and their frequencies; the ratio falls out, and the law's name is simply the label for that gamete behaviour. Examiners award the gamete logic, not the vocabulary.
• YOUR TURN — A hybrid Tt is crossed with a pure tall TT. Will any dwarf offspring appear, and what fraction of offspring are carriers? Answer: no dwarfs — every offspring gets T from the TT parent, giving TT or Tt only; half the offspring (the Tt ones) are carriers.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Dominant" does not mean common, stronger, healthier or more frequent in a population; it means only "expressed in the heterozygote". A dominant allele can be rare (the allele for polydactyly is dominant) and a recessive allele can be widespread (the O blood-group allele i).

The Test Cross — Interrogating a Hidden Genotype
• DEFINITION — In a test cross, the organism showing the dominant phenotype — but unknown genotype — is crossed with the recessive parent. Because the recessive parent can contribute only the recessive allele, the offspring reveal the tested parent's gametes directly.
• WHY the test cross works so cleanly — a tall plant may be TT or Tt and no amount of looking can tell them apart. Cross it with tt: if the plant is TT, every offspring receives T and all are tall; if it is Tt, half its gametes carry t, those gametes meet t from the tester, and half the offspring are dwarf. One dwarf offspring is proof the parent was Tt.
• EXAMPLE — Plant breeders and animal breeders use the test cross as a routine lie detector: a show dog that might carry a recessive coat fault, a champion bull that might carry a recessive defect — mate it to the recessive type and read the offspring like a genotype printout.
• WORKED PATTERN — The test-cross signature ratio: a monohybrid test cross (Tt with tt) gives 1:1 — one dominant phenotype to one recessive phenotype — mirroring the heterozygote's two gamete types in equal numbers. Whenever an exam shows you a 1:1 from a cross with a recessive, you are looking at a heterozygote being read out.
• YOUR TURN — A violet-flowered pea plant (violet is dominant) is test-crossed and 30 offspring are violet, 28 white. What was the parent's genotype? Answer: heterozygous (Vv) — the near 1:1 split is the test cross revealing one hidden recessive allele in the parent.
✔ KEY POINT — Test cross equals diagnosis of genotype: all dominant offspring means homozygous dominant parent; a 1:1 split means heterozygous parent. The recessive tester is the perfect witness because it adds nothing that can mask the answer.

Incomplete Dominance — The Heterozygote Blends
• DEFINITION — Incomplete dominance is the phenomenon in which neither of the two alleles of a gene is completely dominant over the other, so the F1 hybrid shows a phenotype intermediate between the two parents — a fine mixture of the expression of the two alleles.
• EXAMPLE — Cross a true-breeding red-flowered snapdragon (RR) with a true-breeding white one (rr) and every F1 flower is pink (Rr) — not red, not white, but halfway. The same story runs in Mirabilis jalapa, the four o'clock plant: red crossed with white gives pink. Mendel's peas never did this, which is precisely why his dominance law looked universal.
• DEFINITION — When the pink F1 is selfed, the F2 gives red, pink and white in the ratio 1:2:1 — the phenotypic ratio becomes identical to the genotypic ratio, because each genotype now has its own visible phenotype. Nothing has blended at the allele level: red and white reappear pure in F2, proving the alleles stayed discrete even while their effects mixed.
• WHY the blend happens — picture the dominant allele as an instruction to make pigment. Two working copies (RR) make full pigment: red. One working copy (Rr) makes about half: pink. No working copy (rr): white. The phenotype blends because the product amount halves — a dosage effect — while the alleles themselves remain untouched and separable.
• WORKED PATTERN — Spot incomplete dominance instantly: F1 matches neither parent, and the F2 ratio is 1:2:1 for phenotypes. The moment a problem gives a 1:2:1 phenotypic ratio in a single-gene cross, write "incomplete dominance" and assign the middle class to the heterozygote.
• YOUR TURN — In Mirabilis, a pink-flowered plant is crossed with a white-flowered plant. What offspring do you expect? Answer: half pink (Rr) and half white (rr) — the pink parent gives R or r gametes, the white parent only r, so no red can appear.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Incomplete dominance does NOT mean the alleles blended into a permanent pink gene. The clean 1:2:1 F2 with pure parental colours returning is the disproof of blending — if alleles truly mixed, red and white could never be recovered. State this whenever a question hints that blending disproves Mendel; it modifies dominance, not segregation.

Codominance and the ABO Blood Groups
• DEFINITION — Codominance is the condition in which both alleles of a heterozygote express themselves fully and side by side, so the F1 resembles both parents at once rather than an intermediate between them. The heterozygote shows two products, not a halved one.
• EXAMPLE — Human ABO blood groups are the textbook case. The gene I has three alleles: IA and IB each produce a slightly different form of sugar (antigen) on red blood cells, while i produces none. A person of genotype IA IB makes both the A sugar and the B sugar — blood group AB. Neither allele hides the other: that is codominance in action on your own blood cells.
• DEFINITION — The ABO system gives six genotypes but only four phenotypes: IA IA and IA i give group A; IB IB and IB i give group B; IA IB gives group AB; and i i gives group O. IA and IB are both completely dominant over i, yet codominant with each other — one gene showing two different dominance relations at once.
• WHY AB differs from pink — a pink snapdragon has one product at half dose; AB blood has two different products at full display. Blend of quantity versus coexistence of kinds: that single contrast separates incomplete dominance from codominance in any exam question.
• WORKED PATTERN — For ABO problems, first write each parent's possible gametes from genotype (a group A parent may be IA IA or IA i — unknowns multiply cases), then combine and read the sugar logic: any IA present shows A, any IB shows B, both show AB, neither shows O. A group O child (i i) is forensic gold: it proves each parent donated an i, so both parents must carry i whatever their visible group.
• YOUR TURN — A group A mother and a group B father have a group O child. Write all three genotypes. Answer: mother IA i, father IB i, child i i — the O child forces both parents to be carriers of i; without that child, both could have been homozygotes.
✔ KEY POINT — ABO in one line: three alleles (IA, IB, i), six genotypes, four phenotypes; IA and IB codominant with each other, both dominant over i. It is simultaneously the example for codominance AND for multiple alleles.

Multiple Alleles and Pleiotropy — More Than Two, More Than One
• DEFINITION — Multiple alleles exist when a gene has more than two alternative forms in the population. Any one diploid individual still carries only two of them — one on each homologous chromosome — but the population as a whole offers three or more choices. The ABO gene, with IA, IB and i, is the standard example.
• WHY this modification matters — Mendel's two-allele picture was a special case of a general system. Once you accept a population-level menu of alleles, blood groups, coat colours and many enzyme variants all fit the same segregation machinery: the individual still passes one allele per gamete; only the menu is longer.
• DEFINITION — Pleiotropy is the phenomenon in which a single gene exhibits multiple phenotypic expressions — one gene, many effects. In sickle-cell anaemia, one changed gene shows up as altered haemoglobin, sickled red cells, anaemia, organ effects and changed malaria resistance — many characters from a single molecular cause.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT's second pleiotropy example is starch synthesis in pea seeds, controlled by one gene with alleles B and b. BB seeds make large starch grains and are round; bb seeds make smaller grains and are wrinkled; the heterozygote Bb makes intermediate-sized starch grains — yet for seed shape Bb still looks round. One gene, two traits (grain size and seed shape), and dominance behaving differently for each: B dominates for shape but shows incomplete dominance for grain size.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify any "one gene, many effects" description as pleiotropy and any "three-plus forms of one gene" description as multiple alleles — and never confuse either with polygenic inheritance, where many genes build one trait. The arrow directions differ: pleiotropy is one to many, polygenic is many to one.
• YOUR TURN — A gene controls both the shape of a fly's wing and the number of bristles on its body. Name the phenomenon, and state what would NOT count as an example. Answer: pleiotropy; a trait like human height, built by many genes, would not — that is polygenic inheritance, the reverse arrangement.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — An individual can never carry three alleles of one gene (normal diploid), even when the population has a dozen. Questions phrase it slyly — "a person with blood group AB carries IA, IB and i" is false; the AB person carries exactly IA and IB.

Polygenic Inheritance — Traits That Come in Shades
• DEFINITION — Polygenic inheritance is inheritance in which a single trait is controlled by three or more genes, with each dominant allele adding a similar quantitative contribution. The combined effect produces continuous variation — a smooth range of phenotypes rather than a few separate classes.
• EXAMPLE — Human skin colour is NCERT's example: controlled by three genes (call them A, B and C in outline), where the dominant alleles darken the skin and the recessive ones lighten it. A person with all six dominant alleles is darkest, one with none is lightest, and the great majority fall in between — which is why skin colour forms a gradient, not categories.
• EXAMPLE — The historical demonstration was Nilsson-Ehle's wheat kernel colour: two gene pairs produced red kernels in graded shades from deep red to white, with the number of dominant alleles (four, three, two, one, none) setting the intensity — proof that several Mendelian genes, each segregating normally, can together imitate smooth blending at the whole-trait level.
• WHY Mendel never met these traits — he chose characters with clean either-or forms. Quantitative traits hide their genes behind statistics: with several additive genes, F2 classes merge into a bell curve and no simple ratio survives. The genes still segregate by Mendel's rules; only the bookkeeping changes.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any polygenic question, count dominant alleles: the phenotype is set by the count, from maximum (all dominant) to minimum (none). If a question offers genotypes, tally the capital letters — the tally, not the particular combination, fixes the shade. Environment then nudges the result, because quantitative traits also respond to nutrition, sunlight and upbringing.
• YOUR TURN — Two parents of intermediate skin colour (AaBbCc, three dominant alleles each) have a child. Can the child be darker than both parents, and can it be lighter? Answer: yes to both — segregation can concentrate up to six dominant alleles in one child (darkest class) or none (lightest), which is why children of two intermediate parents span almost the whole range.
✔ KEY POINT — Three inheritance modifiers, three signatures: incomplete dominance — heterozygote intermediate, F2 1:2:1; codominance — heterozygote shows both, as in AB blood; polygenic — many genes, one trait, continuous shades, as in skin colour. Multiple alleles widen the menu; pleiotropy lets one gene pull many strings.

The Dihybrid Cross — Two Traits, Independent Assortment
• DEFINITION — A dihybrid cross follows two characters at once. Mendel crossed pea plants with yellow round seeds (YYRR) with plants having green wrinkled seeds (yyrr). The F1 were all yellow round (YyRr); selfing them gave an F2 phenotypic ratio of 9:3:3:1 — nine yellow round, three yellow wrinkled, three green round, one green wrinkled.
• WHY the new combinations are the real discovery — yellow wrinkled and green round seeds appear in F2 although neither parent plant had them. Seed colour and seed shape were inherited independently: a gamete carrying yellow was just as likely to carry round as wrinkled. The two genes did not drag each other along.
• DEFINITION — Law of Independent Assortment: when two pairs of contrasting characters are combined in a hybrid, the segregation of one pair of characters is independent of the other pair at the time of gamete formation — each pair shuffles on its own. This is the generalised form of the dihybrid result.
• WORKED PATTERN — Build the dihybrid without a 16-box square when speed matters: treat it as two monohybrid crosses multiplied. For each gene the F2 split is 3 dominant : 1 recessive; multiplying (3:1) by (3:1) gives 9:3:3:1 directly. The same forked-line trick predicts any multigene ratio — and it is exactly what Figure 23.2 displays cell by cell.
• EXAMPLE — A dihybrid test cross makes the law audible: YyRr crossed with yyrr gives four offspring types in equal 1:1:1:1 ratio, because YyRr makes four gamete types — YR, Yr, yR, yr — in equal numbers, and the tester contributes only yr. Equal gamete classes are the fingerprint of independent assortment; remember this fingerprint, because linkage breaks it in two sections' time.
• YOUR TURN — In a dihybrid F2 of 1600 seeds, how many do you expect to be green and round, and what is their genotype class? Answer: three sixteenths of 1600, which is 300 — genotype yyRR or yyRr, the green-round recombinant class.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Independent assortment applies to genes on different chromosomes — or, as Morgan showed, far apart on the same one. Mendel's seven genes happened to assort independently in his crosses, but the law has a condition attached; a question that puts two genes on the same chromosome is testing whether you know the exception.

The Chromosomal Theory — Sutton, Boveri and Parallel Lines
• DEFINITION — The chromosomal theory of inheritance, proposed independently by Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri in 1902, states that genes are located on chromosomes, and that the behaviour of chromosomes during meiosis is the physical basis of Mendel's laws. Sutton united knowledge of chromosomal segregation with Mendelian principles and called it the chromosomal theory of inheritance.
• WHY the parallel was impossible to ignore — lay the two lists side by side. Genes occur in pairs; chromosomes occur in pairs. Alleles segregate so a gamete gets one of each pair; homologous chromosomes separate so a gamete gets one of each pair. A zygote restores the pair by fertilisation in both stories. The knowledge of chromosomes was moving in exactly the same direction as Mendel's factors — because they were the same things.
• EXAMPLE — Segregation has a visible address: in meiosis I, homologous chromosomes separate (anaphase I), so each gamete physically receives one member of every pair — one allele of every gene. Independent assortment is the same event seen from another angle: different chromosome pairs orient independently at the metaphase plate, so a gene on chromosome 3 holds no leverage over a gene on chromosome 7.
• WORKED PATTERN — Map every Mendelian statement to its chromosomal event and you can answer "explain at the cellular level" questions: dominance is about gene expression in the heterozygote; segregation is homologous separation in anaphase I; independent assortment is independent orientation of bivalents in metaphase I; linkage (coming next) is physical togetherness on one DNA molecule.
• YOUR TURN — A student says independent assortment happens because alleles "choose freely". Correct the picture. Answer: nothing chooses; each pair of homologous chromosomes lines up in meiosis independently of the others, and which homologue faces which pole is a coin flip per pair — the combinations of gametes are the physical result.
✔ KEY POINT — Sutton and Boveri, 1902: chromosomes were identified as the vehicles of heredity because their meiotic behaviour parallels the behaviour of genes Mendel had inferred. The theory was proposed from observation — proof needed an experiment, and the experiment needed a fly.

Morgan and Drosophila — Proof Flies In
• DEFINITION — Thomas Hunt Morgan worked with Drosophila melanogaster, the tiny fruit fly, and provided experimental verification of the chromosomal theory — demonstrating that specific genes sit on specific chromosomes, including the first gene shown to ride on a sex chromosome: white eye on the X.
• WHY the fruit fly was the perfect instrument — Drosophila can be grown on simple synthetic medium in the laboratory; it completes its life cycle in about two weeks; a single mating produces a large number of progeny; males and females are easily distinguished; and it has many hereditary variations visible under a low-power microscope. Fast, cheap, countable and varied — Mendel's pea advantages, upgraded.
• EXAMPLE — Morgan crossed white-eyed males with red-eyed females: the F1 were all red-eyed, but in F2 the white eye returned only in males (in his famous cross, roughly half the males), never in females of that cross. The trait's itinerary followed the X chromosome exactly — the gene for eye colour must physically sit on the X, the first concrete gene-to-chromosome address ever established.
• WORKED PATTERN — The criss-cross signature: a trait carried on the X passes from father to daughter (she receives his only X) and from mother to son (he receives her X). When a cross shows a trait zig-zagging between the sexes like this, suspect sex linkage and track the X chromosomes one by one through the generations.
• YOUR TURN — In Morgan's white-eye cross the F2 ratio of red to white overall was about 3:1 — so why did the result still surprise everyone? Answer: because the white class was not spread across both sexes as a plain autosomal recessive would be; it appeared only among males, tying the gene to the X chromosome.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Morgan did not propose the chromosomal theory — Sutton and Boveri did; Morgan verified it experimentally. Also remember Alfred Sturtevant, Morgan's student, who used recombination frequencies from these fly crosses to produce the first genetic map — a favourite one-line question.

Linkage and Recombination — Genes That Travel Together
• DEFINITION — Linkage is the physical association of genes on the same chromosome — their tendency to be inherited together rather than assorting independently. Morgan coined the term recombination for the generation of non-parental gene combinations, produced when crossing over exchanges segments between homologous chromosomes in meiosis.
• EXAMPLE — Morgan's two classic test crosses in Drosophila give the numbers this chapter lives on. For body colour and eye colour genes (yellow body y and white eye w, both on the X), the parental combinations were 98.7 percent of offspring and recombinants only 1.3 percent — very tight linkage. For white eye w and miniature wing m, parentals were 62.8 percent and recombinants 37.2 percent — the same chromosome, but a much looser grip.
• WHY the percentages differ — crossing over is more likely to strike between two loci that lie far apart on the chromosome than between neighbours. Recombination frequency therefore measures distance: 1 percent recombination equals one map unit (one centimorgan). Sturtevant turned those percentages into the first chromosome maps — y and w sit about 1.3 units apart; w and m about 37.2.
• DEFINITION — A linkage group is the set of all genes carried on one chromosome; the number of linkage groups in an organism equals its haploid chromosome number (Drosophila has four linkage groups; humans have 23). Genes of one linkage group are not all equally linked — linkage strength falls as the distance between loci grows.
• WORKED PATTERN — Reading any two-gene test cross: if the four classes are equal (1:1:1:1), the genes assort independently — different chromosomes or far apart. If two classes tower over the other two, the genes are linked; the tall classes are the parental combinations, the short ones the recombinants, and recombinant percentage equals (recombinant offspring divided by total offspring) multiplied by 100.
• YOUR TURN — A test cross gives 940 parental-type and 60 recombinant-type offspring out of 1000. Find the recombination frequency and the linkage verdict. Answer: 6 percent recombinants — the genes are linked on the same chromosome, about 6 map units apart; independent assortment would have given roughly 50 percent.
✔ KEY POINT — Linkage weakens, never strengthens, assortment: parental combinations above 50 percent mean linkage; exactly 50 percent recombinants is indistinguishable from independent assortment, so tightly it is the ceiling — recombinant frequency can never exceed 50 percent.

Sex Determination — Four Systems Across Animals
• DEFINITION — Sex determination is the mechanism that establishes whether an individual develops as male or female. In many organisms it is genetic and chromosomal: one pair of chromosomes — the sex chromosomes (allosomes) — carries the deciding genes, while the remaining pairs are autosomes.
• EXAMPLE — The XX-XY system: in humans and Drosophila, females carry two X chromosomes (XX) and males carry one X and one Y (XY). The male is the heterogametic sex — he makes two types of sperm (X-bearing and Y-bearing) in equal numbers, so the sex of the child is set by which sperm fertilises the X-carrying egg; the chance is 50:50 each time.
• EXAMPLE — The ZZ-ZW system reverses the roles: in birds, females are heterogametic (ZW) and males are homogametic (ZZ), so here it is the egg that decides the sex. The XX-XO system, seen in insects like the grasshopper, gives females XX and males a single X with no partner (XO) — 24 chromosomes in the female, 23 in the male. Same XX or ZZ labels, opposite sexes — read the organism before applying a system.
• DEFINITION — Haplodiploidy runs the honeybee colony: females (queens and workers) develop from fertilised eggs and are diploid with 32 chromosomes; males (drones) develop from unfertilised eggs by parthenogenesis and are haploid with 16 chromosomes. The drone has no father and cannot have sons, but he does have a grandfather — his mother's father.
• WHY the bee system is more than a curiosity — a drone's sperm are produced without reducing an already haploid set, so every sperm is genetically identical; a queen stores sperm from her mating flight and controls fertilisation egg by egg, effectively choosing the sex of each offspring. Sex determination here is a developmental switch thrown by fertilisation itself.
• WORKED PATTERN — Ask one question first in any sex-determination problem: which sex is heterogametic? If the male, think XX-XY or XX-XO and sperm decides; if the female, think ZZ-ZW and the egg decides; if ploidy decides, think honeybee. Then, and only then, write gametes. Most errors in this topic come from applying the human system to a bird or a bee.
• YOUR TURN — A honeybee queen lays an egg that is not fertilised. What hatches, and how many chromosomes does it carry? Answer: a male drone, haploid with 16 chromosomes — development by parthenogenesis from an unfertilised egg is the male pathway in bees.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — In birds, ZW means female and ZZ means male — the exact reverse of the mammal pattern. And in humans the Y determines maleness (through its sex-determining region), while in Drosophila it is the ratio of X chromosomes to autosome sets that counts — do not export the human rule into the fly.

Mutation — When the Script Itself Changes
• DEFINITION — Mutation is a phenomenon that results in the alteration of DNA sequences and consequently changes the genotype and the phenotype of an organism. Along with recombination, mutation is the raw material of the variation that inheritance then passes on — it creates new alleles.
• DEFINITION — A point mutation alters a single base pair. The classic case is sickle-cell anaemia: one base change in the beta-globin gene swaps one amino acid (glutamic acid to valine) in the protein, and the whole physiology shifts. Small edit, enormous consequence — because proteins are read letter by letter.
• DEFINITION — Frameshift mutations arise from insertions or deletions of base pairs: because the genetic code is read in consecutive triplets, adding or removing bases shifts the entire reading frame downstream, scrambling every codon after the change. Contrast point mutations, which touch one codon at most, with frameshifts, which rewrite the rest of the sentence.
• WHY mutations link this chapter to the disorders — every Mendelian disorder in the next sections is a mutation that got inherited: a changed globin, a missing enzyme. Mutation explains where harmful alleles come from; segregation explains how they travel; pedigree analysis explains how we detect them in families.
• EXAMPLE — Physical and chemical agents called mutagens — ultraviolet radiation is the everyday example — raise the mutation rate above the spontaneous background. Mutation itself is random with respect to need: it does not aim at usefulness, which is why most new mutations are neutral or harmful and only selection sorts them afterwards.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify a mutation question by scale: one base substituted equals point mutation; bases inserted or deleted with a shifted reading frame equals frameshift; failure of chromosome separation equals aneuploidy, the chromosomal-disorder route of the final section. The scale of the change predicts the scale of the effect.
• YOUR TURN — A single base is deleted near the start of a gene. Why is this usually more damaging than a substitution at the same place? Answer: the deletion shifts the reading frame for every codon after it, changing many amino acids and usually creating an early stop; the substitution changes at most one amino acid.
✔ KEY POINT — Mutation plus recombination are the two sources of variation in this chapter: recombination reshuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation writes genuinely new alleles into the script.

Pedigree Analysis — Reading a Family Like a Cross
• DEFINITION — A pedigree is a record of the inheritance of a trait across generations of one family, drawn with standard symbols: a square for a male, a circle for a female, a filled symbol for an affected person, a dot inside a circle for a carrier female, a horizontal line joining partners, and a double line for a consanguineous (related-partner) mating. Pedigree analysis is how Mendel's rules are applied to humans, where controlled crosses are impossible.
• WHY families substitute for experiments — we cannot design human marriages for science, but nature runs the crosses anyway: relatives share alleles in predictable fractions, and a few generations of records contain the same information as several pea crosses. The analyst hunts for patterns — skipping generations, sex bias, affected children of unaffected parents — that only one inheritance mode can produce.
• WORKED PATTERN — The four-move reading method: (1) Affected child, two unaffected parents — the trait is recessive, and both parents are carriers. (2) Trait in every generation, affected persons often having unaffected children only when the other parent contributes normally — suspect dominant. (3) Far more affected males than females, passing through carrier mothers — suspect X-linked recessive. (4) Affected daughter with an unaffected father — X-linked recessive is impossible, because a daughter needs her father's X; look to the autosomes. Figure 23.5 drills these moves on a worked family.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT's own practice pair: myotonic dystrophy represents autosomal dominant inheritance in pedigree problems — it does not skip generations — while sickle-cell anaemia represents autosomal recessive — carriers look normal and the trait surfaces where two carriers meet, more often when marriages are consanguineous.
• YOUR TURN — In a pedigree, two carrier parents have four children; one son is affected with an autosomal recessive disorder. What is the chance their next child is affected, and does the affected son change it? Answer: still one in four — each conception is an independent segregation event; the family's history does not load the dice.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — An affected person in a recessive pedigree is homozygous recessive, but an unaffected sibling of an affected child is not automatically a carrier — conditioned on being unaffected, the chance is two in three (the AA : Aa ratio among the unaffected is 1:2). Questions love this conditional probability precisely because intuition says one in two.

Haemophilia and Colour Blindness — The X-Linked Pair
• DEFINITION — Haemophilia is a sex-linked recessive disorder in which a single defective gene on the X chromosome impairs the blood-clotting cascade, so the blood of an affected person does not clot properly and even a small cut can bleed dangerously. It is often called the bleeder's disease.
• EXAMPLE — The disorder ran through the royal families of Europe descended from Queen Victoria, who was a carrier: her daughters carried the allele into other royal houses, and sons who received it — like the Russian Tsarevich Alexei — were haemophilic. The pedigree is the classic demonstration that the allele travels silently through carrier females and strikes males.
• WORKED PATTERN — The haemophilia cross arithmetic: a carrier female (one normal X, one affected X) crossed with a normal male gives, among sons, half normal and half haemophilic; among daughters, all look normal but half are carriers. A haemophilic male passes his only X to all his daughters — every daughter of a haemophilic man is an obligate carrier — while his sons get his Y and are unaffected by his allele.
• DEFINITION — Colour blindness is a sex-linked recessive defect in which a person fails to distinguish red and green colours, caused by defects in the red- or green-cone pigment genes on the X chromosome. It occurs in about 8 percent of males but only about 0.4 percent of females.
• WHY the sex gap is a probability lesson — a male needs only one copy of the recessive allele (he has a single X) to show the defect: probability q. A female needs the allele on both of her X chromosomes: probability roughly q squared — a much smaller number. One equation explains the 8 percent versus 0.4 percent asymmetry, and the same logic governs haemophilia.
• YOUR TURN — A colour-blind man marries a woman with normal vision whose father was colour-blind. What fraction of their sons will be colour-blind? Answer: half — the woman is an obligate carrier (she received her father's affected X), so half her sons receive that X and are colour-blind.
✔ KEY POINT — X-linked recessive fingerprint: many more affected males than females; no father-to-son transmission (the father gives sons his Y); transmission through carrier females; the trait can skip generations. If a pedigree shows an affected son with a normal father, nothing is contradicted — the allele came from the mother.

Sickle-Cell Anaemia — One Base, One Amino Acid
• DEFINITION — Sickle-cell anaemia is an autosomal recessive disorder of red blood cells, controlled by a single pair of alleles HbA and HbS. Only the homozygote HbS HbS shows the severe disease; the heterozygote HbA HbS is a carrier with sickle-cell trait — apparently healthy, yet transmitting the allele.
• DEFINITION — The molecular cause is a single point mutation: in the beta-globin gene, the codon GAG becomes GUG, so the sixth amino acid of the beta chain changes from glutamic acid (Glu) to valine (Val). That one substitution makes the haemoglobin polymerise when oxygen is low, deforming the red cell from a biconcave disc into an elongated sickle that blocks small vessels and breaks down early — hence anaemia and pain crises.
• WHY carriers persist in the population — the heterozygote gains a survival edge where malaria is common, because the malaria parasite fares poorly in trait-carrying red cells. Selection therefore keeps HbS present in malaria regions even though the homozygote suffers severe disease — a pleiotropic gene whose "harmful" allele is maintained by a hidden benefit.
• WORKED PATTERN — The carrier cross: HbA HbS crossed with HbA HbS gives one quarter HbA HbA (normal, non-carrier), half HbA HbS (carriers) and one quarter HbS HbS (affected) — the standard autosomal recessive 1:2:1 in modern dress. Expect exam stems to describe unaffected parents with an affected child and ask you to name the disorder and the parents' genotypes; both are answered by this pattern.
• EXAMPLE — The same allele counted three ways in this chapter: it is a point mutation at the DNA level, a pleiotropic gene at the phenotype level (anaemia, sickling, organ effects, malaria resistance), and an autosomal recessive at the pedigree level. Examiners rotate the viewpoint — recognise the allele in all three costumes.
• YOUR TURN — A sickle-cell carrier marries a normal non-carrier. Can any child have sickle-cell anaemia? What fraction will be carriers? Answer: no child can be HbS HbS, because the normal parent always gives HbA; half the children will be carriers (HbA HbS).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Sickle-cell anaemia is autosomal, not sex-linked — it sits on an autosome, so males and females are affected equally. It is paired with thalassemia in questions precisely because students blur the two haemoglobin disorders; keep sickle-cell as a quality defect (wrong amino acid) and thalassemia as a quantity defect (too little chain), as the next section shows.

Phenylketonuria and Thalassemia — Two More Single-Gene Stories
• DEFINITION — Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism: the affected person lacks the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, which converts the amino acid phenylalanine into tyrosine. Phenylalanine therefore accumulates and is converted into phenylpyruvic acid and derivatives, whose build-up in the brain causes mental retardation; the excess is excreted through the urine, which gives the disease its name.
• WHY PKU is the hopeful disorder — its damage comes not from the missing gene product directly but from the toxic pile-up of what the enzyme should have processed — and a pile-up can be prevented by diet. Newborns are screened precisely so phenylalanine intake can be restricted before the brain is harmed: the genetics is fixed, the outcome is not.
• DEFINITION — Thalassemia is an autosomal recessive blood disorder caused by reduced synthesis of one of the globin chains of haemoglobin — a quantitative problem, not a wrong-chain problem. In alpha thalassemia, production of alpha chains falls because of defects in the closely linked HBA1 and HBA2 genes on chromosome 16; in beta thalassemia, beta-chain synthesis is affected by mutation in the single HBB gene on chromosome 11.
• WORKED PATTERN — The sickle-cell versus thalassemia contrast, a guaranteed comparison: cause — one amino acid wrong versus chains produced in reduced amount; molecule — abnormal haemoglobin versus too little normal haemoglobin; genes — HBB for sickle-cell and beta thalassemia (both chromosome 11 stories), HBA1 and HBA2 on chromosome 16 for alpha thalassemia. Note the elegant trap: beta thalassemia and sickle-cell involve the very same gene, differently broken.
• EXAMPLE — Both thalassemias worsen with gene dosage: losing one alpha copy may be silent while losing more copies brings deepening anaemia — the same more-copies-more-effect logic as incomplete dominance, now read at the level of whole genes.
• YOUR TURN — A child makes normal beta-globin amino-acid sequences but far too few beta chains, and is severely anaemic. Sickle-cell or thalassemia, and which type? Answer: beta thalassemia — the chain is correct but scarce (a quantity defect from HBB on chromosome 11), whereas sickle-cell makes a faulty chain in normal amounts.
✔ KEY POINT — The four Mendelian disorders to command cold: haemophilia and colour blindness (X-linked recessive), sickle-cell anaemia and phenylketonuria (autosomal recessive, single-gene), plus thalassemia (autosomal recessive, chain quantity — alpha on chromosome 16, beta on chromosome 11).

Chromosomal Disorders — Down's, Turner's and Klinefelter's
• DEFINITION — Chromosomal disorders arise from the absence, excess or abnormal arrangement of one or more chromosomes. The commonest cause is aneuploidy — the gain or loss of a chromosome through failure of segregation of chromatids during cell division, called non-disjunction; the gamete ends with an extra chromosome or one missing, and fertilisation fixes the error in every cell of the child.
• EXAMPLE — Down's syndrome, first described by Langdon Down in 1866, is caused by an additional copy of chromosome 21 — trisomy 21 — giving 47 chromosomes. The affected person is short-statured with a small round head, a furrowed tongue and partially open mouth; the palm has a characteristic crease; physical, psychomotor and mental development are retarded.
• EXAMPLE — Klinefelter's syndrome comes from an extra X chromosome in a male — karyotype 47, XXY. Such persons show overall masculine development but also feminine features such as development of breasts (gynaecomastia), and they are sterile. Turner's syndrome is the mirror loss: one X missing, karyotype 45, XO — such females are sterile, with rudimentary ovaries and underdeveloped secondary sexual characters.
• WHY dosage explains all three — a whole chromosome carries hundreds of genes, so one extra or missing copy unbalances hundreds of products at once; that is why chromosomal disorders affect many systems together (growth, face, fertility, intellect) while single-gene disorders strike narrower targets. The width of the symptom list is a clue to the width of the genetic error.
• WORKED PATTERN — Karyotype arithmetic for exam stems: count total chromosomes and check the sex pair. 47 with three 21s is Down's; 47, XXY is Klinefelter's (extra X, male); 45, XO is Turner's (missing X, female). If the total is 46 but symptoms are single-system, think Mendelian, not chromosomal. Figure 23.6 sets the full disorders table side by side.
• YOUR TURN — A sterile male with breast development has 47 chromosomes. Name the syndrome and the sex-chromosome set. Answer: Klinefelter's syndrome, XXY — the extra X feminises some features while the Y still directs male development.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Turner's females are 45, XO — not 45, YO: a zygote with no X at all cannot survive, since the X carries essential genes. And Down's syndrome is trisomy of chromosome 21, an autosome — it strikes males and females in line with births, not in the skewed pattern of X-linked disorders.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Mendel used seven pairs of contrasting traits in pea, built true-breeding lines by repeated self-pollination, and worked 1856 to 1863; inheritance is particulate — factors (alleles) do not blend.
✔ KEY POINT — Monohybrid F2: phenotypic ratio 3:1, genotypic ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt; the test cross (unknown dominant crossed with recessive) gives all dominant offspring from TT and a 1:1 split from Tt.
✔ KEY POINT — Law of Dominance: in a dissimilar pair of factors, one dominates and the other stays recessive and hidden. Law of Segregation: alleles separate unchanged at gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one allele and is pure for the trait.
✔ KEY POINT — Incomplete dominance: heterozygote intermediate (snapdragon and Mirabilis pink), F2 phenotypic ratio 1:2:1 equal to the genotypic ratio. Codominance: both alleles fully expressed side by side, as IA IB giving blood group AB.
✔ KEY POINT — ABO blood groups: gene I with three alleles IA, IB, i; six genotypes, four phenotypes; IA and IB dominant over i and codominant with each other. A group O child proves both parents carry i.
✔ KEY POINT — Multiple alleles: three or more forms of one gene in the population, two per individual. Pleiotropy: one gene with many phenotypic effects (sickle-cell; pea starch grains B and b). Polygenic inheritance: many genes, one quantitative trait (skin colour; wheat kernel colour), dominant alleles adding up.
✔ KEY POINT — Dihybrid F2 phenotypic ratio 9:3:3:1; Law of Independent Assortment: segregation of one character pair is independent of the other at gamete formation; dihybrid test cross gives 1:1:1:1.
✔ KEY POINT — Chromosomal theory (Sutton and Boveri, 1902): genes lie on chromosomes whose meiotic behaviour parallels Mendel's factors. Morgan verified it in Drosophila; white eye on the X was the first gene located on a chromosome; Sturtevant built the first genetic map.
✔ KEY POINT — Linkage: genes on one chromosome inherited together; recombination by crossing over creates non-parental combinations. Morgan's numbers: yellow-white 98.7 percent parental, 1.3 percent recombinant; white-miniature 62.8 percent parental, 37.2 percent recombinant. One percent recombination equals one map unit; linkage groups equal the haploid chromosome number.
✔ KEY POINT — Sex determination: XX-XY (humans, Drosophila — male heterogametic), ZZ-ZW (birds — female heterogametic), XX-XO (grasshopper — male has one X less), haplodiploidy (honeybee — females diploid 32 from fertilised eggs, males haploid 16 from unfertilised eggs by parthenogenesis).
✔ KEY POINT — Mutation alters DNA sequence: point mutation changes one base (sickle-cell, GAG to GUG, Glu to Val); frameshift insertions or deletions shift the reading frame; non-disjunction causes aneuploidy. Mutagens like UV raise the rate.
✔ KEY POINT — Pedigree rules: affected child of unaffected parents means recessive; affected daughter of unaffected father excludes X-linked recessive; X-linked recessive shows far more affected males and no father-to-son passage; NCERT's pair is myotonic dystrophy (autosomal dominant) and sickle-cell anaemia (autosomal recessive).
✔ KEY POINT — Mendelian disorders: haemophilia (X-linked recessive, clotting fails); colour blindness (X-linked recessive, about 8 percent males, 0.4 percent females); sickle-cell anaemia (autosomal recessive, HbS HbS affected, HbA HbS carrier); phenylketonuria (autosomal recessive, phenylalanine hydroxylase missing); thalassemia (autosomal recessive, alpha genes HBA1 and HBA2 on chromosome 16, beta gene HBB on chromosome 11).
✔ KEY POINT — Chromosomal disorders: Down's syndrome (trisomy 21, 47 chromosomes, described by Langdon Down in 1866); Klinefelter's (47, XXY, sterile males with gynaecomastia); Turner's (45, XO, sterile females with rudimentary ovaries).

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Saying the pink F1 of snapdragon proves blending inheritance and refutes Mendel. Why it is wrong: the F2 of that cross gives red, pink and white in 1:2:1 — the parental colours return pure, so the alleles never blended; only their expression in the heterozygote is intermediate. Correct principle: incomplete dominance modifies dominance, not segregation.
• Mistake 2: Calling codominance and incomplete dominance the same thing. Why it is wrong: in incomplete dominance the heterozygote is an intermediate single phenotype (pink); in codominance both parental products appear fully and separately (A and B sugars in AB blood). Correct principle: blend of one product versus coexistence of two products.
• Mistake 3: Writing that a person with three ABO alleles exists because the gene has three alleles. Why it is wrong: multiple alleles describe the population; a diploid individual holds only two alleles of the gene, one per homologous chromosome. Correct principle: menu size belongs to the population, portion size to the individual.
• Mistake 4: Assuming independent assortment holds for any two genes. Why it is wrong: genes on the same chromosome are linked and produce parental combinations in excess; Morgan's 98.7 : 1.3 split is the standing counterexample. Correct principle: independent assortment is exact for genes on different chromosomes and approximate only for distant loci on one chromosome.
• Mistake 5: Treating sickle-cell anaemia as sex-linked because it is "in the blood". Why it is wrong: its gene is on an autosome (the beta-globin gene HBB, chromosome 11), and males and females are affected in equal proportion. Correct principle: locate the gene first — the X-linked pair of this chapter is haemophilia and colour blindness only.
• Mistake 6: Swapping sickle-cell and thalassemia mechanisms. Why it is wrong: sickle-cell makes a faulty beta chain (Glu replaced by Val — a quality defect); thalassemia makes too little of a normal chain (a quantity defect). Correct principle: wrong chain versus less chain.
• Mistake 7: Saying a drone honeybee has a father, or that unfertilised eggs give females. Why it is wrong: drones develop from unfertilised eggs by parthenogenesis and are haploid (16); females develop from fertilised eggs and are diploid (32). Correct principle: in bees, fertilisation itself is the sex switch.
• Mistake 8: Assigning ZW to male birds by mammal habit. Why it is wrong: in birds the female is heterogametic (ZW) and the male homogametic (ZZ) — the egg, not the sperm, decides the sex. Correct principle: identify the heterogametic sex before writing any cross.
• Mistake 9: Reading a recessive pedigree as dominant because affected people appear in two generations. Why it is wrong: recessive traits can appear in successive generations when carriers marry in; the decisive sign is the unaffected-parents-with-affected-child event, which dominance cannot produce (two recessive-phenotype parents cannot hide a dominant allele). Correct principle: hunt for the impossible event for each mode, not for surface frequency.
• Mistake 10: Giving Turner's syndrome the karyotype 45, YO or Down's syndrome a sex-chromosome cause. Why it is wrong: YO lacks the essential X entirely and is not viable; Turner's is 45, XO, and Down's is trisomy of autosome 21. Correct principle: count the total and name the chromosome involved before naming the syndrome.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A tall pea plant of unknown genotype is crossed with a dwarf plant. All 60 offspring are tall. What is the tall parent's most likely genotype, and what cross name applies?
• Given: Dominant phenotype parent crossed with the recessive (tt) parent; zero recessive offspring in a large sample.
• Principle: This is a test cross; a heterozygous parent would give about half dwarf offspring, so their complete absence points to a homozygous parent.
• Steps: If the parent were Tt, each offspring would have a one-half chance of tt; 60 straight tall offspring from Tt is vanishingly unlikely. If the parent is TT, all offspring must be Tt and tall — exactly what is seen.
• Answer: The parent is almost certainly TT; the cross is a test cross.
• Reasoning: Test-cross logic is statistical: absence of the recessive class in a big sample is itself data.
• Mistake to avoid: Claiming certainty from a tiny sample — with only two offspring, a Tt parent could easily give two talls by chance.
• Example 2 — Problem: In snapdragons, a pink flower is crossed with a red flower. Predict the offspring phenotypes and their proportions, and state whether any white flowers can appear.
• Given: Pink is Rr; red is RR; red-white inheritance here is incomplete dominance.
• Principle: List gametes — pink gives R and r equally; red gives only R — and combine.
• Steps: R (from red) with R (from pink) gives RR, red. R with r gives Rr, pink. Each class arises in half the offspring. White needs rr, requiring r from both parents; red supplies none.
• Answer: Half red (RR) and half pink (Rr); no white offspring are possible.
• Reasoning: The missing class is diagnosed from gametes, not from the ratio table — one parent simply has no r to give.
• Mistake to avoid: Predicting a 1:2:1 ratio — that ratio belongs to the self-cross of two heterozygotes, not to a heterozygote crossed with a homozygote.
• Example 3 — Problem: A woman with blood group A and a man with blood group B have four children with groups A, B, AB and O respectively. Write the parents' genotypes and explain the O child.
• Given: All four phenotypes appear among the children, including O (i i).
• Principle: An O child receives i from each parent, so each parent must carry i alongside their expressed allele.
• Steps: Mother must be IA i (group A carrying i). Father must be IB i (group B carrying i). Gamete pairs then generate IA IB (AB), IA i (A), IB i (B) and i i (O), each with probability one quarter.
• Answer: Mother IA i, father IB i; the O child is the double-recessive combination that exposes both hidden i alleles.
• Reasoning: Work backwards from the most informative child — the recessive phenotype reveals both parents' hidden alleles at once.
• Mistake to avoid: Declaring the parents IA IA and IB IB because "pure groups give mixed children" — homozygotes could never produce an O child.
• Example 4 — Problem: Two carriers of sickle-cell trait marry. A doctor tells them each child independently has a 25 percent chance of the disease. Their first child is affected. What is the risk for the second child, and what is the chance a healthy child of theirs is a carrier?
• Given: Both parents are HbA HbS; the cross is the standard carrier-by-carrier cross.
• Principle: Segregation is independent at each conception; among unaffected offspring the genotype split is 1 HbA HbA : 2 HbA HbS.
• Steps: Second child's disease risk stays one in four (HbS HbS) regardless of the first child. A healthy child is either HbA HbA (one third of the healthy class) or HbA HbS (two thirds) — so the carrier chance, given health, is two in three.
• Answer: Risk for the second child: 25 percent. Carrier probability for a healthy child: two thirds.
• Reasoning: Conditional probability is the engine of genetic counselling — the condition "healthy" deletes the affected class and rescales the rest.
• Mistake to avoid: Answering one in two for the carrier question — that is the carrier fraction among ALL children, not among the healthy ones the question specifies.
• Example 5 — Problem: A colour-blind man has a daughter with normal vision. She marries a man with normal vision. What proportion of her sons and of her daughters are expected to be colour-blind?
• Given: The daughter received her father's only X, which carries the colour-blindness allele: she is an obligate carrier. Her husband is normal.
• Principle: A carrier mother passes the allele-bearing X to half her children; sons who receive it are colour-blind (single X), daughters who receive it are carriers (their father's normal X covers the trait).
• Steps: Sons: half get the allele X, so half are colour-blind. Daughters: half get the allele X but also get a normal X from father, so none are colour-blind, though half are carriers.
• Answer: Half the sons colour-blind; no daughters colour-blind, half of them carriers.
• Reasoning: Criss-cross inheritance computed one X at a time — the father's X to daughters, the mother's two Xs shared between all children.
• Mistake to avoid: Saying the grandchildren's risk comes from the grandfather "through the father" — the son-in-law contributes nothing here; the allele travelled grandfather to daughter to grandsons.
• Example 6 — Problem: In Drosophila, a test cross of a female heterozygous for yellow body (y) and white eye (w) gives 1950 parental-type and 50 recombinant-type offspring out of 2000. Find the recombination frequency, the distance between the genes, and compare with Morgan's actual result.
• Given: Both genes are on the X chromosome; parental classes greatly exceed recombinant classes.
• Principle: Recombination frequency equals recombinant offspring divided by total offspring, times 100; 1 percent equals 1 map unit.
• Steps: 50 divided by 2000 equals 0.025, times 100 equals 2.5 percent recombinants. Distance is therefore 2.5 map units. Morgan's actual y–w cross gave 1.3 percent (98.7 percent parental), meaning the genes lie even closer — about 1.3 map units.
• Answer: 2.5 percent recombination, 2.5 map units apart in this cross; Morgan measured 1.3 percent, about 1.3 map units — tighter linkage.
• Reasoning: The parental landslide (above 50 percent) is the linkage signal; the small recombinant tail is the measuring tape.
• Mistake to avoid: Reporting 97.5 percent as the "linkage frequency" and stopping — map distance is defined by the recombinant percentage, the minority class.
• Example 7 — Problem: A pedigree shows two unaffected parents with an affected daughter, and an affected son in the next family branch whose parents are also both unaffected. A classmate claims it must be X-linked recessive because "mostly hidden traits are X-linked". Give two reasons the trait is autosomal recessive instead.
• Given: Unaffected parents producing affected children of both sexes.
• Principle: Unaffected parents with affected children establish recessive inheritance; the affected daughter's status then tests the X hypothesis.
• Steps: Reason one — an X-linked recessive daughter must receive an affected X from her father, so her father would be affected; he is unaffected, so X-linkage is excluded. Reason two — affected children of both sexes appear from unaffected parents in the pattern expected for an autosomal recessive with carrier parents on both sides.
• Answer: Recessive is proven by the skipping pattern; autosomal is proven because an unaffected father has an affected daughter, impossible for X-linked recessive.
• Reasoning: One well-chosen individual — the affected daughter — eliminates an entire hypothesis; pedigree solving is elimination, not guess-counting.
• Mistake to avoid: Arguing from how many males versus females are affected in a small family — tiny samples mislead; structural impossibilities do not.

Practice Questions — Self-Test Set
• Q1. A monohybrid F2 shows 750 tall and 250 dwarf pea plants. State the phenotypic and genotypic ratios and the genotype of every dwarf plant.
• Q2. A violet-flowered plant test-crossed with a white-flowered plant gives violet and white offspring in equal numbers. What was the violet parent's genotype, and what would a homozygous violet parent have given instead?
• Q3. Two pink Mirabilis plants are crossed. Give the expected proportions of red, pink and white offspring, and name the phenomenon.
• Q4. A man of blood group AB marries a woman of blood group O. Which blood groups are possible in their children, and which are impossible? Justify with genotypes.
• Q5. In a dihybrid test cross (YyRr with yyrr), the offspring appear in a 1:1:1:1 ratio. What does this tell you about the two genes, and what ratio would strong linkage have suggested instead?
• Q6. A test cross produces 460 parental-type and 40 recombinant-type offspring. Calculate the recombination frequency and the map distance between the genes.
• Q7. A queen honeybee's unfertilised egg develops into a drone. State the drone's chromosome number and the name of this mode of development, and explain why the drone has a grandfather but no father.
• Q8. Two unaffected parents have a daughter with thalassemia. State the inheritance pattern, both parents' status, and the chance that their next child is affected.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. Phenotypic ratio 3:1 (750:250); genotypic ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. Every dwarf is tt — the recessive phenotype always exposes the homozygous recessive genotype. Misconception watch: do not assign Tt to any dwarf; a heterozygote shows the dominant phenotype by definition of dominance.
• A2. The parent was heterozygous (Vv) — the 1:1 split is the test cross reading out its two gamete types. A homozygous VV parent would have given all violet offspring. Misconception watch: the white offspring are vv, getting v from the tested parent; the white tester parent alone cannot explain them since it always gives v — it is the combination that diagnoses.
• A3. One quarter red (RR), half pink (Rr), one quarter white (rr) — incomplete dominance, where the phenotypic ratio equals the genotypic ratio 1:2:1. Misconception watch: pink parents do not "average out" to all pink; segregation restores both pure colours.
• A4. Children can be group A (IA i) or group B (IB i) only. The AB father gives IA or IB; the O mother gives only i. Group AB is impossible (needs both IA and IB, but mother supplies neither) and group O is impossible (needs i from the father, who has none). Misconception watch: children of an AB parent are never AB or O — one of the most-tested ABO facts.
• A5. The 1:1:1:1 tells you the heterozygote made four gamete types in equal numbers — the genes assort independently (different chromosomes, or far apart on one). Strong linkage would instead give two large parental classes and two small recombinant classes, nowhere near equality. Misconception watch: 1:1:1:1 is the test-cross signature; the self-cross signature of independence is 9:3:3:1 — do not swap them.
• A6. Recombination frequency equals 40 divided by 500, times 100, equals 8 percent; the genes are 8 map units apart on the same chromosome. Misconception watch: use the recombinant (minority) classes for the calculation — the parental 460 is the complement, not the distance.
• A7. The drone is haploid with 16 chromosomes, developing by parthenogenesis. He has no father because he came from an unfertilised egg — no sperm contributed. He has a grandfather because his mother, the queen, developed from a fertilised egg and so had a father herself. Misconception watch: drones are not "half-female" or sterile workers; they are fertile haploid males whose sperm are produced without a reduction division.
• A8. Autosomal recessive; both parents are unaffected carriers. Each next child independently has a one-in-four chance of being affected. Misconception watch: thalassemia is not X-linked — an affected daughter with unaffected parents fits carrier parents on an autosome, and the equal-sex risk confirms it.

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: Mendel's pea experiments established inheritance as particulate: paired alleles segregate unchanged into gametes, one allele dominates in the heterozygote, and different gene pairs assort independently — producing the signature ratios 3:1, 1:1 in test crosses, and 9:3:3:1 in dihybrid crosses.
• Summary: The exceptions refine rather than refute the rules: incomplete dominance and codominance change how heterozygotes look; multiple alleles widen the gene's menu; pleiotropy lets one gene touch many traits; polygenic inheritance lets many genes build one graded trait; and linkage — genes sharing a chromosome — bends independent assortment, with recombination frequency measuring the distance between loci.
• Summary: Sutton and Boveri placed genes on chromosomes, Morgan proved it in Drosophila, and sex-determination systems (XX-XY, ZZ-ZW, XX-XO, haplodiploidy) show the chromosome machinery deciding whole developmental fates. Mutation writes new alleles — a single base change can sickle a red cell — while pedigrees let us read all of this inside human families, from haemophilia to Down's syndrome.
