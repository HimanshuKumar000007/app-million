Introduction — Every Breath Is a Pressure Story
• You are breathing right now — about 12 to 16 times a minute — without spending a single thought on it. Yet every one of those breaths is a precisely engineered piece of physics: muscles change the volume of your chest, the pressure inside falls, and the atmosphere itself pushes air into your lungs. This chapter follows one breath all the way — in through the airways, across the alveoli, onto haemoglobin, out to a working muscle, and back again as carbon dioxide.
• DEFINITION — Breathing is the physical process of moving air in and out of the lungs: inspiration brings fresh air in, expiration expels the used air. It is a purely mechanical act of volumes and pressures — no chemistry happens in the act of breathing itself.
• DEFINITION — Respiration, in the full NCERT sense, is the whole exchange story: oxygen from the atmosphere is taken in and used by the cells, and the carbon dioxide the cells produce is released out. Cellular respiration — the oxidation of food inside cells that actually spends the oxygen — is its final, chemical step.
• WHY the oxygen must keep coming — a cell cannot stockpile oxygen, and it produces carbon dioxide continuously as it works. Stop the supply line for a few minutes and cells, brain cells first, begin to fail. Breathing is therefore not a task the body does occasionally; it is a conveyor belt that must never pause, awake or asleep.
• EXAMPLE — At rest, an average adult moves roughly 500 mL of air in each breath and breathes about 14 times a minute — close to 7000 mL, seven litres of air, every single minute, rising many times over during exercise. The numbers in this chapter — volumes, pressures, percentages — all describe that conveyor belt.
• WORKED PATTERN — Read this chapter as a journey with five checkpoints: (1) the airway — what path does air travel? (2) the pump — how do muscles move it? (3) the volumes — how much air is involved? (4) the exchange — why do gases cross at the alveoli and the tissues? (5) the transport and control — how do gases ride the blood, and who sets the rate? Every exam question in this chapter lives at one of these five checkpoints.
• YOUR TURN — A student says "the cells of my hand are breathing". What is loose about that sentence? Answer: the hand's cells respire — they use oxygen and release carbon dioxide. Breathing is the lung-level ventilation that supplies the blood; the cell-level oxidation is cellular respiration. Same story, two different stations.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The one sentence that organises the whole chapter: gases always move from higher partial pressure to lower partial pressure, and everything else — bellows, volumes, haemoglobin, brainstem control — exists to keep those pressure slopes pointing the right way.

Breathing Organs Across the Animal Kingdom
• DEFINITION — A respiratory surface, whatever the animal, must be thin, moist, richly supplied with blood (or directly exposed to the medium), and large in area — because gases cross only by diffusion, and diffusion is fast only over short distances.
• DEFINITION — Many simple animals need no special organ at all: sponges, coelenterates and flatworms exchange oxygen and carbon dioxide over their entire body surface by simple diffusion — the whole skin is the lung.
• DEFINITION — The earthworm breathes through its moist cuticle: gases dissolve in the wet skin and pass into the blood beneath. This is why an earthworm dies if its skin dries out — a dry cuticle cannot dissolve oxygen, however much air surrounds it.
• DEFINITION — Insects use a network of tubes called tracheae, opening to the outside through spiracles and branching deep into the body, delivering air close to the tissues directly. Aquatic arthropods and molluscs, and fishes, use gills — feathery outgrowths with a huge surface bathed in water.
• DEFINITION — Terrestrial vertebrates — reptiles, birds and mammals — breathe by lungs: internal, folded, air-filled organs that protect the moist exchange surface inside the body. Frogs are the double agents of this list: they use lungs, and can also exchange gases through their moist skin — cutaneous respiration.
• WHY gills cannot work on land — a gill's fine filaments collapse and stick together in air and its surface dries; the internal lung keeps the exchange surface moist, folded and ventilated inside a protected chamber. Land life required moving the watery surface indoors.
• EXAMPLE — Match organ to animal, the standard one-mark drill: sponge — body surface; earthworm — moist cuticle; cockroach — tracheae; prawn and fish — gills; frog — lungs plus moist skin; human — lungs. Examiners shuffle this list endlessly, and it is pure pattern memory.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any "which organ" question ask two things: where does the animal live (water points to gills or surface; land points to tracheae or lungs), and how big is it (tiny and thin means surface diffusion can suffice; large means a dedicated organ must exist).
• YOUR TURN — Why can a flatworm manage with no respiratory organ while a fish of similar length cannot? Answer: the flatworm is so thin that every cell sits within diffusion distance of the surface; the fish is thick and highly active, so diffusion across the skin cannot supply its inner cells — it needs gills and a circulation.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Insects do not breathe through lungs, and their tracheae open through spiracles — not through the mouth. And the earthworm has no special respiratory organ at all: "moist cuticle" is the complete answer, not a stepping stone to one.

The Human Respiratory System — The Airway Road Map
• DEFINITION — The human airway runs in one fixed order: external nostrils, nasal passage, nasal chamber, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli. Learn it as a road with named stops — sequence questions simply scramble this list.
• DEFINITION — The pharynx is the common passage for food and air — both the nasal chamber and the mouth open into it, which is why a gatekeeper is needed further down. The larynx, the voice box, is a cartilaginous box that helps produce sound and guards the airway's entrance, the glottis.
• DEFINITION — The epiglottis is a thin, leaf-shaped elastic cartilaginous flap that covers the glottis during swallowing, steering food into the oesophagus behind and keeping it out of the airway. The occasional swallow that "goes down the wrong way" is this flap caught a fraction late.
• DEFINITION — The trachea is a straight tube running down to the mid-thoracic cavity, where at about the level of the fifth thoracic vertebra it divides into a right and a left primary bronchus. Each bronchus divides repeatedly into secondary and tertiary bronchi, then into bronchioles and finally terminal bronchioles — a branching tree that ends in millions of tiny air sacs, the alveoli.
• DEFINITION — The trachea, the primary, secondary and tertiary bronchi and the initial bronchioles are supported by incomplete, C-shaped cartilaginous rings. The rings hold the airway open against pressure changes while the open side of the C leaves room for the oesophagus behind to expand when food passes.
• DEFINITION — The system divides into two functional parts. The conducting part — external nostrils up to the terminal bronchioles — transports the air, clears it of foreign particles, humidifies it and brings it to body temperature; no exchange happens here. The exchange part — the alveoli and their ducts — is the actual site of diffusion between air and blood.
• DEFINITION — An alveolus is a very thin-walled, irregular, bag-like structure wrapped in a dense network of blood capillaries. Across its wall — squamous epithelium, capillary endothelium and the basement substance between them — oxygen and carbon dioxide cross in opposite directions, thousands of times a minute.
• EXAMPLE — Think of the tree inverted: the trachea is the trunk, the bronchi the branches, the bronchioles the twigs, and the alveoli the leaves — except that the business of this tree happens not in the leaves' green tissue but across their walls into the blood vessels wrapped around them.
• WORKED PATTERN — Any pathway question is solved by reciting the road in order and placing the asked structure between its two neighbours: nasal chamber before pharynx; larynx between pharynx and trachea; bronchioles after bronchi and before alveoli. If an option inserts oesophagus anywhere in the air route, it is automatically wrong — the oesophagus carries food, never air.
• YOUR TURN — Which named part of the pathway is common to the digestive and respiratory routes, and what protects the airway where the routes cross? Answer: the pharynx is the common passage; the epiglottis covers the glottis during swallowing so food passes behind into the oesophagus.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The larynx is not the epiglottis: the larynx is the whole cartilaginous voice box, the epiglottis is one flap on top of it. And the C-rings belong to the conducting tubes down to the initial bronchioles — alveoli have no cartilage at all; a sac held open by rings could not be thin enough for diffusion.

The Thoracic Chamber — A Bellows of Bone and Muscle
• DEFINITION — The lungs sit in the thoracic chamber, a box bounded by the vertebral column at the back (dorsal side), the sternum in front (ventral side), the ribs along the sides (lateral) and a dome-shaped muscular sheet, the diaphragm, forming its floor.
• DEFINITION — The diaphragm is the principal muscle of breathing: a dome bulging upward into the thorax when relaxed. Between the ribs lie the intercostal muscles — the external intercostals, whose contraction lifts the ribs, and the internal intercostals used when expiration becomes forceful.
• DEFINITION — Each lung is covered by a double-layered membrane, the pleura, with pleural fluid between the layers. The fluid lets the lung surface slide against the chest wall almost without friction as the lung expands and recoils with every breath.
• WHY the chamber must be airtight — the lungs contain no muscle of their own and cannot inflate themselves. The thoracic chamber is anatomically airtight, so any change in its volume is faithfully reflected as a change in the volume of the lungs inside it: expand the box and the lungs are drawn open; shrink the box and they are squeezed. The muscles move the box; the box moves the lungs; the pressure change moves the air.
• EXAMPLE — A puncture wound that opens the chest wall to the outside air breaks the seal: air enters the pleural space, the lung on that side springs back by its own elasticity and collapses — the clinical "collapsed lung" is the airtight principle, demonstrated by its failure.
• WORKED PATTERN — The master causal chain of this chapter, usable in almost every mechanism question: muscle action → thoracic volume changes → intrapulmonary pressure changes inversely → air flows down the pressure slope. Volume up means pressure down; volume down means pressure up. No step in the chain can be skipped in a well-built answer.
• YOUR TURN — The diaphragm contracts. Predict, step by step, what happens to thoracic volume and to the pressure inside the lungs. Answer: the diaphragm flattens downward, thoracic volume increases, intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric pressure — and air will flow in, as the next section details.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Lungs are passengers, not drivers: they have no skeletal muscle, they are moved entirely by the diaphragm and the intercostal muscles acting through the airtight thoracic chamber.

Mechanism of Breathing I — Inspiration, the Active Half
• DEFINITION — Inspiration is the intake of air that occurs when the pressure inside the lungs (intrapulmonary pressure) falls below atmospheric pressure, so air rushes in down the slope. It is an active process — it costs muscle contraction and energy.
• DEFINITION — Inspiration begins when the diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downward. This increases the volume of the thoracic chamber along its vertical (antero-posterior) axis — like pulling the floor of the bellows down.
• DEFINITION — At the same time the external intercostal muscles contract, lifting the ribs upward and outward and carrying the sternum with them. This increases thoracic volume along the dorso-ventral axis — like swinging the bellows' handles apart.
• DEFINITION — With thoracic volume up on both counts, pulmonary volume rises, intrapulmonary pressure drops below the pressure of the outside air, and air flows in through the airway until pressures equalise. That inflow, roughly 500 mL at rest, is one inspiration.
• WHY air moves at all — air is a fluid and obeys slopes: it flows from higher pressure to lower pressure, never the reverse. The body never pulls air in; it lowers the pressure inside and lets the atmosphere, pressing at roughly 760 mm Hg, push the air in for it.
• EXAMPLE — A syringe works on the same law: pull the plunger back, the volume inside grows, pressure falls, and liquid is driven in by the atmosphere pressing on the reservoir — your diaphragm is the plunger, your thorax the barrel.
• WORKED PATTERN — The inspiration chain, to be reproduced exactly in answers: diaphragm contracts and flattens PLUS external intercostals contract and lift ribs → thoracic volume increases → intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric → air rushes in. Marking schemes award one point per arrow in this chain.
• YOUR TURN — A patient's diaphragm is paralysed but the intercostal muscles still work. Will inspiration still occur? Answer: yes, but weakened — rib lifting alone still raises thoracic volume and lowers pressure, so some air enters; the diaphragm normally contributes the larger share, so breathing becomes shallow and laboured.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — During inspiration the diaphragm CONTRACTS — students routinely write the reverse. Fix it with the picture: a contracted diaphragm is a flattened diaphragm, and a flat floor means a bigger room. Relaxed and domed belongs to expiration.

Mechanism of Breathing II — Expiration and the Rate
• DEFINITION — Expiration is the expulsion of air that occurs when intrapulmonary pressure rises above atmospheric pressure. At rest it is a passive process: no muscle contraction is needed, only relaxation and elastic recoil.
• DEFINITION — In quiet expiration the diaphragm relaxes and returns to its dome shape, rising into the thorax, and the external intercostal muscles relax so the ribs and sternum fall back to their resting positions under gravity and elasticity. Thoracic volume falls, the lungs are compressed, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out.
• DEFINITION — The lungs and chest wall are elastic: stretched during inspiration, they recoil during expiration like a released balloon, and this recoil does the work of quiet expiration for free. Forceful expiration — coughing, blowing, hard exercise — is different: it recruits the internal intercostal and abdominal muscles to squeeze the thorax down faster and further.
• DEFINITION — The breathing rate of a healthy resting adult averages 12 to 16 breaths per minute. Rate and depth are not fixed settings; they are continuously adjusted by the control system of a later section to match the body's carbon dioxide production.
• EXAMPLE — After a sprint, you breathe faster and deeper for some minutes: the muscles have flooded the blood with carbon dioxide, the control centres sense it, and the bellows work overtime until the debt is cleared — the rate you observe is regulation made visible.
• WORKED PATTERN — The head-to-head comparison examiners love: Inspiration — diaphragm contracts/flattens, external intercostals contract, ribs up and out, thoracic volume up, pressure down, air in, active. Expiration (quiet) — diaphragm relaxes/domes, external intercostals relax, ribs down and in, volume down, pressure up, air out, passive. Seven paired facts; learn them as pairs and either column can be rebuilt from the other.
• YOUR TURN — In quiet breathing, which phase consumes energy, and why is the other phase free? Answer: inspiration — it needs the diaphragm and external intercostals to contract. Quiet expiration is free because relaxation plus the elastic recoil of lungs and chest wall restores the resting volume without any contraction.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Inspiration is bought with muscle work; quiet expiration is paid for by stored elasticity. That asymmetry — active in, passive out — is a favourite one-mark distinction.

Respiratory Volumes — Four Volumes on the Spirometer
• DEFINITION — The volumes and capacities of the lungs are measured with an instrument called a spirometer, which records the air moved in and out as a trace of rising and falling bands. Four quantities are volumes — they do not overlap; five more, built by adding volumes, are the capacities of the next section.
• DEFINITION — Tidal Volume (TV) is the volume of air inspired or expired during a normal, quiet breath — approximately 500 mL in a healthy adult. It is the small, regular wave in the middle of the spirometer trace, the only volume used in resting breathing.
• DEFINITION — Inspiratory Reserve Volume (IRV) is the additional volume a person can inspire by a forcible inspiration beyond the tidal top — about 2500 to 3000 mL. It is the deep extra draught you take before blowing out a row of candles.
• DEFINITION — Expiratory Reserve Volume (ERV) is the additional volume a person can expire by a forcible expiration after a normal expiration ends — about 1000 to 1100 mL. It is the extra squeeze-out after a quiet breath has already left.
• DEFINITION — Residual Volume (RV) is the volume of air remaining in the lungs even after the most forcible expiration — about 1100 to 1200 mL. It can never be breathed out voluntarily, and therefore never appears on a simple spirometer trace.
• WHY the lungs refuse to empty — if the lungs emptied completely, the alveolar walls would stick together and the work of prising them open for the next breath would be enormous; the residual air also keeps gas exchange ticking over between breaths, so blood oxygen never crashes to zero in the pause between expiration and the next inspiration.
• EXAMPLE — Stack the four volumes as one imaginary staircase: during a maximal breath cycle you could meet, in order, the residual 1100 to 1200 mL that never leaves, the expiratory reserve 1000 to 1100, the tidal 500, and the inspiratory reserve 2500 to 3000 on top — a total lung content of roughly five to six litres, quantified properly as capacities next.
• WORKED PATTERN — Identify a volume from its definition wording: "during a normal breath" means tidal; "additional, by forcible inspiration" means IRV; "additional, by forcible expiration" means ERV; "remaining after forcible expiration" means RV. The trigger phrases are this reliable — questions rarely disguise them any deeper.
• YOUR TURN — A student forcefully exhales everything possible and then, with maximal extra effort, pulls in as much air as she can. Which two named volumes sit at the bottom of that manoeuvre, untouched? Answer: none is untouched by definition except this — the residual volume remains in the lungs throughout; the manoeuvre moves ERV (already expelled), TV and IRV, but the 1100 to 1200 mL of RV never leaves.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Volumes and capacities are different categories: a capacity is always the sum of two or more volumes. An option that defines a capacity as a single band on the trace, or a volume as a sum, is wrong on category grounds before any number is checked.

Respiratory Capacities — Volumes Added into Answers
• DEFINITION — Inspiratory Capacity (IC) is the total volume of air a person can inspire after a normal expiration: IC = TV + IRV = 500 + 2500 to 3000, giving about 3000 to 3500 mL. It answers the question "from rest, how much can I pull in at most?"
• DEFINITION — Expiratory Capacity (EC) is the total volume of air a person can expire after a normal inspiration: EC = TV + ERV = 500 + 1000 to 1100, giving about 1500 to 1600 mL.
• DEFINITION — Functional Residual Capacity (FRC) is the volume of air that remains in the lungs after a normal expiration — the resting level the lungs return to between breaths: FRC = ERV + RV = 1000 to 1100 + 1100 to 1200, giving about 2100 to 2300 mL.
• DEFINITION — Vital Capacity (VC) is the maximum volume of air a person can breathe in after a forced expiration — equivalently, the most that can be expelled after the fullest possible inspiration: VC = IRV + TV + ERV, about 3500 to 4500 mL. It is the largest movable quantity of air and the standard measure of lung strength.
• DEFINITION — Total Lung Capacity (TLC) is the total volume of air in the lungs after a maximal inspiration — every volume added: TLC = VC + RV = IRV + TV + ERV + RV, about 5000 to 6000 mL.
• WHY athletes and singers quote vital capacity — VC measures the usable bellows: how much air can actually be moved per breath when needed. It grows with training and body size and shrinks in many lung diseases, which makes it the clinician's favourite single number — while RV, being unmovable, is excluded from it by definition.
• WORKED PATTERN — The formula bank, one line each: IC = TV + IRV. EC = TV + ERV. FRC = ERV + RV. VC = IRV + TV + ERV. TLC = VC + RV. Notice the map: only FRC and TLC contain RV; VC contains everything movable; IC and EC are the two half-measures built on the tidal volume. If a formula in an option breaks this map, it is wrong regardless of arithmetic.
• EXAMPLE — Take one consistent set inside all the NCERT ranges: TV 500, IRV 2800, ERV 1000, RV 1200 mL. Then IC = 500 + 2800 = 3300 (range 3000 to 3500 — inside). EC = 500 + 1000 = 1500 (range 1500 to 1600 — inside). FRC = 1000 + 1200 = 2200 (range 2100 to 2300 — inside). VC = 2800 + 500 + 1000 = 4300 (range 3500 to 4500 — inside). TLC = 4300 + 1200 = 5500 (range 5000 to 6000 — inside). One honest set of volumes reproduces every NCERT capacity range.
• YOUR TURN — A subject has TV 500 mL, ERV 1000 mL and RV 1200 mL. What is his functional residual capacity, and what does it physically represent? Answer: FRC = ERV + RV = 2200 mL — the air still inside his lungs at the end of a normal, unforced expiration.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Vital capacity does NOT include residual volume — the most planted error in this chapter. VC stops at what can be moved; the RV joins the sum only in total lung capacity. Second trap: FRC is measured after a NORMAL expiration, not a forced one — after a forced expiration only the RV remains.

Exchange of Gases — Diffusion Down Pressure Slopes
• DEFINITION — Exchange of gases occurs at two sites: at the alveoli, between air and the blood of the pulmonary capillaries, and again between the blood and the tissues throughout the body. Both exchanges run entirely by simple diffusion — no carrier, no energy, only pressure slopes.
• DEFINITION — The partial pressure of a gas is the pressure that one gas contributes to the total pressure of the mixture it sits in: pO2 for oxygen, pCO2 for carbon dioxide. A gas always diffuses from where its partial pressure is higher to where its partial pressure is lower — partial pressure, not total pressure and not percentage, is the deciding quantity.
• DEFINITION — At the alveolus, blood and air are separated by the respiratory membrane of three layers: the thin squamous epithelium of the alveolus, the endothelium of the alveolar capillary, and the basement substance lying between them. Together they are much less than a millimetre thick — a wall built to be crossed.
• DEFINITION — Solubility tilts the race: the solubility of carbon dioxide is 20 to 25 times higher than that of oxygen, so the amount of CO2 that can diffuse through the membrane per unit of partial-pressure difference is far higher than for oxygen. A tiny pCO2 slope moves as much CO2 as a large pO2 slope moves oxygen.
• WHY diffusion alone is enough here — the distances are microscopic and the surface is enormous: hundreds of millions of alveoli unfold into a sheet of capillaries, so an oxygen molecule leaving the air is never more than a fraction of a millimetre from a red blood cell. Nature solved a transport problem with geometry, not machinery.
• EXAMPLE — Smoke spreading through a still room is diffusion at walking pace over metres — minutes to hours. Across the respiratory membrane, distances are thousands of times smaller, and diffusion times shrink with the square of the distance — which is why a process that looks hopelessly slow in a room is more than fast enough across a membrane.
• WORKED PATTERN — To predict any gas movement, ask two questions only: (1) what is the gas's partial pressure on each side? (2) which side is higher? The gas flows from the higher side to the lower, full stop. Every exchange question in this chapter — alveolus or tissue, oxygen or carbon dioxide — is this one pattern with different numbers plugged in.
• YOUR TURN — Blood entering a lung capillary has pO2 of 40 mm Hg; the alveolar air beside it has pO2 of 104 mm Hg. Which way does oxygen move, and what drives it? Answer: from alveolus into blood, driven by the 64 mm Hg partial-pressure difference — diffusion down the slope.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Exchange does not need equal percentages or equal total pressures — only the partial pressure of the individual gas matters. Air is about 21 percent oxygen everywhere in the airway story, yet oxygen still moves, because the blood arriving at the alveolus has a much lower pO2.

The Partial-Pressure Table — One Table, Every Gradient
• DEFINITION — The whole exchange story fits into one NCERT table of partial pressures, in mm Hg. Atmospheric air: pO2 159, pCO2 0.3. Alveoli: pO2 104, pCO2 40. Deoxygenated blood arriving at the lungs: pO2 40, pCO2 45. Oxygenated blood leaving the lungs: pO2 95, pCO2 40. Tissues: pO2 40, pCO2 45.
• DEFINITION — At the alveolus, oxygen faces a slope from 104 in the air down to 40 in the arriving blood — a 64 mm Hg gradient driving it into the blood. The blood picks up oxygen until it leaves, oxygenated, at pO2 95 — close to, though just below, the alveolar value it was chasing.
• DEFINITION — Carbon dioxide at the alveolus faces the reverse slope: 45 in the arriving blood against 40 in the alveolar air — a gradient of only 5 mm Hg. It is enough, because of the solubility advantage of the previous section: CO2 crosses so readily that a 5 mm slope empties it as effectively as the 64 mm slope loads oxygen.
• DEFINITION — At the tissues the whole pattern flips. Oxygenated blood arrives with pO2 95 while the tissue fluid sits near pO2 40, so oxygen leaves the blood and enters the tissues. The tissues, busy producing CO2, sit at pCO2 45 against the blood's 40, so carbon dioxide enters the blood. The blood that leaves the tissues has fallen to pO2 40 and risen to pCO2 45 — exactly the deoxygenated values the table lists at the lungs' door, and the circle closes.
• WHY alveolar pO2 is 104 and not the atmospheric 159 — the air in the alveoli is not fresh atmosphere: it is a mixture of incoming air with the air left behind from the previous breaths, and it is saturated with water vapour. Quoting 159 for the alveolus is a classic way to lose a mark — 159 belongs to the outside air only.
• WORKED PATTERN — Use the table as a subtraction machine. For any exchange, find the two compartments in the story, read their two numbers for that gas, subtract, and the direction is from the larger to the smaller. The four oxygen numbers form one chain to memorise cold: 159 atmosphere, 104 alveoli, 95 oxygenated blood, 40 tissues and deoxygenated blood. Learn the chain as a descending staircase and every gradient question becomes subtraction.
• EXAMPLE — Sanity-check the chain: no gradient anywhere runs uphill. Oxygen descends 159 to 104 to 95 to 40 as it travels air to blood to tissue — if any value you recall breaks the descending order, you have recalled it wrong.
• YOUR TURN — At the tissues, how large is the pCO2 gradient and in which direction does CO2 move? Answer: tissues 45 minus blood 40 gives 5 mm Hg, driving CO2 from the tissues into the blood — the same small-but-sufficient slope seen at the alveolus, working in reverse.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The numbers 104 and 95 are a pair the examiners separate deliberately: 104 is the pO2 of alveolar AIR; 95 is the pO2 of the oxygenated BLOOD leaving the lungs. Air and blood never quite equalise — keep the two numbers on their own shelves.

Transport of Oxygen — Haemoglobin Does the Lifting
• DEFINITION — Oxygen is carried in two ways: about 97 percent is transported by the red blood cells, bound to haemoglobin as oxyhaemoglobin, and only about 3 percent travels dissolved in the plasma. Without haemoglobin, the dissolved fraction alone could supply only a tiny share of the body's needs.
• DEFINITION — Haemoglobin is the red, iron-containing pigment packed inside every RBC. Each haemoglobin molecule contains four haem groups, each with an iron that can bind one oxygen molecule — so a single haemoglobin can carry a maximum of four oxygen molecules.
• DEFINITION — The binding is reversible and the direction is set by conditions: in the oxygen-rich alveoli, haemoglobin loads up, forming oxyhaemoglobin; in the oxygen-poor tissues, it lets go. Loading and unloading are the same reaction read in two directions, steered by the local pO2.
• DEFINITION — Formation of oxyhaemoglobin is favoured, in the alveolar region, by a specific team of conditions: high pO2, low pCO2, low concentration of hydrogen ions (higher pH), and lower temperature. In the tissues, every one of these conditions is reversed — and each reversal favours dissociation, the release of oxygen. The tissue both needs the oxygen and creates exactly the chemistry that releases it.
• WHY reversibility is the whole trick — a carrier that bound oxygen permanently would be a trap, not a transport system: it would fill up in the lungs and deliver nothing. A carrier that bound nothing would deliver nothing either. Haemoglobin's genius is conditional loyalty — loyal in the lungs, disloyal in the tissues, with the local chemistry flipping the switch.
• EXAMPLE — A muscle working hard heats up, produces CO2 and lactic acid, and burns through its oxygen — all four unloading signals rise together at the exact place where oxygen demand is highest. Delivery is automatically addressed to the neediest tissue, with no central dispatcher involved.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any "favourable conditions" question, build the two columns: Alveoli (loading): high pO2, low pCO2, low H+, low temperature. Tissues (unloading): low pO2, high pCO2, high H+, high temperature. Questions hand you one cell of the table and ask for the verdict — locate the cell, read the column.
• YOUR TURN — Blood reaches a tissue where pO2 is low and pCO2 is high. Does haemoglobin hold on tighter or release? Answer: it releases — low pO2 weakens the loading signal while high pCO2 (and the H+ that comes with it) actively promotes dissociation, so oxyhaemoglobin gives up its oxygen precisely there.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The maximum load is four oxygen molecules per haemoglobin — per molecule of pigment, not per red blood cell (one RBC carries millions of haemoglobin molecules). And binding is oxygenation, a reversible attachment to the iron — the iron is not permanently oxidised; if it were, no release could occur.

The Oxyhaemoglobin Curve — Reading the Sigmoid
• DEFINITION — The oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve is the graph obtained by plotting the percentage saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen against the pO2. It is sigmoid — S-shaped — and it is the one graph this chapter expects you to read fluently.
• DEFINITION — The curve has two working regions. The flat upper part is the loading zone: at the high pO2 of the lungs, saturation is nearly complete, and it stays high even if the alveolar pO2 falls somewhat — loading is protected. The steep middle part is the unloading zone: across the pO2 range found in the tissues, a small fall in pO2 releases a large fraction of the carried oxygen — delivery is generous exactly where it must be.
• DEFINITION — A rightward shift of the curve means haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen has fallen: at any given pO2, less is held and more is released. The conditions that shift the curve right are high pCO2, low pH (more hydrogen ions) and high temperature — the tissue signature from the previous section.
• DEFINITION — The effect of carbon dioxide on this unloading has a name: the Bohr effect. As CO2 enters the blood in the tissues, it raises the hydrogen ion concentration, which lowers haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen and pushes oxygen off the pigment — the waste product of work personally evicts the fuel supply from its carrier.
• WHY the S-shape beats a straight line — a linear relationship would lose loading at altitude and lose unloading at rest. The sigmoid loads almost fully across a wide range of high pressures, then dumps cargo steeply across the narrow pressure range of the tissues. It is the shape of a good delivery contract: generous terms where demand lives, stingy terms where supply lives.
• EXAMPLE — Exercising muscle is the Bohr effect in action: it is hot, acidic and CO2-rich, so the curve shifts right locally and the blood releases more oxygen there than it would at the same pO2 elsewhere. At the lungs the conditions reverse — CO2 leaves, the blood cools relatively, and affinity rises for loading. The same blood obeys two different curves in two different places, written by local chemistry.
• EXAMPLE — Fetal haemoglobin plays the affinity game one level deeper: it has a higher affinity for oxygen than the mother's adult haemoglobin, so at the placenta oxygen moves from maternal blood to fetal blood across the shared pressure — the fetal curve sits to the left of the adult one, and the difference is what feeds the fetus.
• WORKED PATTERN — Every curve question reduces to three moves: (1) name the shift — right means lower affinity, more release; left means higher affinity, more loading. (2) name the cause from the factor list: pCO2 up, pH down, temperature up all push right. (3) state the consequence at the tissue or the lung. Never answer with the shift alone; the mark is in step 3.
• YOUR TURN — A patient has a high fever. At his tissues, will his haemoglobin release oxygen more or less readily than usual, and why? Answer: more readily — raised temperature is a right-shift factor, lowering affinity, so at any tissue pO2 a larger share of the oxygen is unloaded.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Right shift does not mean "more oxygen loaded" — it means the opposite: lower affinity, easier release, less held at a given pO2. Students who memorise "right is more" fail every Bohr question. Anchor it physically: right shift happens in hot, acidic, CO2-rich tissue — and tissue is where oxygen must be RELEASED.

Transport of Carbon Dioxide — Three Tickets Home
• DEFINITION — Carbon dioxide rides the blood in three forms: about 70 percent as bicarbonate ions, carried in the plasma; about 20 to 25 percent bound to haemoglobin as carbamino-haemoglobin; and about 7 percent dissolved in the plasma in the physical state. The bicarbonate route is the headline; the other two are supporting lanes.
• DEFINITION — At the tissues, where pCO2 is high, CO2 diffuses into the blood and enters the RBCs. There it combines with water to form carbonic acid — a reaction made fast by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, which RBCs contain in high concentration. The carbonic acid dissociates at once into hydrogen ions and bicarbonate ions.
• DEFINITION — The bicarbonate ions diffuse out of the RBC into the plasma, which is where they are actually transported. To keep the inside of the cell electrically neutral as negative bicarbonate leaves, chloride ions move from the plasma into the RBC — this exchange is the chloride shift, also called the Hamburger phenomenon. The hydrogen ions left behind are largely buffered by haemoglobin inside the RBC.
• DEFINITION — At the alveoli, where pCO2 is low, the entire machinery runs backwards: bicarbonate re-enters the RBC (chloride moving out in exchange), recombines with hydrogen ions to form carbonic acid, which splits into water and CO2 — and the CO2 diffuses out into the alveolar air to be expired. One system, wound up at the tissues and unwound at the lungs.
• DEFINITION — The carbamino lane is a separate binding: CO2 attaches to the amino groups of the globin part of haemoglobin, forming carbamino-haemoglobin. Note the address — the protein part, not the iron. The iron site belongs to oxygen; carbon dioxide never competes for it in this lane.
• WHY most CO2 refuses to travel as itself — carbon dioxide as a dissolved gas is a small-capacity, acid-forming cargo. Converting it into bicarbonate converts it into a harmless, highly soluble ion that the plasma can carry in bulk — the blood transports the exhaust mostly in disguise, and the disguise is assembled and removed by the RBC's enzyme.
• EXAMPLE — Follow one CO2 molecule from a working thigh muscle: it diffuses into a capillary, slips into an RBC, meets carbonic anhydrase, becomes bicarbonate, rides the plasma to the lungs, re-enters an RBC at the alveolus, becomes CO2 again, and leaves in the next expiration — possibly within the same minute it was produced.
• WORKED PATTERN — The direction rule that settles every CO2-transport question: where pCO2 is high (tissues), the reactions run toward bicarbonate formation; where pCO2 is low (alveoli), they run toward CO2 release. If an option describes bicarbonate forming in the lungs or breaking down in the tissues, it has the geography backwards.
• YOUR TURN — A drug blocks carbonic anhydrase. Which lane of CO2 transport is hit hardest, and what accumulates? Answer: the bicarbonate lane — the 70 percent route — because forming carbonic acid quickly inside the RBC depends on that enzyme; CO2 removal slows and CO2 (and acidity) builds up in the tissues.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Three addresses to keep separate: bicarbonate travels in the PLASMA but is MANUFACTURED inside the RBC; carbamino CO2 binds the GLOBIN, not the haem iron; and the percentages rank bicarbonate (70) far above carbamino (20 to 25) above dissolved (7). Options that swap any of these are testing filing, not understanding.

Regulation of Breathing — The Brainstem Autopilot
• DEFINITION — Breathing rhythm is generated and regulated by specialised centres in the brainstem. A respiratory rhythm centre, located in the medulla region of the brain, is primarily responsible for setting the basic rhythm of inspiration and expiration.
• DEFINITION — A second centre, the pneumotaxic centre, located in the pons region of the brain, moderates the functions of the rhythm centre. Its signal limits the duration of inspiration — it switches off the inspiratory drive — and by doing so it can alter the respiratory rate: shorter inspirations mean a faster, shallower pattern.
• DEFINITION — Beside the rhythm centre lies a chemosensitive area that is highly sensitive to carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions. When CO2 and H+ levels rise, this area is activated and signals the rhythm centre to make the necessary adjustments — ventilation increases, and the extra CO2 is eliminated.
• DEFINITION — The medulla is not the only listening post: receptors associated with the aortic arch and the carotid arteries can also recognise changes in CO2 and hydrogen ion concentration in the blood and send signals to the rhythm centre for remedial action.
• DEFINITION — Against this CO2-centred system, the role of oxygen in the regulation of the respiratory rhythm is quite insignificant — a direct NCERT statement and a deliberate trap for students who assume the gas we need must be the gas being monitored.
• WHY CO2, and not oxygen, runs the system — carbon dioxide is the variable the body produces moment to moment, and its level is chained to blood acidity through the bicarbonate chemistry of the previous section: rising CO2 means rising H+, and acidity is what cells cannot tolerate drifting. Oxygen stores in blood and tissues buffer its level; acidity has no such buffer of time. The body regulates the variable that can hurt it fastest.
• EXAMPLE — Hold your breath and feel the mounting urge to breathe: that urge is not your oxygen running out — it is CO2 and H+ climbing past the chemosensitive area's trigger. The reverse experiment proves it too: after deliberate over-breathing, which washes CO2 out, the urge to breathe vanishes for a while even though your oxygen stores were barely changed.
• WORKED PATTERN — Draw regulation as one negative-feedback loop: CO2 rises → chemosensitive area and aortic/carotid receptors detect it → rhythm centre (medulla) increases rate and depth → more CO2 is expired → CO2 falls → drive subsides. Any regulation question is answered by pointing at the step it asks about — sensor, centre, or effect.
• YOUR TURN — A climber at high altitude breathes faster and deeper. In terms of this chapter's control logic, what problem is his ventilation solving, and which molecule's level is the control system watching? Answer: his blood oxygen falls in the thin air and his breathing rises to defend gas exchange; the moment-to-moment controlled variable the brainstem watches remains CO2 (with H+) — ventilation is being driven hard, and the CO2 it blows off keeps falling, which is why altitude breathing feels unstable.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The centre-location swap is set every single year: rhythm centre is in the MEDULLA, pneumotaxic centre is in the PONS. And the pneumotaxic centre does not create the rhythm — it moderates it, chiefly by limiting inspiration. "Pneumotaxic centre generates the basic rhythm" fails on both halves.

Disorders — When the Bellows and Bags Fail
• DEFINITION — Asthma is a difficulty in breathing caused by inflammation of the bronchi and bronchioles, which narrows the airways. Its signature sound is wheezing — air forcing its way through narrowed tubes — and attacks are often triggered by allergens. The problem is in the conducting pipes, not the exchange surface.
• DEFINITION — Emphysema is a chronic disorder in which the alveolar walls are damaged, so the respiratory surface area shrinks — neighbouring alveoli break down into larger, fewer sacs with far less total wall for exchange. One major cause is cigarette smoking. The problem is in the exchange surface, not the pipes.
• DEFINITION — Occupational respiratory disorders strike workers exposed for years to large amounts of dust — in industries such as stone breaking and grinding, and mining. The body's defence mechanisms cannot clear the load; the dust provokes inflammation leading to fibrosis — a proliferation of fibrous tissue in the lungs — and serious loss of lung function. Silicosis (from silica dust) and asbestosis (from asbestos dust) are the named forms, and workers in such industries are advised to wear protective masks.
• WHY these three are grouped for the exam — each disorder breaks a different checkpoint of the chapter's journey: asthma narrows the airway, emphysema destroys the exchange area, occupational fibrosis stiffens and scars the lung tissue itself. A question describing a broken checkpoint is really asking you to name which station failed.
• EXAMPLE — Three patients, three stations: a teenager who wheezes after exposure to pollen — asthma (bronchi and bronchioles inflamed). A lifelong smoker whose breathlessness grows as his alveolar walls give way — emphysema (surface lost). A stone-grinder of twenty years with progressive fibrosis — occupational disorder (dust-driven scarring). Diagnosis in this chapter is location-matching, nothing more exotic.
• WORKED PATTERN — The disorder key, one line each: wheezing plus bronchi/bronchiole inflammation equals asthma. Damaged alveolar walls plus reduced surface plus smoking history equals emphysema. Long dust exposure plus fibrosis equals occupational respiratory disorder. Learn the pairs as fused units and the options sort themselves.
• YOUR TURN — A disorder reduces the respiratory surface by damaging alveolar walls, in a patient who has smoked for decades. Name it, and name the structural quantity that has fallen. Answer: emphysema; the total surface area available for gas exchange has decreased.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Asthma and emphysema are swapped relentlessly: asthma lives in the bronchi and bronchioles (narrowing), emphysema lives in the alveolar walls (area loss). If an option gives asthma "damaged alveolar walls", it has stolen emphysema's fact — reject it.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Airway order: nostrils, nasal passage, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli. The pharynx is the common food-and-air passage; the epiglottis guards the glottis during swallowing.
✔ KEY POINT — Trachea, bronchi down to the initial bronchioles are held open by incomplete C-shaped cartilaginous rings. Conducting part: nostrils to terminal bronchioles — transport, filtering, humidifying, no exchange. Exchange part: alveoli and their ducts.
✔ KEY POINT — Lungs have no muscle of their own. The airtight thoracic chamber — vertebrae behind, sternum in front, ribs at the sides, diaphragm as floor — moves them; pleural fluid lets the pleura slide without friction.
✔ KEY POINT — Inspiration is active: diaphragm contracts and flattens, external intercostals lift ribs up and out, thoracic volume rises, intrapulmonary pressure falls below atmospheric, air flows in. Quiet expiration is passive: relaxation, recoil, volume falls, pressure rises, air flows out.
✔ KEY POINT — Breathing rate of a healthy resting adult: 12 to 16 per minute. Tidal volume used in quiet breathing: about 500 mL.
✔ KEY POINT — Volumes: TV about 500 mL; IRV 2500 to 3000 mL; ERV 1000 to 1100 mL; RV 1100 to 1200 mL — and RV can never be expired or read on a simple spirometer.
✔ KEY POINT — Capacities: IC = TV + IRV (3000 to 3500); EC = TV + ERV (1500 to 1600); FRC = ERV + RV (2100 to 2300); VC = IRV + TV + ERV (3500 to 4500); TLC = VC + RV (5000 to 6000). Only FRC and TLC contain RV.
✔ KEY POINT — Partial pressures in mm Hg: atmosphere pO2 159, pCO2 0.3; alveoli 104 and 40; deoxygenated blood 40 and 45; oxygenated blood 95 and 40; tissues 40 and 45. Gases diffuse from higher to lower partial pressure — always.
✔ KEY POINT — The respiratory membrane has three layers: alveolar squamous epithelium, capillary endothelium, basement substance — total thickness much less than a millimetre. CO2 solubility is 20 to 25 times that of O2.
✔ KEY POINT — Oxygen transport: about 97 percent as oxyhaemoglobin in RBCs, about 3 percent dissolved in plasma. One haemoglobin binds at most four O2 molecules — one per haem group.
✔ KEY POINT — Loading is favoured by high pO2, low pCO2, low H+ and low temperature (the alveolar team); unloading by the exact reverse (the tissue team). The dissociation curve is sigmoid; right shift means lower affinity and easier release — the Bohr effect.
✔ KEY POINT — Carbon dioxide transport: about 70 percent as bicarbonate ions in plasma, 20 to 25 percent as carbamino-haemoglobin (bound to globin), about 7 percent dissolved. Carbonic anhydrase inside RBCs runs the bicarbonate chemistry; the chloride shift (Hamburger phenomenon) balances it.
✔ KEY POINT — Regulation: rhythm centre in the medulla sets the beat; pneumotaxic centre in the pons limits inspiration and moderates the rhythm centre; the chemosensitive area plus aortic-arch and carotid receptors watch CO2 and H+ — the role of oxygen in regulation is quite insignificant.
✔ KEY POINT — Disorders by station: asthma — inflamed bronchi and bronchioles, wheezing; emphysema — alveolar walls damaged, surface area lost, smoking the major cause; occupational disorders — dust-driven fibrosis such as silicosis and asbestosis.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Including residual volume in vital capacity. Why it is wrong: VC is built only from movable air — IRV + TV + ERV. The residual volume by definition cannot be moved, so it can join only totals that are not about movement: FRC and TLC. Correct principle: VC = IRV + TV + ERV; if RV appears in your VC formula, delete it and put it in TLC instead.
• Mistake 2: Writing that the diaphragm relaxes and flattens during inspiration. Why it is wrong: the two halves contradict each other — contraction flattens the diaphragm, relaxation re-domes it, and flattening belongs to inspiration. Correct principle: inspiration means diaphragm contracts and flattens; expiration means diaphragm relaxes and domes upward.
• Mistake 3: Saying the lungs suck air in, or that air is pulled in by the lungs. Why it is wrong: lungs contain no muscle and cannot pull anything. The thoracic chamber expands, intrapulmonary pressure falls, and the pressure of the outside atmosphere pushes air in. Correct principle: always write the chain — volume up, pressure down, air in — and credit the atmosphere with the pushing.
• Mistake 4: Quoting the pO2 of oxygenated blood as 104 mm Hg. Why it is wrong: 104 is the pO2 of alveolar air; the blood leaving the lungs is recorded at 95 — close, but not equal, and the table keeps them apart on purpose. Correct principle: 159 atmosphere, 104 alveoli, 95 oxygenated blood, 40 deoxygenated blood and tissues — recite the staircase.
• Mistake 5: Giving bicarbonate a minority share, or giving the biggest share of CO2 transport to carbamino-haemoglobin. Why it is wrong: the ranking is bicarbonate about 70 percent, carbamino 20 to 25 percent, dissolved about 7 percent — carbamino is the second lane, not the highway. Correct principle: 70-20-7, in that order, is the chapter's most-tested number trio.
• Mistake 6: Saying the CO2 of carbamino-haemoglobin binds to the haem iron. Why it is wrong: the iron of haem is oxygen's binding site; carbamino CO2 attaches to amino groups of the globin protein. Correct principle: iron for oxygen, globin for carbamino carbon dioxide — different addresses on the same molecule.
• Mistake 7: Claiming breathing rate is controlled mainly by blood oxygen level. Why it is wrong: NCERT states the role of oxygen in regulating rhythm is quite insignificant; the chemosensitive area and the aortic and carotid receptors respond to CO2 and hydrogen ions. Correct principle: CO2 and H+ are the monitored variables; oxygen is nearly a bystander in normal regulation.
• Mistake 8: Placing the pneumotaxic centre in the medulla, or the rhythm centre in the pons. Why it is wrong: the pair is fixed — rhythm centre in the medulla, pneumotaxic centre in the pons, and only the second one limits inspiration. Correct principle: medulla makes the rhythm; pons moderates it. Repeat the pairing in that order every time.
• Mistake 9: Believing a deep enough effort can expel the residual volume, or that RV shows on a spirometer. Why it is wrong: RV is defined as what remains AFTER maximal expiration — no effort, however violent, is counted beyond maximal. Air that never leaves cannot be recorded leaving. Correct principle: RV, and anything containing it (FRC, TLC), cannot be fully measured by simple spirometry of expired air.
• Mistake 10: Swapping the lesions of asthma and emphysema. Why it is wrong: asthma is inflammation and narrowing of bronchi and bronchioles; emphysema is destruction of alveolar walls with loss of surface area. Correct principle: asthma narrows pipes; emphysema deletes surface. Match the disorder to the broken station.
• Mistake 11: Putting FRC after a forced expiration. Why it is wrong: FRC is the air left after a NORMAL expiration — ERV plus RV, about 2100 to 2300 mL. After a forced expiration only the RV remains, which is a different, smaller quantity. Correct principle: "functional residual" happens at rest between breaths — normal expiration, not maximal effort.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A healthy adult has tidal volume 500 mL, inspiratory reserve volume 3000 mL and expiratory reserve volume 1100 mL. Find his inspiratory capacity and expiratory capacity, and check both against the NCERT ranges.
• Given: TV 500, IRV 3000, ERV 1100 mL. IC = TV + IRV; EC = TV + ERV.
• Principle: Capacities are sums of volumes; inspiratory capacity counts everything that can be drawn in from the end of a normal expiration.
• Steps: IC = 500 + 3000 = 3500 mL. EC = 500 + 1100 = 1600 mL. NCERT ranges: IC 3000 to 3500 — the answer touches the top of the range, acceptable because the given IRV was the top of its range. EC 1500 to 1600 — again at the top, consistently.
• Answer: IC 3500 mL; EC 1600 mL.
• Reasoning: When givens sit at the ends of their ranges, the computed capacity legitimately sits at the end of its range — ranges compose, and checking a result against its NCERT band is the fastest audit in this chapter.
• Mistake to avoid: Building IC as IRV + ERV or EC as IRV + TV — each capacity is anchored on the tidal volume plus ONE reserve, and the reserve's name matches the capacity's name.
• Example 2 — Problem: A subject's four volumes are IRV 2500 mL, TV 500 mL, ERV 1100 mL and RV 1100 mL. Calculate his vital capacity, total lung capacity and functional residual capacity.
• Given: All four volumes. VC = IRV + TV + ERV; TLC = VC + RV; FRC = ERV + RV.
• Principle: VC counts movable air only; TLC adds the unmovable residue; FRC is what remains after a normal expiration.
• Steps: VC = 2500 + 500 + 1100 = 4100 mL (NCERT band 3500 to 4500 — inside). TLC = 4100 + 1100 = 5200 mL (band 5000 to 6000 — inside). FRC = 1100 + 1100 = 2200 mL (band 2100 to 2300 — inside).
• Answer: VC 4100 mL; TLC 5200 mL; FRC 2200 mL.
• Reasoning: Three different capacities from one volume set — note how RV enters two of the three answers but is barred from VC. The pattern of where RV is allowed is the whole lesson.
• Mistake to avoid: Writing TLC as IRV + TV + ERV and stopping — that sum is VC. TLC must add RV as the final step.
• Example 3 — Problem: At an alveolus, the air has pO2 104 mm Hg and pCO2 40 mm Hg. The blood arriving in the capillary has pO2 40 mm Hg and pCO2 45 mm Hg. State the size and direction of each gas's gradient, and explain why the carbon dioxide gradient, though far smaller, is sufficient.
• Given: The standard alveolar row of the NCERT table. Gradient equals the difference between the two partial pressures.
• Principle: Diffusion runs from higher to lower partial pressure, and CO2 diffuses far more readily because its solubility is 20 to 25 times that of oxygen.
• Steps: Oxygen: 104 minus 40 equals 64 mm Hg, direction air to blood. Carbon dioxide: 45 minus 40 equals 5 mm Hg, direction blood to air. The CO2 slope is about one-thirteenth the oxygen slope, but each unit of CO2 slope moves gas 20 to 25 times as effectively, so both gases complete their exchange within the capillary's transit.
• Answer: O2 — 64 mm Hg inward; CO2 — 5 mm Hg outward; the small CO2 gradient suffices because of its 20 to 25 times greater solubility.
• Reasoning: This is the chapter's favourite numerical contrast: a big slope for the reluctant gas, a small slope for the willing one, and balanced traffic as the result.
• Mistake to avoid: Reporting the CO2 gradient as 40 minus 45 and concluding CO2 enters the blood — keep the sign convention clean: always name the direction in words (blood to air), not just a signed number.
• Example 4 — Problem: Oxygenated blood with pO2 95 mm Hg and pCO2 40 mm Hg reaches a resting tissue where the fluid has pO2 40 mm Hg and pCO2 45 mm Hg. Describe both exchanges and give the partial pressures of the blood that leaves the tissue.
• Given: Tissue row of the table: pO2 40, pCO2 45. Blood leaves in equilibrium with the tissue values it has just served.
• Principle: The tissue exchange is the alveolar exchange with both slopes reversed — oxygen out of the blood, carbon dioxide into it.
• Steps: Oxygen: 95 minus 40 equals a 55 mm Hg slope, blood to tissue. Carbon dioxide: 45 minus 40 equals a 5 mm Hg slope, tissue to blood. Leaving blood therefore carries pO2 40 and pCO2 45 — precisely the deoxygenated row of the NCERT table, which then returns to the lungs for Example 3 to reload.
• Answer: O2 leaves the blood down a 55 mm Hg gradient; CO2 enters down a 5 mm Hg gradient; departing blood is at pO2 40, pCO2 45.
• Reasoning: Examples 3 and 4 are the same table read at two stations — master one and the other is free. The leaving-blood values matching the arriving-at-lungs values is the built-in check that the circle is consistent.
• Mistake to avoid: Sending CO2 from blood to tissue because "blood carries CO2" — at the tissue, the tissue's pCO2 is the higher one, so CO2 flows into the blood; carrying history does not matter, only the local slope.
• Example 5 — Problem: A resting adult has a tidal volume of 500 mL and breathes 14 times a minute. Of each breath, 150 mL remains in the conducting part — the dead space — and never reaches the alveoli. Calculate (a) the total minute ventilation and (b) the alveolar ventilation per minute.
• Given: TV 500 mL; rate 14 per minute; dead space 150 mL per breath, supplied as data.
• Principle: Minute ventilation counts all air moved; alveolar ventilation counts only the part of each breath that actually reaches the exchange surface.
• Steps: (a) 500 times 14 equals 7000 mL per minute moved in total. (b) Fresh air reaching alveoli per breath equals 500 minus 150 equals 350 mL; times 14 equals 4900 mL per minute.
• Answer: (a) 7000 mL per minute; (b) 4900 mL per minute.
• Reasoning: The 2100 mL difference is air that shuttles in and out of the conducting tubes without ever trading gases — which is why breathing PATTERN matters: slow deep breaths deliver more alveolar ventilation than fast shallow ones at the same minute ventilation.
• Mistake to avoid: Reporting 7000 mL per minute as the air available for exchange — the conducting part takes its fixed toll from every breath before the alveoli see a molecule.
• Example 6 — Problem: Tissues load the passing blood with 100 arbitrary units of carbon dioxide. Using the transport percentages of this chapter, state how many units travel in each of the three forms, and where the largest fraction is chemically converted.
• Given: Total CO2 carried 100 units; shares: bicarbonate about 70 percent, carbamino-haemoglobin 20 to 25 percent, dissolved about 7 percent.
• Principle: The percentages are shares of the total carried; the bicarbonate share is manufactured inside the RBC by carbonic anhydrase but travels in the plasma.
• Steps: Bicarbonate: 70 percent of 100 equals about 70 units. Carbamino: 20 to 25 units. Dissolved: about 7 units. The three lanes total roughly 100 (the percentages are approximate, which is why examiners quote them as "about"). The conversion to bicarbonate happens inside the red blood cells, though the ions then ride in the plasma.
• Answer: About 70 units as bicarbonate, 20 to 25 as carbamino-haemoglobin, about 7 dissolved; the bicarbonate is formed within the RBCs.
• Reasoning: The arithmetic is trivial; the examinable payload is the split of addresses — formed in RBC, carried in plasma — which this example welds to the percentages so they are never recalled separately.
• Mistake to avoid: Assigning bicarbonate formation to the plasma because bicarbonate travels there — travel address and factory address differ, and the factory is the RBC with its carbonic anhydrase.
• Example 7 — Problem: Two tissues receive blood at the same pO2. Tissue A is at rest; tissue B is exercising hard, so it is hotter and its fluid is rich in CO2 and hydrogen ions. In which tissue does haemoglobin release more oxygen, and what has happened to its dissociation curve?
• Given: Same incoming pO2 for both; tissue B has high pCO2, high H+ and high temperature.
• Principle: High pCO2, high H+ and high temperature lower haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen — the curve shifts right (the Bohr effect), so at any given pO2 the pigment holds less and releases more.
• Steps: Tissue A: standard conditions, curve in its standard position; unloading is the normal fraction. Tissue B: all three right-shift factors present at once; the curve moves right; at the same tissue pO2 the saturation is lower, meaning a larger share of the carried oxygen has been given up.
• Answer: Tissue B receives more oxygen — its haemoglobin, curve shifted right by heat, CO2 and acidity, unloads more generously exactly where the demand is.
• Reasoning: This is the Bohr effect read as logistics: the by-products of work (heat, CO2, acid) are themselves the unloading signals, so supply automatically follows demand with no nervous instruction needed.
• Mistake to avoid: Saying the curve shifts left in exercising tissue — left means higher affinity and hoarding, the lung's behaviour; exercising tissue needs the opposite, and heat plus acid always push right.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. The epiglottis — a leaf-shaped elastic cartilage flap over the glottis. It prevents swallowed food and liquid from entering the larynx and trachea. Misconception watch: the epiglottis is not the larynx itself, and it does not produce sound — the larynx (voice box) does that; the flap only gates.
• A2. FRC equals ERV plus RV equals 1100 plus 1200 equals 2300 mL — the top of the NCERT band of 2100 to 2300. It is the air remaining after a NORMAL expiration. Misconception watch: after a FORCED expiration only RV (1200 mL here) remains; calling that FRC is the planted error.
• A3. (a) 104 mm Hg. (b) 95 mm Hg. They differ because the blood, though it loads almost to equilibrium with alveolar air, leaves slightly below it — the NCERT table records the two numbers separately and examiners keep them separate too. Misconception watch: 159 mm Hg is the pO2 of the outside atmosphere, not of the alveoli — quoting it for (a) is the classic slip.
• A4. High temperature. Loading at the alveoli is favoured by high pO2, low pCO2, low H+ (higher pH) and LOWER temperature; heat belongs to the tissue unloading team. Misconception watch: students often file temperature under "more energy, more binding" — binding here is favoured by cool conditions; heat drives release.
• A5. About 70 percent. It is produced inside the red blood cells by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase, which speeds the union of CO2 and water into carbonic acid; the bicarbonate ions then pass into the plasma for transport. Misconception watch: "produced in the plasma" is the standard wrong variant — plasma is the vehicle, the RBC is the factory.
• A6. The pneumotaxic centre is in the pons. It moderates the medulla's rhythm centre by limiting the duration of inspiration — switching off the inspiratory signal — and thereby can change the rate and depth pattern of breathing. Misconception watch: it does not generate the basic rhythm; that is the medullary rhythm centre's job, and swapping the two centres is the most repeated error in this unit.
• A7. The alveolar walls are damaged; the total respiratory surface area available for diffusion falls (alveoli merge into fewer, larger sacs). Misconception watch: emphysema is not a narrowing of bronchi — that is asthma. Emphysema deletes exchange surface; the major cause to name is cigarette smoking.
• A8. The diaphragm contracts and flattens (moving down), and — with the external intercostals lifting the ribs — thoracic volume rises so that intrapulmonary pressure falls BELOW atmospheric pressure, letting air flow in. Misconception watch: "diaphragm relaxes during inspiration" inverts the chapter; relaxation and doming belong to expiration.

• Summary: One breath runs a fixed circuit: air descends the airway (nostrils to alveoli) because the diaphragm and external intercostals expand the airtight thorax and drop intrapulmonary pressure; gases then cross the three-layered respiratory membrane by diffusion, down partial-pressure slopes read straight from one table (atmosphere 159, alveoli 104, oxygenated blood 95, tissues 40 for oxygen).
• Summary: Haemoglobin is the oxygen courier — 97 percent of oxygen rides it, four molecules per pigment, loading under the cool, oxygen-rich, CO2-poor conditions of the alveoli and unloading under the hot, acidic, CO2-rich conditions of the tissues, as the sigmoid curve and the Bohr effect describe.
