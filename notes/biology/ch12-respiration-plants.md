Introduction — Breathing Is Not Respiration
• Plants have no lungs, no blood and no breathing muscles, yet every living cell in a plant respires day and night. This chapter is about the chemistry inside the cell — how glucose is taken apart in controlled steps and its energy is trapped as ATP — and it starts by separating two words students constantly fuse: breathing and cellular respiration.
• DEFINITION — Breathing (gas exchange) is the physical exchange of gases with the surroundings: oxygen diffuses in and carbon dioxide diffuses out. In plants this happens through stomata in leaves, lenticels in woody stems, and the general surface of roots, with air moving through the loose, interconnected spaces between parenchyma cells.
• DEFINITION — Cellular respiration is the stepwise enzymatic breakdown of food substances, chiefly glucose, inside the cell, releasing energy that is captured in ATP. Its summary equation is: C6H12O6 + 6O2 gives 6CO2 + 6H2O + energy. The equation is a balance sheet, not a description — the real process has dozens of intermediates.
• WHY the release must be stepwise — if all the energy locked in glucose were freed in a single burst, it would escape as heat and the cell would cook. Respiration instead oxidises glucose through a sequence of small, enzyme-controlled steps, skimming a usable packet of energy at several points and storing it in ATP, the cell's spendable currency.
• EXAMPLE — Drop a germinating seed lot into a thermos flask with a thermometer and the temperature climbs measurably: respiration is running and some of its energy always leaks as heat. Contrast that with a waterlogged potted plant — its roots suffocate because the soil air spaces flood, oxygen cannot reach the root cells, and respiration is throttled even though the shoot is in open air.
• WORKED PATTERN — For any cell in any organism, ask two questions: where does its oxygen arrive from, and where is its food oxidised? In plants the answers are "locally, through stomata, lenticels or the root surface" and "inside the cell itself, first in the cytoplasm and then in the mitochondrion". Gas exchange is a surface event; respiration is a cytoplasm-and-mitochondrion event.
• YOUR TURN — A leaf is photosynthesising brightly at noon. Is it respiring at the same time? Answer: yes — respiration never switches off in a living cell; at noon photosynthesis simply runs faster, so the leaf takes in carbon dioxide on balance. The two processes are independent, and respiration continues in the dark when photosynthesis stops.
✔ KEY POINT — Respiration is controlled, stepwise oxidation of food to release energy as ATP; it is not burning, and it is not the same act as breathing. Burning releases everything at once as heat; respiration meters the energy out through enzyme-catalysed intermediates.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The statement "plants photosynthesise, animals respire" is false and is planted in options every year. Plants respire in all living cells at all times; photosynthesis happens only in green cells and only in light. If a root cell is alive, it is respiring.

The Big Map — Four Stages of Aerobic Respiration
• DEFINITION — Aerobic respiration in eukaryotic cells runs in four stages: (1) glycolysis, the splitting of glucose to pyruvate in the cytoplasm; (2) the link reaction, in which pyruvate is oxidatively decarboxylated to acetyl CoA in the mitochondrial matrix; (3) the Krebs cycle, which fully oxidises the acetyl group in the matrix; and (4) the electron transport system with oxidative phosphorylation, on the inner mitochondrial membrane.
• DEFINITION — A respiratory substrate is any substance oxidised during respiration to release energy. Glucose is the favoured substrate and the one this chapter audits in full, but cells are not obliged to use it: fats, proteins and organic acids can all be respired when the situation demands, entering the pathway at different doors (the amphibolic section at the end shows exactly where).
• WHY a map before the details — almost every numerical question in this chapter is secretly a map question. The ATP value of a molecule depends on which stage produced it and where: a NADH made in the matrix is worth more than one made in the cytoplasm. Fix the four stages, their sites and their order first, and the whole ledger later becomes bookkeeping rather than memorisation.
• EXAMPLE — Compare two cells running the same fuel differently. A yeast cell in an airless vat stops after stage 1 and ferments, collecting just 2 ATP per glucose. A cell in your thigh muscle during a gentle jog runs all four stages on the same glucose and collects around 36 ATP. Same starting molecule, eighteen-fold difference in harvest — the map is the difference.
• WORKED PATTERN — The location drill that settles half the MCQs in this chapter: cytoplasm means glycolysis only. Mitochondrial matrix means link reaction and Krebs cycle. Inner mitochondrial membrane means electron transport and ATP synthase. When a question names a site, the stage is already decided; when it names a stage, the site comes free.
• YOUR TURN — Which stage of respiration is shared by a bacteria-filled curd culture, a yeast cell and a human muscle cell, and why can all three run it? Answer: glycolysis — it needs neither oxygen nor mitochondria, and it sits in the cytoplasm of virtually every living cell.
✔ KEY POINT — Glycolysis is the only stage that is anaerobic and the only stage outside the mitochondrion. If oxygen is absent, the story ends at pyruvate plus whatever fermentation the cell can manage; if oxygen is present, pyruvate enters the mitochondrion and the remaining three stages run.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Follow one glucose molecule through the map: it is split in the cytoplasm into two pyruvates; each pyruvate slips into a mitochondrion and is stripped down until nothing remains but carbon dioxide; the hydrogen it carried rides on NADH and FADH2 to the inner membrane, where oxygen finally accepts it and water forms. Carbon leaves as CO2 at the middle stages; hydrogen leaves as water at the end.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two location swaps are planted constantly: the Krebs cycle is in the mitochondrial matrix, NOT on the inner membrane, and the electron transport system is on the inner membrane, NOT floating in the matrix. Learn them as a pair so a swapped option looks wrong on sight.

Glycolysis — Splitting Glucose in the Cytoplasm
• DEFINITION — Glycolysis, also called the EMP pathway after its discoverers Embden, Meyerhof and Parnas, is the partial oxidation of one molecule of glucose (6 carbons) into two molecules of pyruvic acid (3 carbons each). It takes place in the cytoplasm, uses no oxygen, and is found in virtually all living organisms, aerobic and anaerobic alike.
• The pathway in words: glucose is first phosphorylated to glucose-6-phosphate, rearranged to fructose-6-phosphate, and phosphorylated again to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. This doubly phosphorylated 6-carbon sugar then splits into two interconvertible 3-carbon sugars: 3-phosphoglyceraldehyde (PGAL) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP).
• DEFINITION — PGAL is the effective continuation unit of glycolysis: DHAP is converted into PGAL, so from the split onward every step happens twice per glucose. Each PGAL is oxidised — hydrogen is removed and picked up by NAD+ to form NADH + H+ — giving 1,3-bisphosphoglyceric acid, which then passes through 3-phosphoglyceric acid, 2-phosphoglyceric acid and phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) before ending as pyruvic acid.
• WHY the split matters for every number that follows — one 6-carbon glucose becomes two 3-carbon units, so every yield quoted "per PGAL" must be doubled to get the yield per glucose. ATP figures, NADH figures and pyruvate figures in this chapter all carry this factor of two, and forgetting it is the single most common arithmetic slip in respiration questions.
• EXAMPLE — Mature human red blood cells have no mitochondria at all, yet they survive for months on glycolysis alone, fermenting pyruvate to lactic acid. They are living proof that glycolysis is a complete, self-sufficient energy system for modest needs — the rest of respiration is an optional upgrade for high-demand cells.
• WORKED PATTERN — Track the carbons and nothing can confuse you: 6 (glucose) stays 6 through glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-6-phosphate and fructose-1,6-bisphosphate; then 6 becomes 3 + 3 at the split; then each 3 stays 3 all the way to pyruvate. No carbon is lost in glycolysis — no carbon dioxide is released in this stage at all.
• YOUR TURN — Starting from four molecules of glucose, how many molecules of pyruvate does glycolysis deliver, and how much carbon dioxide? Answer: eight pyruvate, and zero carbon dioxide — the carbons are all still locked inside the pyruvate; CO2 release begins only in the mitochondrion.
✔ KEY POINT — Glycolysis: cytoplasm, no oxygen, glucose (6C) gives two pyruvate (3C each), discovered as the EMP pathway. Pyruvic acid is the key product of glycolysis, and what happens to it next depends entirely on the cell's circumstances.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Glycolysis does not release carbon dioxide and does not need mitochondria — two "facts" that wrong options assert with confidence. Also note the spelling trap: the sugar phosphates PGAL and DHAP are 3-carbon compounds; any option making them 6-carbon is describing the pre-split sugars.

The Glycolysis Ledger — Costs, Gains and Net Two ATP
• DEFINITION — Substrate-level phosphorylation is the direct manufacture of ATP by transferring a phosphate group from a high-energy substrate molecule to ADP. Glycolysis earns its ATP this way — no membrane, no oxygen, no electron chain involved. This term is worth learning precisely, because the ATP made in Krebs (as GTP) is also substrate-level, while the ATP made on the inner membrane is not.
• The investment steps: two ATP are spent early in glycolysis — one when glucose is converted to glucose-6-phosphate, and one when fructose-6-phosphate is converted to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. Think of it as paying an entry fee: phosphorylation makes the sugar reactive and traps it inside the cell so the pathway can proceed.
• The payoff steps: ATP is formed at two points in the second half — when 1,3-bisphosphoglyceric acid converts to 3-phosphoglyceric acid, and when phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) converts to pyruvic acid. Because there are two 3-carbon units running, each payoff happens twice: two plus two makes four ATP produced in total per glucose.
• The hydrogen harvest: when PGAL is oxidised to 1,3-bisphosphoglyceric acid, NAD+ collects the removed hydrogen, forming NADH + H+. Two PGAL per glucose means two NADH + H+ are produced. These carry energy too, but their cash value is only realised later, on the electron transport system — and only if oxygen is present.
• WORKED PATTERN — The ledger that every answer in this area must reproduce: gross ATP produced = 4; ATP invested = 2; NET gain = 4 minus 2 = 2 ATP per glucose, plus 2 NADH + H+ held as promissory notes. Write "gross 4, cost 2, net 2" as one inseparable phrase and the standard trap disarms itself.
• EXAMPLE — Why is the investment not wasteful? Spending 2 ATP to gain 4 is a 100 per cent return, and phosphorylation also prevents glucose from leaking back out of the cell. A sprinter's muscle accepts this bargain millions of times per second; the bargain only becomes a problem when the cell cannot finish the aerobic stages and must live on the net 2 alone.
• YOUR TURN — A cell processes ten glucose molecules through glycolysis and fermentation only. State its gross ATP production, its ATP investment and its net gain. Answer: gross 40 ATP, invested 20 ATP, net gain 20 ATP — fermentation adds nothing beyond glycolysis, so the net stays at 2 per glucose.
✔ KEY POINT — Net gain of glycolysis is 2 ATP per glucose (4 produced minus 2 spent), with 2 NADH + H+ additionally formed in the cytoplasm. Anyone quoting "4 ATP" as the gain of glycolysis has forgotten the entry fee.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Notice how little of glucose's energy glycolysis actually collects: pyruvate still holds most of it. Glycolysis is a down payment, not the settlement — which is precisely why anaerobic cells must burn through glucose frantically fast to meet the same energy bill that an aerobic cell pays with a fraction of the fuel.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The 2 NADH of glycolysis are made in the CYTOPLASM, and their value (up to 3 ATP each if fully cashed on the ETS) is separate from the net 2 ATP. Options that fold the NADH into the "net gain of glycolysis" or that place glycolytic NADH inside the mitochondrion are both wrong.

The Fate of Pyruvate — Three Doors at the Crossroads
• DEFINITION — Pyruvic acid is the key product of glycolysis, and its fate depends on the cellular need and on oxygen availability. NCERT names three fates: aerobic respiration (leading eventually to carbon dioxide and water), lactic acid fermentation, and alcoholic fermentation.
• Door one — oxygen available, mitochondria present: pyruvate is transported into the mitochondria, where it is completely oxidised through the link reaction, the Krebs cycle and the electron transport system. This is the high-yield route, the subject of the next four sections.
• Door two — oxygen absent in animal muscle cells and some bacteria: pyruvate is reduced to lactic acid by the enzyme lactate dehydrogenase, using NADH + H+ as the reducing agent and thereby regenerating NAD+.
• Door three — oxygen absent in yeast: pyruvate is converted to carbon dioxide and ethanol (alcoholic fermentation), catalysed by pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase, again regenerating NAD+ in the process.
• WHY regeneration of NAD+ is the hidden plot — glycolysis consumes NAD+ when it oxidises PGAL. A cell holds only a small stock of NAD+, so if NADH cannot hand over its hydrogen somewhere, NAD+ runs out and glycolysis itself grinds to a halt. Fermentation exists to recycle NAD+; aerobic respiration recycles it through the electron transport system instead. Every pyruvate fate is, at heart, a NAD+ recycling strategy.
• EXAMPLE — The burning ache in sprinting muscles is lactic acid fermentation doing emergency duty: oxygen delivery cannot keep pace, pyruvate is parked as lactic acid, and the debt is settled later when oxygen returns and the lactic acid is oxidised. In a brewery vat there is no "later" — the yeast's ethanol simply accumulates until it kills the yeast, as the next section shows.
• WORKED PATTERN — Decide the door with two questions: (1) Is oxygen available and does the cell have mitochondria? If yes, aerobic. (2) If no — which organism? Yeast gives ethanol plus carbon dioxide; muscle cells and certain bacteria give lactic acid with no carbon dioxide released. Organism plus oxygen status fixes the products completely.
• YOUR TURN — A culture of yeast is bubbling vigorously in a sealed flask. The flask is opened to air with glucose still present. What happens to ethanol production and why? Answer: it slows or stops — with oxygen available, pyruvate is diverted into the mitochondria for aerobic respiration instead of being fermented, and the cell earns far more ATP per glucose.
✔ KEY POINT — The three fates of pyruvate: complete oxidation in mitochondria (aerobic), reduction to lactic acid (muscle cells, some bacteria), or conversion to ethanol plus CO2 (yeast). In both fermentations the essential service is regeneration of NAD+ so glycolysis can continue.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Lactic acid fermentation releases NO carbon dioxide — the 3-carbon pyruvate simply becomes 3-carbon lactic acid. Alcoholic fermentation does release CO2 (3C pyruvate becomes 2C ethanol plus 1C CO2). Options that give CO2 to lactic acid fermentation are a perennial plant.

Fermentation — Survival Without Oxygen
• DEFINITION — Fermentation is the incomplete oxidation of glucose under anaerobic conditions. In yeast, pyruvic acid is converted to carbon dioxide and ethanol; in animal muscle cells under oxygen debt, and in some bacteria, it is converted to lactic acid. Both are stop-gap arrangements that let glycolysis keep running when the aerobic machinery cannot.
• The yeast route in steps: pyruvic acid loses its carboxyl group as carbon dioxide, becoming acetaldehyde — catalysed by pyruvic acid decarboxylase. Acetaldehyde then accepts hydrogen from NADH + H+, becoming ethanol, and NAD+ is regenerated — catalysed by alcohol dehydrogenase. Net effect: the pyruvate is disposed of, and NAD+ returns to service glycolysis.
• The muscle route in steps: pyruvic acid directly accepts hydrogen from NADH + H+, becoming lactic acid — catalysed by lactate dehydrogenase. No carbon is lost, no gas is evolved, and again NAD+ is regenerated. The lactic acid is later carried to the liver or oxidised in place once oxygen supply recovers.
• WHY fermentation is a bad long-term bargain — the products, ethanol and lactic acid, still contain most of the energy of the original pyruvate. Fermentation releases only a small fraction of glucose's energy, some of it lost as heat, and the cell keeps only the net 2 ATP of glycolysis. Anaerobic cells survive, but they must consume far more glucose to do the same work — bakers and brewers exploit exactly this furious glucose appetite.
• WHY fermentation is dangerous for the fermenter — the products are toxic to the cells that make them. Lactic acid acidifies muscle and forces rest; ethanol poisons yeast itself: yeasts poison themselves to death when the concentration of alcohol reaches about 13 per cent, which is why naturally fermented drinks never climb past roughly that strength and stronger drinks must be distilled.
• EXAMPLE — Bread dough rises because yeast fermentation releases carbon dioxide bubbles trapped in the dough; the ethanol mostly evaporates in baking. Wine stops fermenting near 13 per cent alcohol because the yeast has poisoned its own vat. Two kitchen facts, both pure NCERT fermentation chemistry.
• WORKED PATTERN — Fermentation accounting, once and for all: glucose in; net 2 ATP (from glycolysis, none from fermentation itself); NAD+ regenerated; products — ethanol plus 2 CO2 per glucose in yeast, or 2 lactic acid per glucose in muscle. If a question asks "how much ATP does fermentation produce", the honest answer is zero directly — it merely enables glycolysis's 2.
• YOUR TURN — Why can a yeast cell not simply keep its NADH and stop making ethanol once some alcohol has accumulated? Answer: because NAD+ is present in tiny amounts and glycolysis halts without it — converting pyruvate to ethanol is the yeast's only way, without oxygen, of turning NADH back into the NAD+ that keeps glucose splitting and ATP flowing.
✔ KEY POINT — Fermentation = incomplete oxidation; its ATP is only the net 2 from glycolysis; its true function is NAD+ regeneration; its products are toxic, capping natural alcohol at about 13 per cent in yeast cultures.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Options love to claim that fermentation yields extra ATP beyond glycolysis, or that lactic acid builds up in yeast, or that yeast tolerates unlimited alcohol. All three are wrong: no extra ATP, lactic acid is the muscle and bacterial product, and about 13 per cent alcohol is lethal to yeast itself.

The Link Reaction — Pyruvate Becomes Acetyl CoA
• DEFINITION — Once pyruvate enters the mitochondrion, it undergoes oxidative decarboxylation in the mitochondrial matrix: pyruvic acid + CoA + NAD+ gives acetyl CoA + CO2 + NADH + H+. The reaction is catalysed by the enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase and requires magnesium ions (Mg2+) for its activity.
• Unpack the name, because the name is the mechanism: "decarboxylation" means a carboxyl group leaves as carbon dioxide — the 3-carbon pyruvate becomes a 2-carbon acetyl group. "Oxidative" means hydrogen is simultaneously removed and collected by NAD+, forming NADH. "Link reaction" is its nickname because it links glycolysis to the Krebs cycle.
• The per-glucose count: glycolysis delivered two pyruvates, so everything here happens twice. Two pyruvate give two acetyl CoA, two carbon dioxide and two NADH + H+. Carbon tracking: 3C becomes 2C + 1C, twice over — so of glucose's original six carbons, two have now left as CO2 and four remain, riding on the two acetyl groups.
• WHY the product is mounted on coenzyme A — the acetyl group is handed to the Krebs cycle as acetyl CoA, an activated carrier form that the cycle's first enzyme can accept. Acetyl CoA is one of the great crossroads molecules of the cell: it is also the form in which fatty acids enter respiration, as the amphibolic section will show.
• EXAMPLE — Think of pyruvate dehydrogenase as a customs gate at the mitochondrion's inner border: pyruvate shows its papers in the matrix, one carbon is confiscated as CO2, hydrogen is declared on NADH, and the remaining 2-carbon fragment is stamped onto coenzyme A and waved through to the Krebs cycle.
• WORKED PATTERN — Any yield question spanning glycolysis plus the link reaction: pyruvate out of glycolysis = 2 per glucose; multiply every link-reaction figure by 2. If a question starts with four glucose, the link reaction runs on eight pyruvates and yields eight acetyl CoA, eight CO2 and eight NADH — the doubling discipline again.
• YOUR TURN — From one molecule of glucose, how many NADH have been produced in total by the end of the link reaction, counting both cytoplasm and matrix? Answer: four — two from glycolysis (in the cytoplasm) and two from the link reaction (in the matrix). Keep their addresses separate; their ATP values will differ.
✔ KEY POINT — Link reaction: matrix of the mitochondrion; pyruvate (3C) gives acetyl CoA (2C) + CO2 + NADH + H+; enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase, Mg2+ required; per glucose the yield is 2 acetyl CoA, 2 CO2 and 2 NADH + H+.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The link reaction is NOT part of the Krebs cycle, though it is often bundled with it in sloppy notes — NCERT treats it as the separate gateway step (oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvic acid) that feeds acetyl CoA into the cycle. It also does not occur in the cytoplasm: pyruvate must first enter the mitochondrion.

The Krebs Cycle — The Rotary Engine of the Matrix
• DEFINITION — The Krebs cycle, also called the citric acid cycle or tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle after its first product, is the cyclic sequence of reactions in the mitochondrial matrix that completely oxidises the acetyl group of acetyl CoA. With this step, aerobic respiration reaches its carbon endgame: every remaining carbon of the original glucose leaves as carbon dioxide here.
• Step one — the entry: acetyl CoA combines with oxaloacetic acid (OAA, a 4-carbon compound) and water to form citric acid (6 carbons), the first product of the cycle; coenzyme A is released unchanged, free to fetch another acetyl group. The enzyme is citrate synthase. Note the arithmetic of the wheel: 4C acceptor plus 2C fuel gives the 6C first product.
• The decarboxylation stretch: citric acid is rearranged to its isomer and then oxidatively decarboxylated twice in succession. First it loses one carbon as CO2 and one hydrogen pair to NAD+ (forming NADH + H+), becoming alpha-ketoglutaric acid (5C). Then alpha-ketoglutarate undergoes a second oxidative decarboxylation — again CO2 out, NADH formed, CoA briefly attached — arriving at succinic acid (4C), with one GTP generated at this stage (next section audits it).
• The regeneration stretch: succinic acid is oxidised to fumaric acid, and this time the hydrogen is collected by FAD, forming FADH2 — the only place in the cycle where FAD is the acceptor. Fumaric acid adds water to become malic acid, and malic acid is oxidised back to oxaloacetic acid, yielding one more NADH + H+. The wheel has returned exactly to its starting acceptor, ready for the next acetyl CoA.
• WHY the cycle must be a cycle — OAA is a catalyst-like acceptor present in small amounts; if it were consumed, the pathway would stall after a few turns. Regenerating OAA every turn means the cell can burn an endless stream of acetyl groups on a tiny stock of carrier. The continued running of the cycle requires the regeneration of OAA — NCERT states this as a requirement, not a detail.
• WORKED PATTERN — Carbon chain for recall and for checking options: OAA 4C + acetyl 2C gives citrate 6C; then 5C (alpha-ketoglutarate); then 4C (succinate) — and 4C it stays through fumarate and malate back to OAA 4C. The two carbons lost per turn are precisely the two CO2 of the cycle, one at each decarboxylation. Any option showing a 3C intermediate inside the cycle, or a single CO2 per turn, is wrong.
• EXAMPLE — The rotary-engine picture is more than a metaphor for the ledger: per turn, the wheel accepts 2 carbons as acetyl and ejects 2 carbons as CO2 — a balanced exchange. That is why, over two turns, the four carbons that entered as two acetyl groups all leave as four CO2, completing (with the link reaction's two) the six CO2 promised by the respiration equation.
• YOUR TURN — A cell's mitochondria are supplied with abundant acetyl CoA but the enzyme converting malate to oxaloacetate is blocked. Predict the effect on the cycle. Answer: the cycle stalls — OAA is not regenerated, so citrate formation stops; intermediates downstream of the block (malate) pile up while citrate production ceases, even though fuel is plentiful.
✔ KEY POINT — Krebs cycle: mitochondrial matrix; acetyl CoA + OAA (4C) gives citrate (6C), the first product; the chain runs 6C to 5C to 4C; OAA is regenerated; the cycle turns twice per glucose because glycolysis supplies two pyruvates and hence two acetyl CoA.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Krebs cycle questions frequently smuggle glycolysis events into the cycle (ATP investment, PGAL) or place the cycle on the inner membrane. The cycle's own events are only: citrate formation, two decarboxylations with NADH, one FADH2, one GTP, and OAA regeneration — all in the matrix.

The Krebs Ledger — What One Acetyl CoA Pays Out
• The per-turn ledger, to be memorised as a single row: one turn of the Krebs cycle (one acetyl CoA) produces 3 NADH + H+, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP and 2 CO2. The three NADH come from the citrate-to-alpha-ketoglutarate step, the alpha-ketoglutarate-to-succinate step, and the malate-to-OAA step; the FADH2 comes from succinate-to-fumarate; the GTP from the succinyl stage; the two CO2 from the two decarboxylations.
• DEFINITION — GTP is guanosine triphosphate, a close cousin of ATP, and the cycle's single substrate-level phosphorylation product. NCERT's bookkeeping counts it as an ATP equivalent because GTP is converted to GDP with the simultaneous synthesis of ATP from ADP — energy is not lost in the exchange, so "1 GTP" and "1 ATP" are interchangeable in the balance sheet.
• The per-glucose ledger: the cycle turns twice for every glucose (two acetyl CoA), so double the row — 6 NADH + H+, 2 FADH2, 2 GTP and 4 CO2 per glucose from the Krebs cycle. Add the link reaction's separate 2 NADH and 2 CO2, and the mitochondrial carbon account for one glucose closes: 6 CO2 in all, matching the summary equation exactly.
• WHY the cycle makes so little ATP directly — the Krebs cycle is not in the ATP business; it is in the hydrogen business. Its job is to strip hydrogen (electrons) from carbon skeletons and load them onto NAD+ and FAD, because each loaded carrier is worth several ATP on the electron transport system. Judging Krebs by its 1 GTP per turn is like judging a mine by the coins in the manager's pocket — the real wealth leaves by truck, as NADH and FADH2.
• WORKED PATTERN — Quote the ledger in the unit the question uses. "Per pyruvate" and "per acetyl CoA" are the same count (each pyruvate becomes one acetyl CoA). "Per glucose" is exactly double. The classic error is quoting per-turn numbers for a "per glucose" question — or the reverse, doubling the link reaction figures into the Krebs row. Keep three rows separate: link (per glucose: 2 NADH, 2 CO2), Krebs per turn, Krebs per glucose.
• EXAMPLE — Suppose a question asks for the NADH + H+ formed inside mitochondria during complete oxidation of one glucose, before the ETS runs. Audit by rows: link reaction 2, Krebs 6 — total 8 NADH, plus 2 FADH2, all in the matrix. The cytoplasmic 2 NADH from glycolysis are NOT in this count because the question said "inside mitochondria" — address words in questions are marks.
• YOUR TURN — Three molecules of glucose are completely oxidised. How many turns of the Krebs cycle occur, and how much CO2 does the cycle itself release? Answer: six turns (two per glucose), releasing 12 CO2 — two per turn. (The link reaction releases 6 more CO2, bringing the grand total to 18, which is 6 per glucose as the summary equation demands.)
✔ KEY POINT — Per acetyl CoA (per turn): 3 NADH + H+, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, 2 CO2. Per glucose: 6 NADH + H+, 2 FADH2, 2 GTP, 4 CO2 from Krebs, plus 2 NADH and 2 CO2 from the link reaction.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The figure "24 ATP from Krebs per glucose" belongs to the balance sheet AFTER its 6 NADH and 2 FADH2 are cashed on the ETS (18 + 4) plus its 2 GTP. The cycle itself directly manufactures only the 2 GTP. Questions asking what the cycle "produces" want carriers; questions asking its "ATP value" want the converted total — read which one is being asked.

The Electron Transport System — Passing Electrons Downhill
• DEFINITION — The electron transport system (ETS) is the metabolic pathway, located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, through which electrons pass from one carrier to another in a fixed sequence. It exists to process the NADH and FADH2 harvested by the earlier stages: those carriers are oxidised here, and the energy released is used to make ATP.
• The route for a matrix NADH: it is oxidised by NADH dehydrogenase (Complex I), which transfers the electrons to ubiquinone (coenzyme Q, CoQ), a mobile carrier within the membrane. Reduced ubiquinone is then oxidised via the cytochrome bc1 complex (Complex III), handing electrons to cytochrome c. Cytochrome c is a small protein attached to the outer surface of the inner membrane that ferries electrons from Complex III to Complex IV.
• The final complex: Complex IV, the cytochrome c oxidase complex, contains cytochromes a and a3 and two copper centres. It passes the electrons to oxygen. Oxygen sits at the very end of the chain: it acts as the final hydrogen acceptor, picking up electrons and hydrogen ions to form water, and by removing hydrogen from the system it keeps the whole chain flowing.
• The FADH2 shortcut that costs ATP: FADH2 (from the Krebs cycle) is oxidised through Complex II and delivers its electrons to ubiquinone — joining the chain downstream of Complex I. Because its electrons skip the first station, FADH2's run releases less usable energy than NADH's, which is exactly why it yields fewer ATP when the accounts are settled in the next section.
• WHY "downhill" is the right picture — each carrier in the sequence holds electrons a little less tightly than the one before, so electrons pass spontaneously from carrier to carrier, releasing energy in small controlled packets rather than one destructive burst. The released energy is used to pump protons across the inner membrane, setting up the gradient that the next section turns into ATP.
• WORKED PATTERN — Trace any electron pair by station numbers: NADH enters at I, then CoQ, then III, then cytochrome c, then IV, then oxygen. FADH2 enters at CoQ (via II), then III, cytochrome c, IV, oxygen. If an option routes FADH2 through Complex I, or puts oxygen anywhere but the terminus, or makes cytochrome c a membrane complex, it is false.
• EXAMPLE — Cyanide kills by blocking Complex IV: electrons back up along the whole chain, NADH cannot be oxidised, the proton gradient collapses, and ATP synthesis stops despite oxygen-rich blood. The poison proves the chapter's plumbing — one blocked station at the end floods every stage upstream, including the Krebs cycle, which stalls for want of NAD+.
• YOUR TURN — A mitochondrion has functioning Complexes I to III but its cytochrome c is removed. Can NADH still be oxidised, and can ATP be made by the ETS? Answer: no to both — cytochrome c is the only bridge from Complex III to Complex IV, so electrons cannot reach oxygen, the chain halts, and oxidative phosphorylation stops.
✔ KEY POINT — ETS order: Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) to CoQ to Complex III (cytochrome bc1) to cytochrome c (mobile) to Complex IV (cytochromes a and a3, two Cu centres) to oxygen, the final hydrogen acceptor; water is the end product. FADH2 joins at CoQ through Complex II.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Oxygen does not participate in the Krebs cycle, and it is not consumed in glycolysis — it has exactly one job, at the very end of the ETS. Yet remove it and everything stops: the role of oxygen is limited to the terminal stage, but it drives the whole process by removing hydrogen from the system.

Oxidative Phosphorylation — The Proton Turbine
• DEFINITION — Oxidative phosphorylation is the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate using energy released by oxidation-reduction reactions in the ETS. It is called phosphorylation because a phosphate group is added to ADP; it is called oxidative because the driving energy comes from oxidation of NADH and FADH2. It contrasts with substrate-level phosphorylation, where ATP forms by direct phosphate transfer (glycolysis, Krebs GTP step), and with photophosphorylation in photosynthesis, where the energy source is light.
• The mechanism in steps: as electrons flow through the ETS, the energy released pumps protons (H+) from the matrix into the intermembrane space, so protons accumulate there and a gradient builds across the inner membrane. The membrane is impermeable to protons except through one channel — the F0 part of ATP synthase (Complex V). Protons stream back through F0 down their gradient, and this flow drives the F1 headpiece to join ADP and Pi into ATP.
• The anatomy of the turbine: ATP synthase has two parts — F1, the headpiece, a peripheral membrane protein that projects into the matrix and carries the catalytic site where ATP is actually formed; and F0, an integral membrane protein that forms a channel across the membrane for proton passage. NCERT's conversion figure for the machine: for each ATP produced, 2H+ pass through F0 from the intermembrane space to the matrix, down the electrochemical proton gradient.
• The settling of accounts — why NADH beats FADH2: a matrix NADH starts its electrons at Complex I and its run powers the production of 3 ATP; FADH2, entering later at CoQ, powers only 2 ATP. NCERT's balance-sheet rates are therefore: one NADH + H+ gives rise to 3 molecules of ATP, and one FADH2 gives rise to 2 molecules of ATP. These two exchange rates power every calculation in the next section.
• WHY the gradient had to be invented by evolution — substrate-level phosphorylation makes ATP one molecule at a time at fixed stations; a gradient is a rechargeable battery. By storing energy as a proton reservoir, the membrane lets every NADH and FADH2 — wherever it was made — pay into the same account, and lets ATP production continue smoothly between electron deliveries. It is the difference between carrying cash and holding a bank balance.
• EXAMPLE — The hydroelectric dam is the standard analogy and it earns its place: the ETS complexes are pumps lifting water (protons) into the reservoir (intermembrane space); the only spillway turbine is F0-F1; water flowing back down (protons returning to the matrix) spins the turbine (ATP synthase) and generates current (ATP). Punch a hole in the dam — an uncoupler — and the water drains without generating anything: energy escapes as heat and ATP yield crashes.
• WORKED PATTERN — Convert carriers to ATP in two multiplications and never from memory soup: ATP from ETS = (matrix NADH × 3) + (FADH2 × 2). For one fully aerobic glucose, matrix NADH = 2 (link) + 6 (Krebs) = 8, giving 24; FADH2 = 2, giving 4; the ETS alone therefore delivers 28 ATP per glucose under the NCERT scheme, before substrate-level ATP is added.
• YOUR TURN — A mitochondrion oxidises 5 matrix NADH and 3 FADH2 through a complete ETS. How many ATP result, and what else is consumed and produced? Answer: 5 × 3 + 3 × 2 = 21 ATP; oxygen is consumed as the final acceptor, and water is produced.
✔ KEY POINT — Oxidative phosphorylation: proton gradient across the inner membrane, protons returning through F0 of ATP synthase (Complex V), ATP formed at F1 — 2H+ per ATP; NCERT rates: NADH + H+ gives 3 ATP, FADH2 gives 2 ATP.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — F1 is the catalytic head and F0 is the channel — options swap them regularly, and also swap locations: F1 projects towards the matrix side, F0 spans the membrane. And remember, ATP synthase is Complex V; the electron-carrying complexes are only I to IV.

The ATP Balance Sheet — Thirty-Six or Thirty-Eight
• NCERT invites a theoretical exercise: assume respiration runs as one orderly, sequential pathway with no intermediates withdrawn for other uses, and that the NADH made during glycolysis is transferred into the mitochondria and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation. Under those ideal assumptions the full harvest of one glucose can be calculated exactly — here it is, line by line.
• Line 1, glycolysis (cytoplasm): substrate-level ATP, 4 produced minus 2 invested = net 2 ATP. Its 2 cytoplasmic NADH, valued at the full ETS rate of 3 ATP each, contribute 6 ATP. Stage total: 8 ATP.
• Line 2, link reaction (matrix): 2 NADH per glucose, at 3 ATP each = 6 ATP. Stage total: 6 ATP.
• Line 3, Krebs cycle (two turns per glucose): 2 GTP = 2 ATP directly; 6 NADH at 3 ATP each = 18 ATP; 2 FADH2 at 2 ATP each = 4 ATP. Stage total: 24 ATP.
• The theoretical total: 8 + 6 + 24 = 38 ATP per glucose — the maximum the NCERT scheme can produce, and the figure that appears in the classical balance sheet.
• Now the reality clause, in NCERT's own spirit: the assumptions are not really valid in a living system. All the pathways work simultaneously rather than in strict sequence; substrates are withdrawn from and fed into the pathway as needed; ATP is utilised where and when it is needed; enzymatic rates are adjusted moment to moment. And in most eukaryotic cells the two cytoplasmic NADH cannot cash in at the full 3-ATP rate — handing their hydrogen into the mitochondrion via a shuttle effectively costs the value of 2 ATP across the pair.
• The textbook verdict, therefore: there can be a net gain of 36 ATP molecules during aerobic respiration of one glucose in eukaryotic cells. Hold the two numbers with their labels attached: 38 is the theoretical maximum under ideal assumptions; 36 is the accepted net gain in most eukaryotic cells. For brutal contrast, fermentation of the same glucose nets 2 ATP — aerobic respiration is some eighteen times more rewarding.
• WORKED PATTERN — Verify any balance-sheet question by rebuilding rather than recalling: direct substrate-level net ATP = 2 (glycolysis) + 2 (Krebs GTP) = 4. Carrier totals: NADH 2 + 2 + 6 = 10; FADH2 = 2. ETS value at full rates = 10 × 3 + 2 × 2 = 34. Total = 4 + 34 = 38. Then apply the eukaryotic shuttle correction (cytoplasmic NADH worth 2 each, not 3) to land on 36. If your two routes disagree, you have mixed schemes — rebuild cleanly.
• EXAMPLE — Why the honest presentation matters beyond exams: modern research actually measures lower yields (around 30-32 ATP) because the real proton costs differ from the textbook's neat rates. NCERT's 36-versus-38 is itself a simplification — but it is the sanctioned scheme for your examination, and the textbook says so with unusual frankness. Answer 36 for "net gain in eukaryotes"; reserve 38 for "theoretical maximum".
• YOUR TURN — A muscle cell completely oxidises two glucose molecules aerobically (eukaryotic), and ferments three more during a sprint. Total net ATP? Answer: 2 × 36 + 3 × 2 = 72 + 6 = 78 ATP.
✔ KEY POINT — Balance sheet per glucose: glycolysis stage 8 (net 2 direct + 6 from its NADH), link 6, Krebs 24 — theoretical total 38 ATP; net gain in most eukaryotic cells 36 ATP once the cytoplasmic NADH shuttle is accounted; fermentation nets only 2 ATP.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The entire ATP economy of the chapter compresses into four multiplications: NADH × 3, FADH2 × 2, GTP × 1, and the direct net of glycolysis = 2. Master the carrier counts per stage and every balance question in any paper becomes arithmetic, not memory.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Scheme-mixing is the examiner's favourite ambush here: if you count cytoplasmic NADH at 3 ATP each you must arrive at 38, not 36; if the question says "net gain in a eukaryotic cell", answer 36. Also note the Krebs stage total of 24 already includes ETS conversion of its carriers — do not add those NADH again in a separate ETS line.

Respiratory Quotient — Reading the Fuel from the Gases
• DEFINITION — The respiratory quotient (RQ), also called the respiratory ratio, is the ratio of the volume of carbon dioxide evolved to the volume of oxygen consumed during respiration: RQ = volume of CO2 evolved divided by volume of O2 consumed. It is a diagnostic number: measure a tissue's gas exchange and the value of RQ reports which substrate is being burned.
• When carbohydrates are the substrate and are completely oxidised, the glucose equation gives the answer directly: C6H12O6 + 6O2 gives 6CO2 + 6H2O — six volumes of carbon dioxide leave for six volumes of oxygen taken in, so RQ = 6/6 = 1. Equal exchange is the signature of carbohydrate burning.
• When fats are the substrate, RQ is less than 1. NCERT's example is the fat tripalmitin: 2(C51H98O6) + 145O2 gives 102CO2 + 98H2O + energy, so RQ = 102/145 = 0.7. When proteins are the respiratory substrates, the ratio is about 0.9. Fats and proteins are oxygen-poor fuels compared with carbohydrate, so they demand extra oxygen per carbon dioxide released — and the ratio falls below one.
• When organic acids are the substrate, RQ exceeds 1. NCERT's example is oxalic acid: 2(COOH)2 + O2 gives 4CO2 + 2H2O + energy, so RQ = 4/1 = 4. Organic acids are oxygen-rich: much of the oxygen needed for their oxidation is already inside the molecule, so little is taken from the air while plenty of CO2 pours out.
• The special case of no oxygen at all: in anaerobic respiration (fermentation), carbon dioxide is evolved but oxygen is not consumed at all — the denominator is zero, and RQ is accordingly treated as infinity. This is why a fermenting flask's gas output, with no matching intake, is diagnostic on its own.
• WHY the ratio tracks fuel chemistry — RQ is really a report on how oxidised the fuel already is. A fuel carrying much of its own oxygen (organic acids) needs little from outside; a fuel carrying almost none (fats, mostly carbon and hydrogen) needs a great deal. The gases outside the tissue betray the molecules inside it.
• EXAMPLE — Germinating seeds make the classic practical: starchy seeds like wheat respire carbohydrate and show RQ near 1; oily seeds like castor and groundnut respire stored fats and show RQ well below 1, around 0.7. The same experiment, two seeds, two substrates, two ratios — the standard laboratory demonstration of this whole section.
• WORKED PATTERN — To compute RQ from any respiration equation: write the balanced equation, read the coefficient of CO2 (numerator) and of O2 (denominator), divide. To predict RQ from a named substrate: carbohydrates 1; fats below 1 (0.7 for tripalmitin); proteins about 0.9; organic acids above 1 (4 for oxalic acid); anaerobic — infinity. One caution from NCERT: in living organisms respiratory substrates are rarely pure — mixtures give intermediate values.
• YOUR TURN — A respirometer shows a tissue consuming 10 units of oxygen while releasing 10 units of carbon dioxide, and a second tissue consuming 20 units of oxygen while releasing 14 of carbon dioxide. Give each RQ and the likely substrate. Answer: first RQ = 1, carbohydrate; second RQ = 0.7, fat.
✔ KEY POINT — RQ = CO2 evolved / O2 consumed. Carbohydrates: 1. Fats: less than 1 (tripalmitin 0.7). Proteins: about 0.9. Organic acids: more than 1 (oxalic acid 4). Anaerobic respiration: infinity.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two planted errors: inverting the ratio (O2 over CO2) and claiming fats have RQ greater than 1. Anchor the order with the phrase "carbon out over oxygen in" — and remember fats, the oxygen-hungry fuels, sit BELOW 1, while oxygen-rich organic acids sit above it.

The Amphibolic Pathway — Catabolism Meets Anabolism
• DEFINITION — A pathway that takes part in both breakdown and building — catabolism and anabolism — is called amphibolic. The respiratory pathway is the textbook example: it is involved in the breakdown of carbohydrates, fats and proteins for energy, and its intermediates are simultaneously withdrawn as raw materials for synthesis. NCERT concludes it is better to consider the respiratory pathway as an amphibolic pathway rather than as a catabolic pathway.
• The entry doors for other fuels: glucose is the favoured substrate, but when organisms respire other substances, they enter at characteristic points. Fats are first broken down to glycerol and fatty acids: glycerol is converted into PGAL and so enters glycolysis, while fatty acids are degraded to acetyl CoA and enter at the Krebs cycle's front door.
• The protein door: proteins are broken to amino acids, and depending on their structure the amino acids — after deamination (removal of the amino group) — enter the pathway at different stages: some become pyruvate, some acetyl CoA, some feed directly into the Krebs cycle at alpha-ketoglutarate or oxaloacetate. There is no single protein entry point; the door depends on the amino acid.
• The exit doors for synthesis: when the organism needs fatty acids, acetyl CoA is withdrawn from the respiratory pathway and diverted to fatty-acid synthesis instead of entering the Krebs cycle. Likewise, alpha-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate can be withdrawn to build amino acids, and glycolytic intermediates feed several other syntheses. The wheel both burns fuel and supplies a factory.
• WHY this dual citizenship matters conceptually — a living cell is not a furnace with a separate factory next door; it is one chemical economy. The same acetyl CoA that is burned when energy is scarce is built into fat when food is plentiful. Intermediates on the respiratory highway carry two-way traffic, which is also one of the reasons (from the balance-sheet section) that a perfectly rigid ATP audit is impossible in a real cell.
• EXAMPLE — Consider two seeds: a starchy wheat grain germinating on carbohydrate runs the pathway mainly as a burning engine, while an oily castor seed must first convert its stored fat — fatty acids to acetyl CoA, glycerol to PGAL — before the same engine can burn it. One pathway, multiple fuels, and intermediates flowing both ways: amphibolic, in action.
• WORKED PATTERN — For entry-point questions, learn the three arrows: carbohydrate enters at the top (glucose, then glycolysis); fat enters in two places (glycerol half at PGAL, fatty acids at acetyl CoA); protein enters at many places (pyruvate, acetyl CoA, alpha-ketoglutarate, OAA) after deamination. For exit-point questions, the two named withdrawals are acetyl CoA for fatty-acid synthesis and Krebs intermediates (alpha-ketoglutarate, OAA) for amino-acid synthesis.
• YOUR TURN — A student writes: "Respiration is a purely catabolic pathway because it breaks glucose down." Correct the statement in one line. Answer: the respiratory pathway is amphibolic, because alongside breakdown it supplies intermediates — acetyl CoA, alpha-ketoglutarate, OAA — that are withdrawn for synthesising fatty acids and amino acids.
✔ KEY POINT — Respiratory pathway = amphibolic: fats enter via glycerol to PGAL and fatty acids to acetyl CoA; amino acids enter at pyruvate, acetyl CoA or Krebs intermediates after deamination; acetyl CoA and Krebs intermediates are withdrawn for fatty-acid and amino-acid synthesis.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Fatty acids do NOT enter glycolysis, and they are never converted into glucose in this scheme — they are degraded straight to acetyl CoA. Likewise, glycerol does not enter the Krebs cycle directly; it becomes PGAL and joins glycolysis. Wrong-door options are this section's favourite trap.

✔ KEY POINT — The four stages and their addresses: glycolysis — cytoplasm; link reaction — mitochondrial matrix; Krebs cycle — mitochondrial matrix; ETS and oxidative phosphorylation — inner mitochondrial membrane.
✔ KEY POINT — Glycolysis (EMP pathway): glucose (6C) gives 2 pyruvate (3C); 4 ATP produced, 2 invested, NET gain 2 ATP; 2 NADH + H+ formed; no oxygen used; no CO2 released.
✔ KEY POINT — Pyruvate's three fates: aerobic oxidation in mitochondria; lactic acid fermentation (muscle cells, some bacteria — no CO2); alcoholic fermentation (yeast — ethanol + CO2). Both fermentations regenerate NAD+; enzymes: lactate dehydrogenase; pyruvic acid decarboxylase and alcohol dehydrogenase.
✔ KEY POINT — Fermentation yields no ATP of its own (net remains 2 per glucose) and its products are toxic: yeast poisons itself at about 13 per cent alcohol, the ceiling of natural fermentation.
✔ KEY POINT — Link reaction (oxidative decarboxylation, matrix): pyruvate (3C) + CoA + NAD+ gives acetyl CoA (2C) + CO2 + NADH + H+; enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase, Mg2+ needed; per glucose: 2 acetyl CoA, 2 CO2, 2 NADH.
✔ KEY POINT — Krebs cycle per turn (per acetyl CoA): 3 NADH + H+, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, 2 CO2; first product citric acid (6C); chain 6C to 5C to 4C; OAA regenerated; two turns per glucose give 6 NADH, 2 FADH2, 2 GTP, 4 CO2.

✔ KEY POINT — Exchange rates (NCERT scheme): NADH + H+ gives 3 ATP; FADH2 gives 2 ATP. ATP synthase is Complex V: F0 is the membrane proton channel, F1 the catalytic head facing the matrix; 2H+ pass through F0 for each ATP produced.
✔ KEY POINT — Balance sheet per glucose: glycolysis stage 8 ATP (net 2 direct + 6 from 2 NADH), link reaction 6 ATP, Krebs stage 24 ATP — theoretical total 38 ATP; net gain in most eukaryotic cells 36 ATP (cytoplasmic NADH shuttle caveat); fermentation only 2 ATP.
✔ KEY POINT — RQ = CO2 evolved / O2 consumed: carbohydrates 1; fats less than 1 (tripalmitin: 102/145 = 0.7); proteins about 0.9; organic acids more than 1 (oxalic acid: 4/1 = 4); anaerobic respiration infinity.
✔ KEY POINT — The respiratory pathway is amphibolic, not purely catabolic: glycerol enters as PGAL, fatty acids as acetyl CoA, amino acids (after deamination) at pyruvate, acetyl CoA or Krebs intermediates; acetyl CoA is withdrawn for fatty-acid synthesis and alpha-ketoglutarate/OAA for amino-acid synthesis.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Quoting the gain of glycolysis as 4 ATP. Why it is wrong: four ATP are indeed produced, but two were spent phosphorylating glucose and fructose-6-phosphate earlier in the same pathway. Correct principle: net gain is 2 ATP per glucose — always subtract the investment.
• Mistake 2: Placing the Krebs cycle on the inner mitochondrial membrane and the ETS in the matrix. Why it is wrong: the two mitochondrial addresses are precisely reversed in this error. Correct principle: matrix hosts the link reaction and Krebs cycle; the inner membrane hosts the ETS and ATP synthase.
• Mistake 3: Crediting fermentation with its own ATP production. Why it is wrong: no step of fermentation makes ATP; its chemistry only converts pyruvate and recycles NADH back to NAD+. Correct principle: fermentation yields zero ATP itself — it keeps glycolysis (net 2 ATP) alive by regenerating NAD+.
• Mistake 4: Doubling errors between per-pyruvate and per-glucose ledgers. Why it is wrong: one glucose makes two pyruvate, so per-turn Krebs figures (3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, 2 CO2) must be doubled for glucose, while glycolysis figures are already per glucose and must not be doubled again. Correct principle: fix the unit of the question first — per glucose, per pyruvate, or per acetyl CoA — then multiply exactly once.
• Mistake 5: Saying oxygen is consumed in the Krebs cycle or needed by glycolysis. Why it is wrong: neither stage uses oxygen directly; oxygen's only role is as the final hydrogen acceptor at the end of the ETS, though without it the whole aerobic sequence backs up and stalls. Correct principle: oxygen is terminal but decisive — absent from every step except the last, yet indispensable for aerobic respiration as a whole.
• Mistake 6: Giving carbon dioxide to lactic acid fermentation. Why it is wrong: pyruvic acid (3C) is merely reduced to lactic acid (3C) — no carbon leaves. It is alcoholic fermentation that decarboxylates pyruvate, releasing CO2 as 3C becomes 2C ethanol. Correct principle: lactic acid — no CO2; alcoholic — CO2 released.
• Mistake 7: Writing RQ upside down, or assigning fats an RQ above 1. Why it is wrong: RQ is CO2 evolved divided by O2 consumed; fats, being oxygen-poor, consume proportionally more oxygen and so score below 1 (0.7). Correct principle: "carbon out over oxygen in"; oxygen-rich organic acids exceed 1, oxygen-poor fats fall below 1.
• Mistake 8: Mixing the 36 and 38 ATP schemes mid-calculation. Why it is wrong: 38 requires valuing cytoplasmic NADH at 3 ATP each; 36 applies the eukaryotic shuttle correction. Summing carriers at full value and then writing 36 (or applying the correction and writing 38) contradicts itself. Correct principle: choose the scheme the question names — theoretical maximum 38, net gain in eukaryotes 36.
• Mistake 9: Calling the respiratory pathway purely catabolic. Why it is wrong: its intermediates are withdrawn for synthesis — acetyl CoA for fatty acids, alpha-ketoglutarate and OAA for amino acids — while fats and proteins also enter it for breakdown. Correct principle: the pathway is amphibolic; breakdown and building share the same highway.
• Mistake 10: Treating cytochrome c as one of the membrane complexes, or giving FADH2 the same 3-ATP value as NADH. Why it is wrong: cytochrome c is a small mobile protein on the outer surface of the inner membrane, shuttling between Complexes III and IV; and FADH2 enters the chain at CoQ, downstream of Complex I, so its electrons do less work. Correct principle: cytochrome c is a carrier, not a complex; NADH = 3 ATP, FADH2 = 2 ATP.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A yeast culture ferments 5 molecules of glucose completely. How many molecules of ATP does it gain in net terms, how many of carbon dioxide are released, and how many of ethanol are formed?
• Given: Alcoholic fermentation; per glucose the products are 2 ethanol + 2 CO2, and the only ATP is glycolysis's net 2.
• Principle: Fermentation adds no ATP of its own; scale the per-glucose figures by 5.
• Steps: Net ATP = 5 × 2 = 10. CO2 = 5 × 2 = 10 molecules. Ethanol = 5 × 2 = 10 molecules.
• Answer: 10 ATP net, 10 CO2, 10 ethanol.
• Reasoning: The carbon check closes the account: 5 glucose carry 30 carbons; 10 ethanol carry 20 and 10 CO2 carry 10 — total 30. Nothing is lost.
• Mistake to avoid: Multiplying some mythical "fermentation ATP" — there is none; and note lactic acid fermentation would give the same 10 ATP but zero CO2.
• Example 2 — Problem: In a eukaryotic cell, two molecules of glucose are completely oxidised aerobically. Compute the net gain of ATP, showing the build-up for one glucose first.
• Given: NCERT scheme; carrier rates NADH = 3 ATP, FADH2 = 2 ATP; cytoplasmic NADH subject to the shuttle correction in eukaryotes.
• Principle: Build the theoretical sheet (38), then apply the eukaryotic correction to reach 36, then double.
• Steps: Per glucose — glycolysis: net 2 direct + 2 NADH valued up to 6; link: 2 NADH = 6; Krebs: 2 GTP + 6 NADH (18) + 2 FADH2 (4) = 24. Theoretical total = 2 + 6 + 6 + 24 = 38. Eukaryotic shuttle correction lowers the two cytoplasmic NADH from 6 to 4 ATP value, giving net 36 per glucose. For two glucose: 2 × 36 = 72 ATP.
• Answer: 72 ATP net.
• Reasoning: Every figure traces to a stage and a site; if an examiner's option list offers 76, it is the uncorrected theoretical figure for two glucose (2 × 38) — the question's word "net" and "eukaryotic" select 36 per glucose.
• Mistake to avoid: Applying the shuttle correction per glucose twice, or correcting the matrix NADH as well — only the two cytoplasmic NADH from glycolysis are affected.
• Example 3 — Problem: Three molecules of glucose are completely oxidised in a plant cell. How many times does the Krebs cycle turn, and how many NADH, FADH2, GTP and CO2 does the cycle itself produce?
• Given: Two acetyl CoA per glucose, so two turns per glucose; per-turn yield 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP, 2 CO2.
• Principle: Turns = glucose × 2 = 6; multiply the per-turn row by 6.
• Steps: Turns = 3 × 2 = 6. NADH = 6 × 3 = 18. FADH2 = 6 × 1 = 6. GTP = 6 × 1 = 6. CO2 = 6 × 2 = 12.
• Answer: 6 turns; 18 NADH, 6 FADH2, 6 GTP, 12 CO2 from the Krebs cycle.
• Reasoning: The question restricts itself to the cycle — the link reaction's additional 6 NADH and 6 CO2 (for 3 glucose) belong to a different row and are excluded.
• Mistake to avoid: Answering 3 turns by equating turns with glucose molecules — each glucose sends TWO acetyl groups around the wheel.
• Example 4 — Problem: A respirometer experiment on germinating seeds records 145 units of oxygen consumed and 102 units of carbon dioxide released. Calculate RQ and identify the probable respiratory substrate.
• Given: RQ = CO2 evolved / O2 consumed = 102 / 145.
• Principle: Divide, then match the value against the substrate signatures: carbohydrate 1, fat about 0.7, protein about 0.9, organic acid above 1.
• Steps: 102 divided by 145 = 0.703, approximately 0.7. A value of 0.7 matches the fat signature — it is exactly the RQ NCERT quotes for tripalmitin.
• Answer: RQ is approximately 0.7; the seeds are respiring a fat (stored oil), as in castor or groundnut seeds.
• Reasoning: Gas volumes alone identified the fuel: fats are oxygen-poor, so the tissue must inhale proportionally more oxygen per carbon dioxide exhaled, dragging the ratio below 1.
• Mistake to avoid: Inverting the ratio (145/102 = 1.42) and concluding "organic acid" — the formula is CO2 over O2, carbon out over oxygen in.
• Example 5 — Problem: Oxalic acid is respired by a tissue according to: 2(COOH)2 + O2 gives 4CO2 + 2H2O + energy. Find the RQ, and state why it exceeds 1.
• Given: Balanced equation with coefficients CO2 = 4, O2 = 1.
• Principle: RQ = coefficient of CO2 / coefficient of O2; organic acids are oxygen-rich substrates.
• Steps: RQ = 4 / 1 = 4. It exceeds 1 because oxalic acid already carries much oxygen within its own molecule, so very little atmospheric oxygen is needed while much CO2 is released.
• Answer: RQ = 4.
• Reasoning: Compare with glucose (RQ 1): the more oxygen the fuel brings with it, the less it must borrow, and the higher RQ climbs. This is the mirror image of fats (RQ below 1), which bring almost no oxygen of their own.
• Mistake to avoid: Counting the "2" before (COOH)2 into the oxygen coefficient — the O2 coefficient in the equation is 1, and only the O2 term (not the substrate) forms the denominator.
• Example 6 — Problem: The mitochondria of a cell oxidise, through the ETS, 5 molecules of NADH formed in the matrix and 3 molecules of FADH2. How many ATP are generated by oxidative phosphorylation?
• Given: NCERT exchange rates — matrix NADH = 3 ATP each; FADH2 = 2 ATP each.
• Principle: ATP = (NADH × 3) + (FADH2 × 2); FADH2 enters at CoQ and so skips the first energy-yielding station.
• Steps: NADH contribution = 5 × 3 = 15. FADH2 contribution = 3 × 2 = 6. Total = 15 + 6 = 21 ATP.
• Answer: 21 ATP.
• Reasoning: Oxygen consumption and water formation accompany this: the 8 carrier molecules deliver 8 electron pairs to oxygen, producing 8 molecules of water at Complex IV's exit.
• Mistake to avoid: Giving FADH2 the 3-ATP rate — that single slip inflates the answer to 24 and is the most common ETS arithmetic error.
• Example 7 — Problem: A sprinter's muscles completely ferment 3 glucose molecules during a race, then oxidise 2 glucose molecules completely and aerobically during recovery (eukaryotic accounting). What is the total net ATP harvested across both phases?
• Given: Fermentation net = 2 ATP per glucose; aerobic net (eukaryotic) = 36 ATP per glucose.
• Principle: Total = (fermented glucose × 2) + (aerobic glucose × 36).
• Steps: Fermentation: 3 × 2 = 6 ATP. Aerobic: 2 × 36 = 72 ATP. Combined: 6 + 72 = 78 ATP.
• Answer: 78 ATP net.
• Reasoning: The contrast inside one problem is the chapter in miniature — the recovery phase, with fewer glucose molecules, earns twelve times what the sprint phase earned, purely because oxygen let the muscle finish the job.
• Mistake to avoid: Using 38 for the aerobic phase: the question's accounting is eukaryotic net gain (36), and consistency of scheme matters more than the size of the number.

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. Cytoplasm; net gain 2 ATP per glucose (4 produced minus 2 invested). Misconception watch: answers of "4 ATP" quote the gross production; the investment steps at glucose-6-phosphate and fructose-1,6-bisphosphate formation are part of the pathway and must be subtracted.
• A2. Citric acid, 6 carbons; acetyl CoA combines with oxaloacetic acid (OAA, 4C) — hence "citric acid cycle" and "tricarboxylic acid cycle". Misconception watch: citrate is not formed from pyruvate directly; pyruvate must first pass the link reaction to become acetyl CoA.
• A3. Ethanol is toxic to yeast itself: the yeast poisons itself to death when the alcohol concentration reaches about 13 per cent, regardless of remaining sugar. Misconception watch: fermentation does not stop for lack of enzymes or NAD+ — the product kills the producer, which is why stronger alcoholic drinks require distillation.
• A4. The substrate is a stored fat (oil). Fats are oxygen-poor relative to their carbon content, so more oxygen must be consumed per CO2 released, pushing CO2/O2 below 1. Misconception watch: an RQ below 1 does not mean respiration is "incomplete" — tripalmitin is completely oxidised at RQ 0.7; the ratio reports fuel chemistry, not completeness.
• A5. Two GTP per glucose (one per Krebs turn, two turns). GTP counts as ATP because it is converted to GDP with the simultaneous synthesis of ATP from ADP, so its energy is fully transferred. Misconception watch: do not answer "one" — that is the per-turn figure; the question asks per glucose.
• A6. FADH2 hands its electrons to ubiquinone (CoQ), via Complex II — downstream of Complex I. Its electrons therefore skip the first station of the chain and release less energy, yielding 2 ATP against NADH's 3. Misconception watch: FADH2 is not oxidised at Complex I, and it is not "weaker" as a molecule — it simply boards the train one station late.
• A7. The cycle would stop after the available OAA is used up, because citrate cannot form; acetyl CoA would then have no acceptor and could not be oxidised by this route. Misconception watch: OAA is a regenerated acceptor, not a fuel — it is neither consumed net nor produced net in the cycle.
• A8. Aerobic (eukaryotic): net 36 ATP. Fermentation: net 2 ATP. Misconception watch: quoting 38 for the aerobic figure mixes schemes — 38 is the theoretical maximum when cytoplasmic NADH is valued at 3 ATP each; with the eukaryotic shuttle accounting the accepted net gain is 36.

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• Summary: Respiration oxidises glucose in controlled stages to trap energy in ATP. Glycolysis in the cytoplasm splits glucose into two pyruvates for a net 2 ATP and 2 NADH; pyruvate is then fermented (lactic acid or ethanol plus CO2, regenerating NAD+, net still 2 ATP) or, with oxygen, carried into the mitochondrion.
• Summary: In the matrix, the link reaction converts each pyruvate to acetyl CoA (releasing CO2 and NADH), and the Krebs cycle oxidises each acetyl group completely, per turn yielding 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, 1 GTP and 2 CO2 while regenerating OAA. On the inner membrane the ETS passes the carriers' electrons to oxygen — the final hydrogen acceptor — and the proton gradient drives ATP synthase (NADH = 3 ATP, FADH2 = 2 ATP).
• Summary: The audited totals: theoretical maximum 38 ATP per glucose, accepted net gain 36 ATP in eukaryotic cells, and only 2 ATP from fermentation. RQ (CO2 evolved / O2 consumed) identifies the fuel: 1 for carbohydrates, about 0.7 for fats like tripalmitin, about 0.9 for proteins, 4 for oxalic acid, infinity for anaerobic respiration. And the whole pathway is amphibolic — fats, proteins and synthesis all share its intermediates.
