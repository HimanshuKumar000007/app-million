Introduction — Why Evolution Is the Chapter Behind All Chapters
• Every other chapter in your biology syllabus describes what living things are like today — this chapter explains how they got that way. Evolution is the single idea that connects the forelimb of a whale to your own hand, a peppered moth to a pesticide-resistant pest, and your appendix-sized brain jokes to a 1400 cc Neanderthal skull.
• DEFINITION — Evolution is the gradual change in inherited traits of populations over generations, driven by variation, inheritance and selection, leading over long periods to the formation of new species from pre-existing ones.
• WHY this chapter is guaranteed marks — it is built from a short list of named stories examiners recycle endlessly: Miller's experiment, homologous versus analogous organs, Darwin's finches, the marsupial-placental parallels, industrial melanism, the Hardy-Weinberg equation, and the human brain-capacity ladder. Learn the stories with their numbers attached.
• EXAMPLE — One moth species, Biston bitularia, changed its dominant colour in England within a human lifetime because soot darkened the trees. That single story carries natural selection, adaptation and directional change all at once.
• WORKED PATTERN — The reading method for this chapter: every topic answers one of three questions — (1) What is the evidence? (2) What is the mechanism? (3) What are the exact numbers or names attached? Tag each fact with its question as you read, and revision becomes sorting, not re-learning.
• YOUR TURN — A question asks: the thorn of Bougainvillea and the tendril of Cucurbita are which kind of organs? Answer: homologous — same developmental origin (modified stem structures), different jobs. Hold that thought; Section 6 makes it automatic.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The chapter in one breath: life arose from non-living chemistry about 4 billion years ago, diversified by branching descent under natural selection, and every fossil, organ plan and gene in this chapter is a receipt for that journey.

The Stage Is Set — An Earth With No Life On It
• DEFINITION — The Big Bang theory attempts to explain the origin of the universe itself: an unimaginably hot, dense beginning from which the universe expanded and cooled, and galaxies, stars and eventually planets formed.
• DEFINITION — The Earth was formed about 4.5 billion years ago. On the early Earth there was no atmosphere as we know it; water vapour, methane, carbon dioxide and ammonia released from the molten mass covered the surface.
• WHY the early atmosphere matters — it was a reducing atmosphere, rich in methane, ammonia and hydrogen-rich gases and poor in free oxygen. That chemistry is the starting condition for every origin-of-life theory, because energy acting on such gases can build the carbon compounds life needs; today's oxygen-rich air would break them down instead.
• EXAMPLE — Ultraviolet rays split water into hydrogen and oxygen; the light hydrogen escaped, and oxygen combined with ammonia and methane to form water, carbon dioxide and other compounds. Sunlight was rewriting the atmosphere long before any organism existed.
• DEFINITION — Life appeared about 500 million years after the Earth formed — that is, almost 4 billion years ago. Between a lifeless planet and the first living thing lies the entire story of chemical evolution.
• WORKED PATTERN — Fix the two anchor dates now, because questions confuse them deliberately: Earth forms 4.5 billion years ago; life appears about 4 billion years ago. Earth first, life half a billion years later — never the same number, never reversed.
• YOUR TURN — In Miller's flask (next sections), why is free oxygen deliberately excluded? Answer: because the early Earth had a reducing atmosphere; oxygen would oxidise and destroy the organic molecules as they formed, so the experiment must copy the early conditions to be a fair test.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Life originated 4.5 billion years ago" is the planted error — 4.5 billion is the Earth's age. Life's birthday is about 4 billion years ago, roughly 500 million years after the planet.

Chemical Evolution — Life From Non-Living Matter
• DEFINITION — Oparin of Russia and Haldane of England proposed that the first form of life could arise from pre-existing non-living organic molecules — substances like RNA and proteins — and that the formation of life was preceded by chemical evolution: a long build-up of increasingly complex organic molecules from simple inorganic ones.
• DEFINITION — Chemical evolution means chemistry before biology: energy from lightning, volcanic heat and ultraviolet light drove methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water vapour to combine into sugars, amino acids, nitrogen bases, pigments and fats — the parts list of a cell, assembled without any cell.
• WHY this idea was revolutionary — it moved the origin of life into testable science: if molecules could self-assemble under early-Earth conditions, a laboratory flask reproducing those conditions should produce the same molecules. That prediction is exactly what Miller tested.
• EXAMPLE — Think of it as cooking: the early ocean was a dilute broth of gases and energy, and chemical evolution was the slow cooking that thickened it from gases into amino acids, then into larger molecules capable of copying themselves.
• WORKED PATTERN — The chain to memorise as one sentence: inorganic gases plus energy give small organics (amino acids, sugars, bases); small organics polymerise into large organics (proteins, nucleic acids); large organics enclosed in membranes give the first cell-like systems. Each arrow is a step questions can ask about separately.
• YOUR TURN — Who proposed that life arose from pre-existing non-living organic molecules through chemical evolution? Answer: Oparin and Haldane — Oparin from Russia, Haldane from England; the theory is often named for both together.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Chemical evolution is not spontaneous generation of a ready-made organism. Nobody claims a frog or even a bacterium assembled in one step; the claim is a staircase of chemistry over hundreds of millions of years, and exam options that caricature it as instant life are always wrong.

Miller's Experiment — Life's Recipe Tested in a Flask
• DEFINITION — In 1953, S.L. Miller created conditions similar to those on the primitive Earth in a laboratory: an electric discharge (simulating lightning) passed through a closed flask containing methane (CH4), hydrogen (H2), ammonia (NH3) and water vapour, held at 800 degrees Celsius.
[diagram fig_bio_25_1_miller]
• DEFINITION — After about a week of running the apparatus, Miller analysed the condensed liquid and found amino acids — the building blocks of proteins — had formed from the simple gases. No living thing, no enzyme and no pre-made organic chemical had been added.
• WHY the experiment is a landmark — it converted a philosophical debate into an experimental result: the first step of chemical evolution, gases to amino acids, demonstrably works under early-Earth conditions. Similar setups later produced sugars, nitrogen bases, pigments and fats.
• EXAMPLE — Read the apparatus like a map of the early Earth: the gas mixture is the early atmosphere; the spark is lightning; the water flask is the ocean; the condenser is rain cooling and washing products into the sea. Every part of the glass has a planet-part it stands for — questions ask this mapping directly.
• WORKED PATTERN — The four gases are the most-tested list in the chapter: CH4, H2, NH3 and water vapour. Note what is absent — no oxygen, and no carbon dioxide in Miller's original mixture. If an option lists O2 among the flask gases, eliminate it on sight.
• YOUR TURN — A similar experiment is run but the spark is removed. Predict the result and the reasoning. Answer: little or no amino acid formation, because the spark supplies the activation energy the reactions need — in the early Earth that energy came from lightning and UV; remove the energy and the chemistry stalls.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Miller produced amino acids, not life, and not cells, and not proteins. Options claiming he "created life in a flask" or "synthesised living cells" overstate the result and are wrong; the accurate claim is organic building blocks from inorganic gases.

What Counts as Evidence — Reading Evolution's Records
• DEFINITION — Evidence for evolution comes from several independent records that all point the same way: palaeontology (fossils), comparative anatomy and morphology, embryology, and biochemistry and molecular biology. Independent lines agreeing is what makes the case strong.
• WHY examiners love evidence questions — each evidence type comes with a fixed cast of examples, and the question is usually a matching exercise: name the organ pair, name the evidence type. There is no calculation and no ambiguity — only whether you have sorted the cast correctly.
• EXAMPLE — A whale's flipper, a bat's wing, a cheetah's foreleg and your arm look utterly different from outside, yet inside they share the same bones in the same pattern. Anatomy notices the shared skeleton; fossils show the lineages splitting; molecules show matching divergence. Three records, one story.
• WORKED PATTERN — Build a two-column habit: evidence type on the left, its signature example on the right. Fossils: rock layers with different life in different ages. Anatomy: homologous versus analogous organs. Embryology: vertebrate embryos sharing features. Molecules: proteins and genes similar across related species. Every evidence question is a row of this table.
• YOUR TURN — Similarities in proteins and genes across very different organisms belong to which line of evidence? Answer: biochemical or molecular evidence — the deeper, modern record that confirms what anatomy suggested.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Evolution is not believed on one proof; it is the only explanation that simultaneously fits rocks, bones, embryos and molecules. In the exam, that means four separate sections of free-standing marks.

Fossils — The Rock Archive
• DEFINITION — Fossils are the preserved remains or impressions of ancient organisms found in rocks. Rocks form sediments, one layer over another over long spans of Earth's history, and different layers contain fossils of the life that existed when that sediment formed.
• DEFINITION — The study of fossils is called palaeontology. By reading which organisms appear in which layers, palaeontologists reconstruct the history of life — the most direct evidence that life changed over time.
• WHY layers are a timeline — a sedimentary column is a stack of pages written in order: lower layers are older, upper layers younger. A fossil fish deep down and a fossil mammal only in shallow layers is the planet's own filing system, showing life forms appearing, changing and vanishing in sequence.
• EXAMPLE — Fossils of simple invertebrates sit in very old rocks; fishes, then amphibians, then reptiles, then mammals and birds appear in progressively younger rocks. No rabbit has ever been found in ancient invertebrate layers — the archive has an order, and the order is the evidence.
• WORKED PATTERN — When a question mentions rocks, sediments, layers or preserved remains, route it instantly to palaeontological evidence; when it mentions the age sequence of groups (invertebrates about 500 million years ago, jawless fish about 350), it is drawing on this same fossil archive.
• YOUR TURN — Two sedimentary layers are compared: the lower holds only invertebrate fossils, the upper holds fish fossils as well. What do you conclude? Answer: fish evolved later than the invertebrates in the lower layer — the upper layer is younger, and the archive records the order of appearance.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Fossils are evidence from palaeontology, not from "comparative anatomy" — a favourite option-swap. Also, a fossil is usually the hard parts or an impression, not a perfectly preserved whole animal; statements claiming soft complete bodies are the norm overreach.

Homologous Organs — Same Blueprint, Different Jobs
• DEFINITION — Homologous organs are organs that share the same basic structural plan and developmental origin but perform different functions in different organisms. Their similarity is anatomical, inherited from a common ancestor whose body plan they all still carry.
• DEFINITION — The classic set: the forelimbs of a whale (swimming), a bat (flying), a cheetah (running) and a human (grasping) look different and do different jobs, yet all are built on the same pattern of bones — humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges in the same arrangement.
• EXAMPLE — Plants give the second NCERT pair: the thorn of Bougainvillea and the tendril of Cucurbita are both modified stems — same origin — yet one defends the plant and the other helps it climb. Origin same, function different: that is homology outside the animal kingdom.
• WHY homology argues for evolution — there is no engineering reason a flipper, a wing and an arm must share one bone pattern. A shared blueprint under different jobs makes sense only as inheritance with modification: divergent evolution from a common ancestor, each lineage adapting the same inherited parts to new uses.
• DEFINITION — Divergent evolution is the process behind homology: related lineages evolve in different directions from a common form, so the same structure becomes modified for different functions.
• WORKED PATTERN — The two-question test for any organ pair: (1) Is the basic structure or origin the same? (2) Is the function the same? Same structure, different function means homologous. Run every pair through these two questions in this order and the classification cannot wobble.
• YOUR TURN — Classify: the forelimb of a frog and the forelimb of a bird. Answer: homologous — both are tetrapod forelimbs on the same bone plan (humerus, radius-ulna and so on), used for jumping and support in one, flight in the other.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Same function" is not part of homology's definition — students who match organs by what they DO misclassify everything. Homology is about origin and structure. Also remember the thorn and tendril are both stem modifications; calling the tendril a leaf modification destroys the pair.

Analogous Organs — Different Blueprints, Same Job
• DEFINITION — Analogous organs are organs that perform the same function in different organisms but differ in basic structure and developmental origin. They look alike on the outside because the job is alike, not because the inheritance is.
• DEFINITION — The standard pairs: the wings of a butterfly (an outgrowth of the body wall, a modified exoskeleton sheet) and the wings of a bird (a modified forelimb with bones and feathers); the eye of an octopus and the eye of a mammal — both cameras, built differently inside; the flippers of penguins and of dolphins — a bird's wing and a mammal's forelimb pressed into the same paddle job.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT adds a plant pair worth memorising: the sweet potato is a modified root and the potato is a modified stem, yet both store food underground and look like tubers. Same job, different origin — analogy in plants.
[diagram fig_bio_25_2_homology_analogy]
• WHY analogy argues for evolution too — unrelated lineages facing the same environmental demand arrive at similar solutions independently. That is convergent evolution: similarity imposed by the job and the environment, not inherited.
• DEFINITION — Convergent evolution is the independent evolution of similar features in unrelated lineages adapting to similar environments or functions — the process behind analogous organs, and the exact mirror image of divergent evolution.
• WORKED PATTERN — Complete the two-question test from the last section: different structure or origin, same function means analogous. If both structure and function match, suspect close relationship instead; if neither matches, the pair is not an exam pair at all.
• YOUR TURN — Classify: the wing of an insect and the wing of a bat. Answer: analogous — the insect wing is a membranous outgrowth with no bones; the bat wing is a forelimb with elongated fingers. Same job (flight), unrelated blueprints.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The octopus eye and mammal eye are analogous, not homologous, despite both being "eyes" — their internal construction differs fundamentally. And penguin versus dolphin flippers: one is a bird, one is a mammal; their paddles converged. Examiners pair these precisely because surface similarity seduces you into "homologous".

Embryology and Molecules — The Supporting Cast
• DEFINITION — Embryological evidence rests on features seen during embryonic development that are shared by all vertebrates but absent in the adults: embryos of fish, salamanders, tortoises, chicks and humans pass through stages with striking common features, such as a row of vestigial gill slits and a tail, that their adult forms do not share.
• DEFINITION — Ernst Haeckel proposed embryological support for evolution from these shared embryonic features, arguing that development replays ancestry. His proposal was then disapproved after careful study by Karl Ernst von Baer, who noted that embryos never pass through the adult stages of other animals — vertebrate embryos resemble each other's embryos, not other adults.
• WHY the correction itself is a lesson — the evidence survived in weaker, accurate form: embryos of related groups genuinely do resemble one another early on, which still points to common ancestry; what died was the slogan of embryos climbing through adult ancestors. Exam answers must state the corrected version.
• DEFINITION — Biochemical and molecular evidence is the modern record: similarities in the proteins and genes performing similar basic functions among diverse organisms point to shared ancestry — the same logic as anatomy, written in molecules; the more recent the common ancestor, the smaller the molecular differences.
• EXAMPLE — The genetic code is essentially universal, and core machinery — ribosomes, ATP, the chemistry of DNA replication — is shared from bacteria to humans. Such deep sameness is exactly what a single origin of life followed by divergence predicts.
• WORKED PATTERN — Attribution questions in this corner are mechanical: embryological proposal means Haeckel; the careful correction means von Baer. Miller goes with the origin experiment; Oparin and Haldane go with chemical evolution theory. Four names, four fixed addresses — never let them drift.
• YOUR TURN — A statement claims: during development, a human embryo passes through the adult stage of a fish. Verdict? Answer: false as stated — von Baer's correction: embryos resemble other embryos, never the adult forms of other animals; the shared features are embryonic features like gill slits that adults lack.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The gill slits of vertebrate embryos are vestigial embryonic structures, not working gills, and the embryo does not become a fish at any point. Also, do not credit Darwin with the embryological proposal — it is Haeckel's name on that line, and von Baer's on the correction.

Adaptive Radiation — One Ancestor, Many Offspring
• DEFINITION — Adaptive radiation is the evolution of many different species from a common ancestor, each adapted to a different habitat or way of life, typically when a lineage enters a region or situation with many unoccupied opportunities. The pattern radiates like spokes from one hub.
[diagram fig_bio_25_3_radiation]
• DEFINITION — Darwin's finches are the type example: on the Galapagos Islands, Darwin observed a variety of small black birds, all similar to the original mainland seed-eating finches but with beaks altered for different foods — some became insectivorous, others vegetarian — many species radiated from one seed-eating stock within the islands.
• WHY islands breed radiations — an island offers empty niches and few competitors. A single arriving species finds seed-eating, insect-catching and fruit-eating jobs all vacant, so descendants that specialise spread out instead of competing head-on. The mainland parent stays one species; the island children become many.
• DEFINITION — The Australian marsupials are the second great example: within the Australian continent, a number of marsupials — kangaroos, wombats, koalas, Tasmanian wolves and many more — each different from the others, evolved from one ancestral marsupial stock.
• EXAMPLE — Picture the radiation as a family firm splitting into trades: the ancestor arrives with a general toolkit; one branch grazes, one digs, one climbs, one hunts; in a few million years the branches look like different animals entirely, yet the shared toolkit betrays the family.
• WORKED PATTERN — Spotting adaptive radiation in a question: look for one ancestor, one region, many descendant species with different adaptations. Finches equal Galapagos plus beaks; marsupials equal Australia plus diversity. If instead two unrelated groups converge on similar forms, that is the next section's parallel evolution, not radiation.
• YOUR TURN — A single ancestral bird stock on an isolated archipelago gives rise to species with crushing beaks, probing beaks and warbler-like beaks. Name the phenomenon. Answer: adaptive radiation — divergence of many species from one ancestor into different feeding niches.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Adaptive radiation is a special case of divergent evolution, not of convergent evolution — the arrow points from one ancestor outward. Also, the finches' ancestor was seed-eating; the insect-eating and vegetarian forms are the derived novelties, not the starting point.

Marsupials and Placentals — The Parallel Experiment
• DEFINITION — When placental mammals evolved in Australia-like isolation elsewhere, they produced forms remarkably similar to the marsupials: marsupials and placental mammals that occupy similar niches on different continents resemble each other closely even though their lineages split long ago — evolution running the same experiment twice.
• DEFINITION — The NCERT parallel pairs — placental first, marsupial second: anteater pairs with numbat; mouse with marsupial mouse; lemur with spotted cuscus; flying squirrel with flying phalanger; bobcat with Tasmanian tiger cat; wolf with Tasmanian wolf; mole with marsupial mole.
• WHY Australia is the control group — its long isolation let marsupials radiate without placental competitors. Comparing the two faunas is comparing two solutions to the same problem set: burrower, glider, runner, hunter — each niche has a marsupial and a placental version, similar in form, unrelated in recent ancestry.
• EXAMPLE — The Tasmanian wolf and the placental wolf: skull shape, body build and hunting role match closely, yet one rears its young in a pouch and the other with a placenta. Their resemblance is the signature of similar selection pressures — which is why this table is evidence for evolution by adaptation.
• WORKED PATTERN — In matching questions, work by niche, not by looks alone: the digger pairs with the digger (mole, marsupial mole), the glider with the glider (flying squirrel, flying phalanger), the wolf-form hunter with the wolf-form hunter. Niche first, name second — the pairs assemble themselves.
• YOUR TURN — Which marsupial parallels the placental flying squirrel, and which parallels the wolf? Answer: the flying phalanger parallels the flying squirrel; the Tasmanian wolf parallels the wolf.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Marsupial-placental similarity is convergent evolution between distant lineages, not proof they are close relatives; the pouch versus placenta difference is the tell. Students who pair by name fragments alone swap the tiger cat (pairs with bobcat) and the wolf (pairs with Tasmanian wolf) — anchor on the role.

Industrial Melanism — Evolution You Can Watch
• DEFINITION — Industrial melanism is the shift in a population from light to dark forms driven by industrial pollution, shown by the peppered moth Biston bitularia in England: the white-winged (typica) form and the dark-winged (melanic, carbonaria) form of the same species traded places as the environment changed.
[diagram fig_bio_25_4_melanism]
• DEFINITION — The record in collections: in collections made in the 1850s, before industrialisation, there were more white-winged moths on the trees than dark-winged ones. In collections from the same area in 1920, after industrialisation, there were more dark-winged moths — the proportion had reversed.
• WHY the reversal happened — before industry, pale lichen-covered bark hid white moths from bird predators and exposed the rare dark ones. Industry killed the lichens with smoke and darkened bark with soot: now the white moths stood out and were eaten, while dark moths were camouflaged. Predators are the selecting agent; the moths' colour genes did the rest.
• DEFINITION — The proof of camouflage: in countryside areas where industrialisation did not occur, the count of melanic moths was low — lichens survive there, bark stays pale, and white remains the protected colour. Lichens themselves are indicators of air quality: they do not grow in polluted areas.
• EXAMPLE — Think of it as a dress code enforced by birds. 1850: the room (bark) is pale, so the pale suit survives. 1920: soot has repainted the room dark, so the dark suit survives. Nobody designed the moths; the predators did the hiring and firing.
• WORKED PATTERN — The logic chain every melanism answer needs: variation exists first (both forms present); environment changes (soot, lichens die); camouflage decides who is eaten (selection); the surviving form breeds more (frequency shifts). Write all four links — dropping the pre-existing variation link is the classic half-answer.
• YOUR TURN — If pollution controls clean the air and lichens return to the bark near a city, predict the moth trend over generations. Answer: white-winged moths should increase again — pale bark once more favours the light form, so selection reverses direction; nothing about the moths' intentions is involved.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The dark moths did not appear because soot "made" them black, and moths did not darken themselves to match. Melanic variants existed before industry; pollution changed which variant survived. Also fix the dates by content, not digits alone: 1850s collections equal more white; 1920 equal more dark.

Anthropogenic Evolution — Resistance in Real Time
• DEFINITION — Evolution by anthropogenic action is evolution we can watch within our own lifetimes because human activity is the selecting agent: excessive use of antibiotics, herbicides and pesticides selects the few resistant organisms, which then multiply into resistant populations.
• DEFINITION — When antibiotics are used excessively, bacteria that happen to carry resistance survive and reproduce, so strains resistant to the drug appear in a timescale of months or years — not the millions of years students imagine evolution always needs. The same story holds for pests that survive pesticide spraying.
• WHY the timescale collapses — microbes and pests have enormous populations and short generations, so variation is plentiful and selection cycles are fast. A bacterium can run through in a season what a mammal lineage needs ages to do.
• EXAMPLE — A farmer sprays the same pesticide season after season. The first spray kills almost everything except the rare resistant insects, which now breed without competition; within a few seasons the field is mostly resistant and the spray fails. The pesticide did not create the resistance; it selected it.
• WORKED PATTERN — NCERT's philosophical footnote is itself a tested line: evolution is a stochastic process, based on chance events in nature and chance mutations in organisms — not a directed, purposeful march. When an option says evolution is deterministic or goal-driven, this sentence is your rebuttal.
• YOUR TURN — A hospital finds that a previously effective antibiotic now fails against a common infection. Explain in one line, selection style. Answer: overuse of the antibiotic selected pre-existing resistant bacteria, which survived and multiplied until the resistant strain dominated the population.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Resistance is not created by the drug in the individual exposed to it: resistant individuals exist in the population beforehand, by chance mutation, and the drug merely changes who survives. Any option saying bacteria "develop resistance because they need it" is Lamarck in disguise — and wrong.

Lamarck — The Theory That Got the Mechanism Wrong
• DEFINITION — Lamarck proposed that evolution occurred through the use and disuse of organs: organs used constantly grow stronger and larger, organs left unused shrink, and these acquired changes are then inherited by the next generation — the inheritance of acquired characters.
• DEFINITION — His textbook example is the giraffe: ancestral giraffes stretching to browse leaves on tall trees gradually lengthened their necks through generations of stretching, and the stretched necks were passed on, producing today's long-necked giraffe.
• WHY it failed — no mechanism was ever found by which a lifetime's stretching, muscle-building or organ wear could rewrite the hereditary material passed to offspring. A bodybuilder's children are not born muscular. Darwin's framework needed no such rewriting: it works on variation that is already heritable.
• EXAMPLE — Lamarck's logic feels natural because practice does change your own body within your lifetime. The error is the second step: assuming the change crosses into the next generation. Keep the steps separate and you can explain both his appeal and his failure.
• WORKED PATTERN — Exam framing of Lamarck is fixed: keywords are "use and disuse", "acquired characters", "giraffe's neck". If an option attributes neck elongation to giraffes with longer necks surviving and breeding more, that is Darwin, not Lamarck — the names attach to mechanisms, not to the animal.
• YOUR TURN — A weightlifter argues his son will be born with larger muscles. Which theory is he (incorrectly) invoking, and what is the one-line refutation? Answer: Lamarck's inheritance of acquired characters; refutation — changes acquired during a lifetime are not written into the hereditary material, so they are not inherited.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not credit Lamarck with natural selection or Darwin with acquired characters when options shuffle the attributions. Also note NCERT's verdict: Lamarck's theory is largely disapproved — state it as historically important but rejected.

Darwin — Branching Descent and Natural Selection
• DEFINITION — Charles Darwin's theory of evolution rests on two key concepts: branching descent — species sharing a common ancestor diverge and branch into new species over time — and natural selection — the process by which heritable variations that fit an organism better to its environment are preserved, while less fit variations are eliminated.
• DEFINITION — Darwin built the theory from observations made during his voyage on HMS Beagle, including the Galapagos finches, and from the logic of populations: organisms produce far more offspring than can survive, heritable variation exists among them, and the struggle for existence leaves the better-fitted variants overrepresented in the next generation.
• DEFINITION — Alfred Wallace, a naturalist who worked in the Malay Archipelago, reached similar conclusions about natural selection at about the same time, independently — the idea was, as the saying goes, in the air, and both names belong to its history.
• WHY selection is not chance alone — mutation and variation supply raw differences partly by chance, but selection is a filter with a direction set by the environment: on dark bark, dark moths; on pale bark, pale moths. Random input, non-random survival — that is the whole engine: stochastic in variation, adaptive in outcome.
• EXAMPLE — Apply the engine to the finches: beak variations existed; islands offered different foods; birds whose beaks matched the local food ate better and bred more; over generations each island population's average beak shifted. No foresight, no striving — differential survival of heritable variants, repeated.
• WORKED PATTERN — A complete natural-selection answer has five links: overproduction, variation (heritable), struggle for existence, survival of the fitter variants, and inheritance shifting the population over generations. Practise writing the five links for moths, finches and antibiotic resistance until the chain is automatic.
• YOUR TURN — State Darwin's two key concepts and the ship and islands tied to his evidence. Answer: branching descent and natural selection; the ship is HMS Beagle, and the finch evidence comes from the Galapagos Islands.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Darwin did not originate the word-story "survival of the fittest" as a claim that the strongest or fastest wins; fitness in this theory means reproductive fit to the environment — leaving more descendants. A camouflaged, unremarkable moth is fitter on matching bark than any athlete. Also, Darwin could not explain the source of variation — that gap is where mutation theory enters next.

de Vries, Mutations and Where Variation Comes From
• DEFINITION — Hugo de Vries, working on the evening primrose, proposed the mutation theory of evolution: new species arise suddenly through large, single-step mutations — he called this saltation — rather than only by the slow accumulation of small variations that Darwin described.
• DEFINITION — A mutation is a sudden, heritable change in the genetic material. For de Vries, mutations were random and directionless — they appear without regard to whether they will help — and a single large mutation could create a new species in one step, a saltational leap.
• WHY both Darwin and de Vries survive in your syllabus — modern understanding combines them: variation arises from mutation (the abrupt changes de Vries emphasised, plus changes arising during reproduction) and from recombination, the reshuffling of parental genes at gamete formation; natural selection then sorts the resulting variants, mostly gradually. Variation supplies, selection decides.
• EXAMPLE — The summary of variation sources: a mutation produces the dark-wing moth variant (chance, directionless); recombination in each finch generation shuffles beak genes into new combinations; selection then changes which variants become common. Two suppliers, one filter.
• WORKED PATTERN — Attribution drill for the mechanism sections: giraffes stretching means Lamarck; finches and branching descent mean Darwin; Malay Archipelago means Wallace; evening primrose and saltation mean de Vries; the equilibrium equation means Hardy and Weinberg. Five names, five anchors — match name to anchor before reading the options fully.
• YOUR TURN — A new plant form appears in one generation, sharply different from its parent and breeding true. Which theorist's mechanism fits best, and what single word names the leap? Answer: de Vries's mutation theory; the leap is saltation — speciation by a single-step large mutation.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — De Vries's mutations are large and single-step; Darwin's variations are small and continuous. Options that give Darwin "large sudden mutations" or de Vries "gradual accumulation" have swapped the theories. Also, mutations are directionless — they do not aim at usefulness.

Hardy–Weinberg — The Equation of No Evolution
• DEFINITION — The Hardy-Weinberg principle states that allele frequencies in a population are stable from generation to generation when nothing disturbs them: the gene pool — the total genes and their alleles in a population — remains constant. This is equilibrium.
[diagram fig_bio_25_5_hardy_weinberg]
• DEFINITION — The mathematics: if p is the frequency of allele A and q the frequency of allele a in the population, then the genotype frequencies are given by the expansion p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1, where p^2 is the frequency of AA individuals, 2pq of Aa heterozygotes, and q^2 of aa individuals. Since p + q = 1, the whole equation is the square of (p + q).
• WHY an equation about nothing changing is an evolution tool — it is the null model. If a real population's genotype frequencies match the equation, no evolution is occurring at that gene; if they depart from it, something — selection, migration, drift — is actively evolving it. Disturbed equilibrium is itself the signature of evolution.
• EXAMPLE — Suppose in a population the recessive allele a has frequency q = 0.3. Then p = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7, the aa genotype frequency is q^2 = 0.09, AA is p^2 = 0.49, and carriers Aa are 2pq = 2 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.42. Nine in a hundred show the recessive trait, but forty-two in a hundred silently carry the allele — that gap is why recessive traits hide in populations.
• WORKED PATTERN — The three-step routine for every Hardy-Weinberg sum: (1) get p and q first — if given the recessive phenotype frequency, that is q^2, so take the square root to get q; (2) p = 1 − q; (3) read off whichever genotype the question wants — p^2, 2pq or q^2. Most errors come from using the phenotype frequency directly as q. Root first, always.
• YOUR TURN — In an equilibrium population, 16 percent show a recessive trait. Find the percentage of heterozygous carriers. Answer: q^2 = 0.16 so q = 0.4 and p = 0.6; 2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.48 — 48 percent are carriers.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — q^2 is the frequency of the recessive GENOTYPE (and of the recessive phenotype), not of the recessive allele; q is the allele frequency. Confusing the two wrecked more Hardy-Weinberg answers than any arithmetic slip. Also, the principle assumes no disturbance — a population being selected is precisely a population where the equation need not hold.

When Equilibrium Breaks — The Five Disturbers
• DEFINITION — NCERT lists five factors known to disturb Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium — that is, to change allele frequencies and cause evolution: gene migration or gene flow, genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination, and natural selection. Memorise the five as one set; questions ask for the set and for each member's meaning.
• WHY five factors, not one — each moves allele frequencies by a different route: alleles migrate, luck reshuffles (drift), new alleles arise (mutation), old ones are recombined, and the environment filters (selection).
• DEFINITION — Gene flow: when a section of a population migrates to another population, gene frequencies change in both — new genes and alleles are added to the new population and lost from the old one. Repeated migration is gene flow.
• DEFINITION — Genetic drift is change in allele frequencies that happens purely by chance, not by advantage — strongest in small populations, where a single generation's luck can delete an allele entirely.
• DEFINITION — The founder effect: sometimes the change in allele frequency in a new population is so different that the drifted population, the founders, effectively becomes a separate sample of the original — and at times this leads to the formation of a new species. The original drifted population becomes the founding population; its skewed frequencies are the founder effect.
• EXAMPLE — A handful of finches blown by a storm to a new island carry only the alleles they happen to have. The island population starts with frequencies nothing like the mainland's — not because those alleles were better, but because the founders rolled those dice. That inherited skew is the founder effect in action.
• WORKED PATTERN — Distinguish the two chance-flavoured factors by direction of travel: gene flow is alleles moving BETWEEN populations (migration), tending to mix them; drift is frequencies wobbling WITHIN a population by chance, strongest when small, and the founder effect is drift's special case at colonisation. If the story has colonists, answer founder effect.
• YOUR TURN — A small group leaves a large beetle population and starts a colony where, by chance, a rare colour allele is common. Which factor is at work? Answer: the founder effect — a case of genetic drift in which the founding sample's skewed frequencies found a new population.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Genetic drift is not selection: drift does not favour the fitter, it favours the lucky, and it can eliminate even useful alleles in tiny populations. Also, "founder effect" is not a sixth factor — it is genetic drift wearing a colonist's hat.

Three Ways Selection Pushes — Stabilising, Directional, Disruptive
• DEFINITION — Natural selection, viewed through the distribution curve of a measurable trait, operates in three patterns. In stabilising selection, more individuals acquire the mean character value — the peak of the curve stays in the middle and grows taller and narrower, trimming both extremes.
• DEFINITION — In directional selection, the peak of the distribution shifts in one direction: more individuals acquire a character value other than the mean — one extreme is favoured, so the population's average itself moves, generation by generation. Industrial melanism is this pattern played on colour: the favoured extreme, dark, dragged the population average with it.
• DEFINITION — In disruptive selection, more individuals acquire the peripheral character values at both ends of the distribution curve: the middle is selected against, the curve splits towards two peaks, and the two extremes both flourish at the expense of the average.
• WHY the three curves matter more than three labels — a graph question gives no names, only a before-and-after distribution. Read the peak, not the caption: peak stays and sharpens means stabilising; peak slides sideways means directional; one peak becomes two humps means disruptive. The shape is the answer.
• EXAMPLE — Run the moth data through the curve lens: moth colour starts centred near pale; after soot, the favoured end is dark and the whole distribution slides dark-ward — the peak has shifted, so the selection was directional.
• WORKED PATTERN — Three questions to classify any selection graph: (1) Did the mean stay put? If yes, and variance shrank: stabilising. (2) Did the mean move? Directional. (3) Did the middle empty out while both tails grew? Disruptive. Answer in that order and the three patterns never blur.
• YOUR TURN — A bird population's beak-size curve, once single-peaked, becomes two-peaked: small beaks for small seeds and large beaks for hard seeds survive, mid-sized beaks do poorly. Which selection? Answer: disruptive — both peripheral values are favoured over the mean.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Disruptive selection does not mean the species is being destroyed; it means the middle loses. And stabilising selection is not "no selection" — it is active selection against both extremes, which is why the peak grows narrower and taller instead of merely sitting still.

A Brief Account of Evolution — The Long Timeline
• DEFINITION — The fossil record assembled into one narrative: about 2000 million years ago, the first cellular forms of life appeared on Earth, arising by a slow, gradual process presumably from non-cellular aggregates, in water. For most of Earth's story, life was single-celled and aquatic.
• WHY this timeline is evidence, not trivia — its order is never violated: no mammal sits in invertebrate layers, no fish precedes invertebrates. A sequence written in rock that life could not rearrange is what gradual change predicts.
• DEFINITION — The milestones in NCERT's order: by about 500 million years ago, invertebrates were formed and active. Jawless fish probably evolved around 350 million years ago. Sea weeds and a few plants existed probably around 320 million years ago. Animals and land came later than plants.
• DEFINITION — Some fishes of about 350 million years ago had stout, strong fins — the lobefins — that could move on land and go back to water; these were the ancestors from which the first amphibians evolved, animals that could live on land and in water. In 1938 a fish thought to be extinct, the coelacanth, was caught in South Africa — a living lobefin relative.
• EXAMPLE — Plants were among the first organisms to colonise land. Giant ferns of the pteridophyte grade grew into trees, and when they fell and were buried, they slowly formed coal deposits — today's coal is that ancient forest, fossilised.
• DEFINITION — Reptiles came after amphibians and laid thick-shelled eggs that, unlike amphibian eggs, did not dry up in the sun, freeing vertebrates from returning to water to breed. Some land reptiles — the ichthyosaurs — went back to water about 200 million years ago as fish-like reptiles.
• DEFINITION — The dinosaurs were the largest land reptiles — Tyrannosaurus rex was about 20 feet high with huge, fearsome dagger-like teeth — and land reptiles of about 200 million years ago were the dinosaur radiation. About 65 million years ago, the dinosaurs suddenly disappeared; proposed causes include climate change and a possible extraterrestrial collision.
• DEFINITION — Early mammals were small, shrew-like animals; mammals are viviparous and protect their unborn young inside the mother's body, and after the dinosaurs vanished, mammals spread. Continental drift then reshaped their history: when South America joined North America, its native mammals were overrun by northern fauna, while in isolated Australia the marsupials survived for lack of competition.
• WORKED PATTERN — Chain the anchor numbers into one rope: 2000 mya first cells; 500 mya invertebrates active; 350 mya jawless fish and lobefins; 320 mya sea weeds and plants; 200 mya ichthyosaurs and dinosaur rise; 65 mya dinosaurs disappear. A rope of six dates answers most ordering questions in this chapter.
• YOUR TURN — Which came first: jawless fish or sea weeds and plants? Answer: jawless fish, about 350 million years ago, before the roughly 320 million year mark given for sea weeds and a few plants — the numbers, not intuition, settle it.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The coelacanth was caught in 1938 off South Africa, and it is a lobefin fish, not a dinosaur or a reptile. And dinosaurs disappeared about 65 million years ago — students who write 200 million years are confusing their rise (about 200 mya) with their fall (65 mya).

The Human Story Begins — Dryopithecus to Australopithecus
• DEFINITION — About 15 million years ago, two primates named Dryopithecus and Ramapithecus were existing. They were hairy and walked like gorillas and chimpanzees — knuckle-walking, tree-and-ground apes of the forests, marking the region where the ape lineage and the human lineage begin to be distinguishable.
• DEFINITION — Of the two, Dryopithecus was more ape-like, while Ramapithecus was more man-like. That single comparison is NCERT's point about the pair and is asked directly; fix it as: Dryo- equals ape, Rama- equals man-leaning.
• DEFINITION — About 2 million years ago, Australopithecines — the australopithecus grade — probably lived in the East African grasslands. Evidence shows they hunted with stone weapons but essentially ate fruit; they were man-like primates about 3 to 4 feet tall who walked upright — bipedal posture arriving before big brains.
• WHY upright came first — the order matters: walking on two legs freed the hands for tools long before the skull expanded. Bipedal Australopithecus (2 mya) always precedes the big-brained Homo species in exam sequences; an option putting large brains first has it backwards.
• EXAMPLE — Picture the grassland scene: a 3 to 4 foot primate standing upright, stone in hand for hunting, fruit for most of its diet. Everything after this in the chapter — tools, meat, camps, art — builds on the two things visible here: stand up, and use the freed hands.
• WORKED PATTERN — The human-lineage ladder has four anchors: 15 mya Dryopithecus and Ramapithecus (ape-like, knuckle-walking); 2 mya Australopithecus (upright, 3-4 feet, stone weapons, mostly fruit); then Homo habilis; then Homo erectus; Neanderthal; Homo sapiens. Every ordering question is this ladder read up or down.
• YOUR TURN — A fossil primate is 3-4 feet tall, walked upright, and is dated about 2 million years ago in East Africa. Name the grade. Answer: Australopithecus (the australopithecines) — upright posture, small stature, East African grasslands.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — NCERT does not give a brain capacity for Australopithecus in this chapter — do not import numbers from other books into answers here. The capacities NCERT actually states begin with Homo habilis, in the next section; options assigning Australopithecus a fancy cc figure are bait.

Homo — Bigger Brains, New Species
• DEFINITION — The first human-like being was Homo habilis, the hominid whose fossils show brain capacities between 650 and 800 cubic centimetres. He probably did not eat meat.
[diagram fig_bio_25_6_human_evolution]
• DEFINITION — Homo erectus, about 1.5 million years ago, had a large brain of around 900 cubic centimetres and probably ate meat — the first of our lineage for whom meat-eating is part of the textbook description.
• DEFINITION — The Neanderthal man had a brain size of 1400 cubic centimetres — equal to the modern human range — and lived in Near East and Central Asia between 100,000 and 40,000 years back. Neanderthals used hides to protect their bodies and buried their dead, the first glimpses of culture in the fossil record.
• DEFINITION — Homo sapiens arose in Africa and moved across continents, developing into distinct races. During the ice age, between 75,000 and 10,000 years ago, modern Homo sapiens arose; prehistoric cave art developed about 18,000 years ago; agriculture came around 10,000 years back and human settlements started — civilisation is a blink at the end of the timeline.
• WHY the capacity ladder is the tested content — examiners reduce the human story to a matching exercise: species to brain size to one behaviour. Habilis 650-800, no meat; erectus 900, meat; Neanderthal 1400, hides and burials; sapiens, art and agriculture. Four rungs, four numbers, four behaviours — memorise them as one table and this whole section becomes retrieval, not reasoning.
• EXAMPLE — Notice the pattern inside the numbers: capacity roughly doubles from habilis to Neanderthal while the timeline compresses — the last few hundred thousand years change more than the millions before them, because culture becomes an evolutionary accelerator once brains and hands cooperate.
• WORKED PATTERN — A reliable memory hook for the capacities: habilis starts the count in the 600s (650-800), erectus stands at about 900, Neanderthal reaches 1400. Round numbers in ascending order — if an option offers erectus at 1400 or Neanderthal at 900, the rungs have been swapped and the option is wrong.
• YOUR TURN — Which species buried its dead and used hides, and what was its brain size? Answer: the Neanderthal man; 1400 cubic centimetres, living 100,000 to 40,000 years ago in Near East and Central Asia.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Homo erectus is the meat-eater of the pair; Homo habilis probably did NOT eat meat — options swap this routinely. And modern Homo sapiens arose during the ice age window of 75,000 to 10,000 years ago, with agriculture only about 10,000 years old — farming is not ancient on this scale.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Dates of the stage: Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago; life appeared about 4 billion years ago (500 million years later); first cellular forms about 2000 million years ago.
✔ KEY POINT — Oparin and Haldane: life arose from pre-existing non-living organic molecules, preceded by chemical evolution. Miller (1953) tested it: CH4, H2, NH3 and water vapour, electric discharge at 800 degrees Celsius, and amino acids formed.
✔ KEY POINT — Homologous organs: same structure and origin, different function — vertebrate forelimbs; Bougainvillea thorn and Cucurbita tendril. They record divergent evolution from a common ancestor.
✔ KEY POINT — Analogous organs: different structure and origin, same function — butterfly and bird wings; octopus and mammal eyes; penguin and dolphin flippers; sweet potato (root) and potato (stem). They record convergent evolution.
✔ KEY POINT — Embryology: Haeckel proposed the support (embryos share features adults lack, like gill slits); von Baer corrected it — embryos resemble other embryos, never adult stages of other animals. Biochemical evidence: proteins and genes similar across lineages.
✔ KEY POINT — Adaptive radiation: one ancestor, many niches — Darwin's Galapagos finches from a seed-eating stock; Australian marsupials from one ancestral stock. Placental-marsupial parallels: wolf with Tasmanian wolf, flying squirrel with flying phalanger, mole with marsupial mole.
✔ KEY POINT — Industrial melanism (Biston bitularia): collections of the 1850s held more white-winged moths; by 1920, after soot darkened bark and killed lichens, dark-winged moths dominated. Selection acted on pre-existing variation — directional selection.
✔ KEY POINT — Anthropogenic evolution: excess antibiotics, herbicides and pesticides select resistant strains within months or years. Evolution is a stochastic process based on chance events in nature and chance mutations in organisms.
✔ KEY POINT — The theorists: Lamarck — use and disuse, inheritance of acquired characters (giraffe's neck), rejected. Darwin — branching descent and natural selection (HMS Beagle, Galapagos). Wallace — Malay Archipelago, same conclusion independently. De Vries — evening primrose, large directionless mutations, saltation.
✔ KEY POINT — Hardy-Weinberg: allele frequencies stay constant at equilibrium; genotype frequencies follow p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1 with p + q = 1. The five disturbers: gene flow, genetic drift (including the founder effect), mutation, genetic recombination, natural selection.
✔ KEY POINT — Selection patterns: stabilising — more individuals acquire the mean value; directional — the peak shifts; disruptive — more individuals acquire peripheral values at both ends.
✔ KEY POINT — Timeline anchors: invertebrates active by about 500 mya; jawless fish about 350 mya; lobefins about 350 mya (coelacanth caught 1938, South Africa); ichthyosaurs and dinosaur rise about 200 mya; dinosaurs disappear about 65 mya; Tyrannosaurus about 20 feet tall.
✔ KEY POINT — Human ladder: Dryopithecus (ape-like) and Ramapithecus (man-like) about 15 mya; Australopithecus about 2 mya, East African grasslands, 3-4 feet, upright, stone weapons, mostly fruit; Homo habilis 650-800 cc, probably no meat; Homo erectus about 1.5 mya, about 900 cc, ate meat; Neanderthal 1400 cc, 100,000-40,000 years ago, hides and burials; Homo sapiens arose in Africa, modern form in the ice age 75,000-10,000 years ago; cave art about 18,000 years ago; agriculture about 10,000 years ago.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Saying life originated 4.5 billion years ago. Why it is wrong: 4.5 billion years is the age of the Earth; life appeared about 500 million years later. Correct principle: Earth 4.5 billion, life about 4 billion — keep the pair in that order.
• Mistake 2: Claiming Miller created life in his flask. Why it is wrong: he produced amino acids from inorganic gases; no cells, no proteins, no living system resulted. Correct principle: Miller proved the first step of chemical evolution, not the origin of an organism.
• Mistake 3: Classifying organs by function alone — calling the bat wing and butterfly wing homologous because both fly. Why it is wrong: homology is decided by structure and origin; wings of insect and bird differ fundamentally in plan. Correct principle: same origin, different function means homologous; different origin, same function means analogous.
• Mistake 4: Pairing the Bougainvillea thorn with a rose thorn as the NCERT example, or calling the Cucurbita tendril a leaf modification. Why it is wrong: the NCERT pair is Bougainvillea thorn with Cucurbita tendril, both stem modifications. Correct principle: thorn and tendril, both from stem, one defends and one climbs.
• Mistake 5: Writing that embryos pass through adult stages of ancestors. Why it is wrong: von Baer showed embryos never pass through adult stages of other animals; vertebrate embryos resemble each other's embryos and share embryonic features like gill slits. Correct principle: Haeckel proposed, von Baer corrected — cite the corrected form.
• Mistake 6: Saying soot turned moths black, or that moths darkened to match the trees. Why it is wrong: melanic variants pre-existed; pollution changed survival, not the moths themselves. Correct principle: variation first, selection after — the environment selects, it does not instruct.
• Mistake 7: Attributing antibiotic resistance to the drug creating it in exposed bacteria. Why it is wrong: resistant bacteria exist by chance mutation before exposure; the drug kills their competitors. Correct principle: anthropogenic evolution is selection on pre-existing variation, on a fast timescale.
• Mistake 8: Using the recessive phenotype frequency as q in Hardy-Weinberg sums. Why it is wrong: the phenotype frequency equals q^2, so q is its square root. Correct principle: root first — q equals the square root of the recessive genotype frequency — then p = 1 − q, then compute 2pq.
• Mistake 9: Listing the founder effect as a sixth factor separate from genetic drift. Why it is wrong: NCERT's five factors are gene flow, genetic drift, mutation, recombination and natural selection; the founder effect is the colonisation case of drift. Correct principle: five factors, and founders live inside drift.
• Mistake 10: Swapping the human-evolution facts: erectus for burials, habilis for meat-eating, or Neanderthal at 900 cc. Why it is wrong: burials and hides are Neanderthal (1400 cc); meat-eating is erectus (about 900 cc); habilis (650-800 cc) probably did not eat meat. Correct principle: 650-800 no meat, 900 meat, 1400 burials — the ladder in that order.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: In Miller's 1953 apparatus, the flask holds CH4, H2, NH3 and water vapour, a spark discharge runs for a week, and the trap liquid is analysed. What is found, and what exactly has been demonstrated?
• Given: Inorganic gases only; energy input by electric discharge at high temperature; no living material or organic chemical added.
• Principle: The experiment tests the first step of Oparin and Haldane's chemical evolution — can organic building blocks form from inorganic matter under early-Earth conditions?
• Steps: The gases mimic the reducing early atmosphere; the spark mimics lightning; condensation mimics rain into the primitive ocean. Analysis of the liquid reveals amino acids. No cells or proteins were produced.
• Answer: Amino acids formed; the demonstration is that organic building blocks of life can arise from inorganic gases under primitive-Earth conditions.
• Reasoning: The experiment's power is its controls — everything found inside was built inside, from gases alone.
• Mistake to avoid: Writing that Miller synthesised life, proteins or cells — the product is amino acids, full stop.
• Example 2 — Problem: Classify each pair as homologous or analogous, with one line of justification: (i) thorn of Bougainvillea and tendril of Cucurbita; (ii) eye of octopus and eye of man; (iii) flipper of penguin and flipper of dolphin.
• Given: Three organ pairs from the chapter's fixed cast.
• Principle: Apply the two-question test — origin and structure same or different; function same or different.
• Steps: (i) Both are modified stems (same origin), one defends and one climbs (different function): homologous. (ii) Both form images (same function) but are built on different internal plans (different origin): analogous. (iii) Both are paddles (same function), but one is a bird's forelimb-wing and the other a mammal's forelimb in separate lineages converging on swimming (different evolutionary origin of the paddle form): analogous.
• Answer: (i) homologous; (ii) analogous; (iii) analogous.
• Reasoning: The test never changes — only the pair does; run origin first, function second, every time.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling the flipper pair homologous merely because both are tetrapod forelimbs in deep ancestry — as organs shaped for the same swimming job in unrelated lineages, NCERT files them under analogy and convergence.
• Example 3 — Problem: In a moth population sampled in 1850, wing colours are mostly white with a few dark individuals. The same woods, sampled in 1920 after decades of factory soot, hold mostly dark moths. Explain the change in four linked steps and name the selection pattern.
• Given: Both colour forms present in 1850; bark darkened by soot and lichen loss by 1920; birds hunt by sight.
• Principle: Natural selection acting on pre-existing heritable variation, with predation as the agent and camouflage as the currency.
• Steps: Variation existed first: dark mutants were present but rare. The environment changed: soot killed lichens and blackened bark. Selection followed: white moths now stood out and were eaten disproportionately; dark moths survived and bred. Frequencies shifted: dark became the majority. Because one extreme was favoured and the population mean moved, the pattern is directional selection.
• Answer: Pre-existing variation, environmental change, differential predation, frequency shift — directional selection.
• Reasoning: All four links must appear; the first link (variation pre-exists) is what separates Darwin's explanation from Lamarck's.
• Mistake to avoid: Writing that the soot induced the dark mutation or that moths adapted by trying to match — selection has no intentions and no induction.
• Example 4 — Problem: In a large population at Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, the frequency of the recessive allele for a trait is 0.2. Calculate the frequencies of the homozygous dominant, heterozygous and homozygous recessive genotypes, and state how many carriers there are per 1000 individuals.
• Given: q = 0.2; population at equilibrium, so p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1 applies.
• Principle: Allele frequencies define genotype frequencies under random mating at equilibrium; heterozygotes are the carriers of a recessive allele.
• Steps: p = 1 − q = 0.8. Homozygous dominant p^2 = 0.64. Heterozygotes 2pq = 2 × 0.8 × 0.2 = 0.32. Homozygous recessive q^2 = 0.04. Check: 0.64 + 0.32 + 0.04 = 1.00. Carriers per 1000 = 0.32 × 1000 = 320.
• Answer: AA 64 percent, Aa 32 percent, aa 4 percent; 320 carriers per 1000 individuals.
• Reasoning: Notice how a rare recessive allele (0.2) still hides in nearly a third of the population as carriers — the arithmetic behind why recessive disorders persist.
• Mistake to avoid: Reporting 2pq as 0.16 by forgetting the factor 2, or using 0.04 as q instead of q^2.
• Example 5 — Problem: Twelve beetles from a mainland population are carried by a storm to an island. On the mainland a certain wing allele has frequency 0.1, but among the twelve founders it is 0.5. The island population descends entirely from these founders. Name the phenomenon, and explain why selection need not be involved.
• Given: A small founding sample whose allele frequency differs sharply from the source population; no migration afterwards.
• Principle: The founder effect — genetic drift at colonisation: the founders carry a random, unrepresentative slice of the gene pool, and all descendants inherit that slice.
• Steps: The founders are a sample of twelve, not a census; by chance the rare allele is over-represented among them. The island gene pool is built from the founders alone, so the skewed frequency becomes the new normal. No fitness advantage is required at any step.
• Answer: The founder effect (a case of genetic drift); allele frequencies changed by sampling chance at founding, not by selection.
• Reasoning: Whenever a question's story features a small colonising group and an odd frequency, reach for founder effect before selection.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling this gene flow — nothing is flowing between populations after the founding; the frequencies were set by the one-time sample.
• Example 6 — Problem: Match each placental mammal to its Australian marsupial parallel and name the evolutionary process the table demonstrates: wolf, flying squirrel, mole, anteater.
• Given: Four placental mammals, each with a marsupial counterpart occupying a similar niche in Australia.
• Principle: Unrelated lineages in similar niches converge in form — the pairs are matched by ecological role.
• Steps: Wolf (running pack hunter) pairs with Tasmanian wolf. Flying squirrel (glider) pairs with flying phalanger. Mole (burrower) pairs with marsupial mole. Anteater (ant-eating specialist) pairs with numbat. In each pair, resemblance tracks the niche, not recent kinship.
• Answer: Wolf–Tasmanian wolf; flying squirrel–flying phalanger; mole–marsupial mole; anteater–numbat; the process is convergent evolution (parallel adaptive radiation in isolation).
• Reasoning: The table is evidence because two separate radiations solved the same niche problems the same way.
• Mistake to avoid: Pairing by word similarity — the Tasmanian tiger cat pairs with the bobcat, not with the wolf; the Tasmanian wolf takes the wolf.
• Example 7 — Problem: Arrange these in evolutionary order and attach each one's signature fact: Neanderthal man, Homo habilis, Australopithecus, Homo erectus, Ramapithecus.
• Given: Five names from the human-evolution ladder, out of order.
• Principle: The sequence is fixed by dates and brain capacities: earlier forms are smaller-brained and more ape-like.
• Steps: Ramapithecus, about 15 million years ago, hairy and knuckle-walking, more man-like than Dryopithecus. Australopithecus, about 2 million years ago, upright at 3-4 feet with stone weapons and a mostly fruit diet. Homo habilis, brain 650-800 cc, probably no meat. Homo erectus, about 1.5 million years ago, brain about 900 cc, ate meat. Neanderthal man, brain 1400 cc, 100,000 to 40,000 years ago, used hides and buried the dead.
• Answer: Ramapithecus, Australopithecus, Homo habilis, Homo erectus, Neanderthal man.
• Reasoning: Order by the ladder anchors — 15 mya, 2 mya, then capacities 650-800, 900, 1400 — and every name falls into place.
• Mistake to avoid: Placing Homo erectus before Homo habilis, or giving Neanderthal the 900 cc figure — capacity order is the sequence check.

• Summary: The evidence interlocks — fossils in layered rocks, homologous organs recording divergence, analogous organs recording convergence, embryology corrected by von Baer, and molecular similarities — and the mechanisms evolved from Lamarck's acquired characters through Darwin and Wallace's natural selection to de Vries's saltational mutations.
• Summary: Populations are the arena: Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium defines no-change, five factors disturb it, selection acts in stabilising, directional and disruptive modes, and the human ladder — Ramapithecus to Australopithecus to habilis, erectus, Neanderthal and sapiens — is the final, most-tested table.

• Revision checklist — I can run the two-question test on any organ pair and correctly file the six NCERT pairs, including thorn-tendril and sweet potato-potato.
• Revision checklist — I can reproduce the seven marsupial-placental parallels by niche and explain why the table is evidence for convergence.
