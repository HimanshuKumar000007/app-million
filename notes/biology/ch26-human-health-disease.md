Introduction — Health Is More Than Not Falling Sick
• DEFINITION — Health, for a long time, was considered a state of body and mind where there was a balance of certain humours; the World Health Organisation defines it as a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being — not merely the absence of disease. Disease is the state in which functioning of one or more organs or systems is adversely affected, characterised by various signs and symptoms.
• EXAMPLE — Two students both feel "fine" this morning. One slept well, eats properly and is calm before the exam; the other has no fever but is anxious, exhausted and skipping meals. By the WHO definition, only the first is fully healthy — health has three dimensions, and disease is only one way to lose it.
• WHY this chapter is a scoring chapter — it is almost entirely classification and fixed pairings: every disease owns a pathogen, a type, a site and a signature, and every immunity term owns a definition with a named example. Examiners ask exactly those slots, so store the chapter as filled slots, not paragraphs.
• WORKED PATTERN — Build a five-slot card for every disease: pathogen, pathogen type, site in the body, route or vector, and signature sign or test. Read any disease question by asking which slot is being tested — the pathogen slot is the most frequently asked.
✔ KEY POINT — Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens and spread from person to person; non-infectious diseases like cancer do not. The second half explains immunity — and what happens when it is exaggerated (allergy), misdirected (autoimmunity) or destroyed (AIDS).
• YOUR TURN — A disease spreads from an infected person to a healthy person through contaminated water. Infectious or non-infectious? Answer: Infectious — it is caused by a pathogen and transmitted between people; cancer, the chapter's contrast case, never travels this way.

Health and Disease — The Two States of the Body
• DEFINITION — Good health, in the older view this chapter opens with, was thought to need a balance of humours, proper functioning of organs, and freedom from infection. The modern NCERT framing keeps the essentials: health is influenced by genetic disorders, infections, and lifestyle — including food and water, rest and exercise. When the functioning of one or more organs or systems is adversely affected, the state is disease, recognised by its signs and symptoms.
• EXAMPLE — The same infection can meet two different bodies: a well-rested, well-fed student shakes off a cold in three days, while an exhausted, underfed classmate stays ill for a week. The pathogen was identical; the state of health it landed on was not — which is why lifestyle sits beside infections in what health depends on.
• WHY the body usually wins — a healthy body is a defended body: pathogens must survive barriers, patrolling cells and, if they persist, a specific immune response with memory. Most infections are ended by this system without your noticing; disease is the visible minority of battles.
• WORKED PATTERN — When a question contrasts health and disease, anchor on function: health is organs and systems functioning normally within complete well-being; disease is functioning adversely affected, with signs and symptoms. Options that define health as "no pathogen present" are wrong — pathogens enter healthy bodies daily and are destroyed.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The immune system is the thread of the whole chapter: it explains why most infections fail, how vaccination works, why allergy and autoimmunity are friendly fire, and why AIDS, which destroys the system itself, is so deadly.
• YOUR TURN — Name the three influences on health that NCERT lists. Answer: genetic disorders, infections, and lifestyle — including the food and water we take, rest and exercise.

Meet the Enemy — Pathogens and Parasites
• DEFINITION — A wide range of organisms can cause disease in humans; such disease-causing organisms are called pathogens. Most pathogens are parasites — organisms that live in or on another organism, the host, and derive nutrition from it at the host's expense. The pathogen groups of this chapter are bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoans and helminths (worms).
• EXAMPLE — Five invaders, five biologies: Salmonella typhi is a bacterium, the rhinovirus a virus, Entamoeba histolytica a protozoan, Ascaris a helminth and Microsporum a fungus. Same job description — live off a human and cause disease — which is why no single medicine treats them all.
• WHY type comes before treatment — antibiotics act on bacterial machinery, so they cannot touch a virus or a fungus; anti-helminthic drugs target worm physiology. Every treatment question here is secretly a classification question: name the type correctly and the management follows.
• WORKED PATTERN — The figure with this section is your master grid: eight diseases with pathogen, type, site and signature in one panel. Learn it row by row, then test yourself column by column — all bacterial diseases together, all mosquito-linked diseases together.
✔ KEY POINT — Disease names in this chapter map one-to-one onto pathogens; there are no shared names and no shared pathogens. Typhoid is always Salmonella typhi; malaria is always Plasmodium.
• YOUR TURN — A pathogen lives inside the human intestine, feeds on the host's digested food and weakens the host. Is it a parasite, and which chapter group will it turn out to belong to? Answer: Yes, it lives off the host at the host's expense; intestinal worms like Ascaris are the helminth example — though Entamoeba, a protozoan, also works the gut, so site alone never fixes the type.

Typhoid — The Fever That the Widal Test Names
• DEFINITION — Typhoid fever is caused by Salmonella typhi, a pathogenic bacterium. It enters the small intestine through contaminated food and water and migrates to other organs through the blood. The patient suffers sustained high fever — 39 to 40 degrees Celsius — weakness, stomach pain, constipation and headache; in severe cases intestinal perforation and death may occur. Typhoid is confirmed by the Widal test.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT's famous case: Mary Mallon, nicknamed Typhoid Mary, was a cook who was a typhoid carrier — she stayed healthy while spreading the disease for years through the food she prepared. One carrier in a kitchen can seed an outbreak, which is why food-handler hygiene is a public-health rule.
• WHY the fever pattern is the giveaway — typhoid fever is sustained: it climbs and stays in the 39 to 40 degree band for days, unlike malaria's fever, which crashes and returns every three to four days. A temperature chart is genuinely diagnostic here, so examiners describe the pattern and expect you to read it.
• WORKED PATTERN — Fever questions sort by rhythm: sustained high fever plus abdominal symptoms plus Widal equals typhoid; cyclic chills every three to four days equals malaria. Match the rhythm first and the pathogen follows.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not let the word Salmonella pull you to food poisoning alone, and never assign the Widal test to any other disease. In this chapter Widal belongs to typhoid exclusively, exactly as ELISA belongs to HIV.
• YOUR TURN — A patient's blood test is reported as "Widal positive". Which disease is confirmed, and which bacterium causes it? Answer: Typhoid fever, caused by Salmonella typhi.

Pneumonia — Fluid Where Air Should Be
• DEFINITION — Pneumonia is a bacterial infection of the alveoli of the lungs. Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae are responsible: the alveoli get filled with fluid, leading to severe problems in respiration. Symptoms are fever, chills, cough and headache, and in severe cases the lips and fingernails turn grey to bluish. It spreads by inhaled droplets from an infected person, or by sharing glasses and utensils with one.
• EXAMPLE — Picture one alveolus as a tiny dry sponge of air. In pneumonia, bacterial growth fills that sponge with fluid — and a fluid-filled sponge cannot exchange gases. Thousands flooded at once is why the patient gasps and the lips, starved of oxygen, turn bluish.
• WHY two bacteria share one disease — pneumonia names a site and a condition — infected, fluid-filled alveoli — not a single species. NCERT therefore lists two responsible bacteria, and options offering only one as "the" cause are incomplete.
• WORKED PATTERN — Site is the separator among respiratory diseases: alveoli flooded means pneumonia; nose and passage inflamed without lung involvement means common cold. When a question mentions alveoli or bluish lips, pick pneumonia.
✔ KEY POINT — The other bacterial diseases NCERT names for outline recall are dysentery, plague and diphtheria — grouped with typhoid and pneumonia as the bacterial set of this chapter.
• YOUR TURN — A student shares a water bottle with a classmate who has pneumonia and falls ill a few days later with fever, chills and cough. Is the transmission route consistent with NCERT? Answer: Yes — sharing glasses and utensils with an infected person is an explicitly listed route, alongside inhaled droplets.

The Common Cold — A Virus in the Nose
• DEFINITION — The common cold is caused by a group of viruses called rhinoviruses. They infect the nose and the respiratory passage but not the lungs. Characteristic symptoms are nasal congestion and discharge, sore throat, hoarseness, cough, headache and tiredness, and the illness usually lasts three to seven days. Infection spreads through droplets from coughing and sneezing, and through contaminated objects such as pens, books, cups and doorknobs.
• EXAMPLE — A doorknob in flu season is a bus stop for viruses: an infected hand leaves droplets on it, your hand picks them up and touches your nose. The virus never needed to fly — contaminated objects do half its travelling, which is why handwashing breaks colds.
• WHY colds stay mild but unconquerable — rhinoviruses confine themselves to the nose and upper passage, never flooding the lungs, so the illness is miserable but brief. And because "rhinoviruses" is a group of many viruses, not one target, no single vaccine closes the question — NCERT's three to seven day course is the honest prognosis.
• WORKED PATTERN — Cold versus pneumonia is the chapter's favourite respiratory contrast: virus versus bacteria, nose versus alveoli, three to seven days versus potentially severe. Build the pair as one card and answer either direction from it.
★ HIGHLIGHT — NCERT also names dengue and chikungunya as viral diseases spread through mosquito vectors — the viral counterpart to malaria's mosquito story, and a favourite one-line recall question.
• YOUR TURN — A cold lasts five days with a blocked nose and sore throat but no breathing difficulty. Which structures are infected, and which are spared? Answer: the nose and respiratory passage are infected; the lungs are spared — that sparing is the definition-level fact about the common cold.

Malaria I — The Parasite and Its Two Hosts
• DEFINITION — Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, a tiny protozoan. Different species — P. vivax, P. malariae and P. falciparum — cause different types of malaria; the malignant malaria caused by P. falciparum is the most serious and can be fatal. Plasmodium enters the human body as sporozoites — the infectious form — through the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, the vector, which transmits the parasite from an infected person to a healthy one.
• DEFINITION — Why a vector at all? Plasmodium completes different parts of its life in two hosts — human and female Anopheles mosquito — and the mosquito is the bridge between them. She is also a host in her own right: fertilisation of the parasite happens inside her gut.
• EXAMPLE — A village pond breeds mosquitoes all monsoon. An infected traveller sleeps there without a net; a female Anopheles takes his blood and, days later, bites a child in the next house. The child never met the traveller — the mosquito introduced them. Every control measure in this chapter tries to cut that introduction.
• WHY only the female bites — female Anopheles mosquitoes need blood protein for their eggs; males feed on plant juices and never bite humans. That is why "female Anopheles" is not decorative wording in NCERT — it is the whole answer to which mosquito transmits malaria.
• WORKED PATTERN — Malaria questions rotate through four slots: species (falciparum most serious), vector (female Anopheles), infective stage for humans (sporozoite), and fever toxin (haemozoin). Fill all four in five seconds and no malaria question here can surprise you.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Malaria is protozoan, not viral or bacterial, however feverish it feels; and its vector is Anopheles, not Culex — Culex belongs to the filariasis story later in this chapter. Swapping these two mosquitoes is the single most repeated error in this chapter.
• YOUR TURN — Which Plasmodium species would worry a doctor most, and why? Answer: P. falciparum — its malignant malaria is the most serious type and can be fatal.

Malaria II — The Life Cycle, Stage by Stage
• DEFINITION — The cycle inside the human runs in two acts. Act one, the liver: sporozoites first infect liver cells and multiply within them. Act two, the blood: the parasites then enter red blood cells and multiply there, causing the RBCs to rupture; with every rupture a toxic substance, haemozoin, is released — and haemozoin is responsible for the chill and high fever recurring every three to four days.
• DEFINITION — For the cycle to continue, some parasites in the blood take a second path: sexual stages called gametocytes develop in the red blood cells. When a female Anopheles bites the infected person, these gametocytes enter the mosquito and develop further — fertilisation and development take place in the mosquito's gut. The mature infective sporozoites escape from the gut, migrate to the salivary glands, and enter the next human with her next bite. The figure draws both hosts.
• EXAMPLE — Follow one sporozoite: injected with saliva into a child's blood, it reaches the liver and clones itself quietly for days — no symptoms yet. Its descendants pour into red blood cells, multiply, and burst them in waves; each wave spills haemozoin, and the fever spikes with chills, falls, and returns three days later like clockwork.
• WHY the fever keeps time — red blood cells infected in the same wave mature and rupture in near-synchrony, so haemozoin hits the blood in pulses. A pulsed toxin makes a pulsed fever: chill and high fever, recovery, then the next pulse three to four days later. The rhythm is the life cycle made visible on a thermometer.
• WORKED PATTERN — Order questions list five events and shuffle them. Fix the anchors first — a human infection always begins with sporozoites infecting liver cells and the mosquito act always ends at the salivary glands — then sort the middle: RBC multiplication and rupture, gametocyte uptake, fertilisation in the mosquito gut.
✔ KEY POINT — Haemozoin is the fever molecule of this chapter: released from ruptured RBCs, it produces the chill and the recurring fever. If a question asks what causes malarial fever, the answer is haemozoin — not the sporozoite and not the gametocyte.
• YOUR TURN — A mosquito bites a malaria patient and, minutes later, bites a healthy person, who does not develop malaria. Why not? Answer: The mosquito took up gametocytes, not sporozoites; fertilisation and development must occur in her gut and sporozoites must reach her salivary glands before she can infect anyone — an instant second bite transfers nothing infective.

Amoebiasis, Ascariasis and Filariasis — The Worm and Gut Team
• DEFINITION — Amoebiasis, or amoebic dysentery, is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, a protozoan parasite of the large intestine. Symptoms include constipation, abdominal pain and cramps, and stools with excess mucus and blood clots. Houseflies act as mechanical carriers, carrying the parasite from the faeces of an infected person to food products; water and food contaminated by faecal matter are the main sources of infection.
• DEFINITION — Ascariasis is caused by Ascaris, an intestinal helminth. Symptoms include internal bleeding, muscular pain, fever, anaemia and blockage of the intestinal passage. The eggs of the parasite are excreted with the faeces of infected persons and contaminate soil, water and plants; a healthy person acquires the infection through contaminated water, vegetables and fruit.
• DEFINITION — Filariasis, whose gross chronic form is called elephantiasis, is caused by the filarial worms Wuchereria bancrofti and Wuchereria malayi, helminths transmitted through the bite of a mosquito vector — classically the female Culex. The worms live in the lymphatic vessels of the lower limbs, causing slowly developing chronic inflammation over the years; the genital organs are also often affected.
• EXAMPLE — Three patients, one clinic: the first has blood-flecked mucus in the stools after eating at a stall swarming with flies — Entamoeba. The second, a child with a blocked, painful intestine and anaemia — Ascaris. The third has one enormously swollen leg after years in a mosquito-dense district — Wuchereria. Site plus route names each one.
• WHY "mechanical carrier" is a precise term — the housefly changes nothing in Entamoeba; it is a flying vehicle, not a host, and no development happens inside it. Contrast the mosquito in malaria, inside whom the parasite must develop. Examiners test exactly this difference: mechanical carriage versus biological transmission.
• WORKED PATTERN — Gut-and-worm questions sort by organ: large intestine plus mucus-and-blood stools is amoebiasis; intestine plus blockage and anaemia is ascariasis; lymphatic vessels plus swollen limbs is filariasis. Learn the three as a row and the symptoms stop blurring together.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Filariasis is caused by a worm, not a virus, and its worms live in lymphatic vessels — not blood capillaries, not the intestine. And amoebiasis is protozoan despite producing a dysentery students reflexively file under bacteria.
• YOUR TURN — A patient has chronic swelling of one leg that developed over years. Name the disease, the genus of the pathogen, and the vessels it inhabits. Answer: Filariasis (elephantiasis), caused by Wuchereria, living in the lymphatic vessels.

Ringworm — The Fungus on the Skin
• DEFINITION — Ringworm is caused by fungi of the genera Microsporum, Trichophyton and Epidermophyton. The main symptoms are dry, scaly lesions on skin, nails and scalp, with intense itching. Heat and moisture help these fungi grow, so they thrive in skin folds such as the groin and between the toes. Ringworm is generally acquired from the soil or by using the towels, clothes or even the comb of an infected individual.
• EXAMPLE — A wrestler shares towels in a humid changing room and develops a circular, scaly, itching patch on the forearm. Every element of NCERT's description is present: fungus, skin site, heat and moisture helping it grow, and a shared towel as the route in.
• WHY a shared comb is a genuine route — fungal spores survive on objects, and scalp ringworm needs only contact with a contaminated comb to change heads. This is the chapter's fomite disease among the fungi, mirroring the common cold spreading through pens, books and doorknobs.
• WORKED PATTERN — Fungal questions are generous: if the options include Microsporum, Trichophyton or Epidermophyton, the disease is ringworm — no other chapter disease claims these three genera. Memorise the trio as one word-chain.
✔ KEY POINT — Ringworm completes the pathogen-type set: bacteria (typhoid, pneumonia), virus (common cold), protozoans (malaria, amoebiasis), helminths (ascariasis, filariasis) and fungi (ringworm). One disease per type is the minimum viable grid for this chapter.
• YOUR TURN — Itchy, dry, scaly patches appear between a footballer's toes during a wet season. Name the disease group and one condition favouring it. Answer: Ringworm, favoured by heat and moisture — the spaces between toes are exactly the moist skin folds NCERT names.

Prevention and Control — Breaking the Chain
• DEFINITION — Personal and public hygiene are very important for preventing and controlling many infectious diseases. Personal hygiene includes keeping the body clean and consuming clean drinking water, food and vegetables; public hygiene includes proper disposal of waste and excreta, periodic cleaning and disinfection of water reservoirs, and standard hygiene in public catering. For air-borne diseases, close contact with infected persons should be avoided.
• DEFINITION — For vector-borne diseases such as malaria and filariasis, the key measure is to control or eliminate the vectors and their breeding places: avoid stagnation of water around homes, clean household coolers regularly, use mosquito nets, introduce fishes like Gambusia in ponds to feed on mosquito larvae, spray insecticides in ditches and swamps, and fit doors and windows with wire mesh.
• EXAMPLE — A housing society applies the whole list in one monsoon month: cooler tanks scrubbed weekly, the blocked drain sprayed, a pond stocked with Gambusia, residents sleeping under nets. Dengue, chikungunya, malaria and filariasis all lose their breeding ground at once — one vector, four diseases weakened.
• WHY breeding places, not just bites — a mosquito population stands on its larvae. Adults live briefly, but stagnant water manufactures replacements daily; remove the nurseries and the population collapses. That is why NCERT's list is dominated by water management.
• WORKED PATTERN — Prevention questions are route-matching in disguise: food-and-water diseases draw hygiene answers, air-borne diseases draw contact-avoidance answers, and vector-borne diseases draw vector-control answers. Identify the route named in the stem, and the correct measure is the one that cuts that route.
✔ KEY POINT — Gambusia is the biological-control star of this chapter: a fish introduced into ponds to eat mosquito larvae — the only living control agent NCERT names here, and a favourite one-mark question.
• YOUR TURN — A town reports rising malaria every monsoon. From NCERT's list, name three measures aimed at the vector's breeding places. Answer: Any three of — avoid stagnation of water around homes, clean household coolers regularly, introduce Gambusia fishes in ponds, spray insecticides in ditches and swamps (and wire mesh on doors and windows keeps adults out).

Innate Immunity — The Four Barriers You Are Born With
• DEFINITION — Innate immunity is the non-specific defence present at the time of birth, accomplished by providing different types of barriers to the entry of foreign agents. It consists of four types of barriers: physical, physiological, cellular and cytokine. Innate immunity asks no questions and keeps no memory — it treats every invader the same way, from the first day of life.
• DEFINITION — Physical barriers: the skin is the main barrier, preventing entry of microorganisms, and the mucus coating of the epithelium lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal and urinogenital tracts traps microbes entering the body. Physiological barriers: the acid in the stomach, saliva in the mouth and tears from the eyes all prevent microbial growth. The figure with this section lays the four barriers out as four quadrants.
• DEFINITION — Cellular barriers: certain white blood cells — polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNL-neutrophils) and monocytes, and natural killer cells (a type of lymphocyte) in the blood, as well as macrophages in tissues — can phagocytose and destroy microbes. Cytokine barriers: virus-infected cells secrete proteins called interferons, which protect non-infected cells from further viral infection.
• EXAMPLE — One swallowed bacterium runs the gauntlet: saliva works on it in the mouth, stomach acid destroys most of its companions, and any survivor reaching the tissue meets a macrophage that engulfs it. Physiological barriers twice, then cellular — one invader handled without the body ever learning its name.
• WHY non-specific is a strength, not a weakness — a barrier that must first identify its enemy is slow; skin, acid and phagocytes act on contact, in the minutes when an infection is still a handful of cells. Specificity and memory are bought later by acquired immunity, at the price of days — the two systems trade speed for precision.
• WORKED PATTERN — Barrier classification is a four-way sort, and each type owns unmistakable examples: skin and mucus are physical; acid, saliva and tears are physiological; neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages and natural killer cells are cellular; interferons are cytokine. If an option places interferons among cellular barriers because cells secrete them, reject it — the barrier is the protein, not the cell.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Interferons protect non-infected cells; they do not kill viruses, and they are not antibodies. NCERT's sentence is exact: virus-infected cells secrete interferons which protect non-infected cells from further viral infection — quote it back whenever an option upgrades interferons into weapons.
• YOUR TURN — Tears wash a dust-borne bacterium off the eye's surface and inhibit its growth. Which barrier is this, and what is its NCERT category-mate in the stomach? Answer: A physiological barrier; its category-mate is the acid in the stomach (saliva in the mouth completes the trio).

Acquired Immunity — Active, Passive and the Two Responses
• DEFINITION — Acquired immunity is the pathogen-specific defence the body develops during its lifetime, and its defining feature is memory. The first encounter with a pathogen produces the primary response, of low intensity; when the same pathogen attacks again, the body produces a highly intensified secondary response — the anamnestic response — because the first encounter is remembered. The figure with this section plots the two responses as antibody level against time.
• DEFINITION — Active immunity is built by the host itself: when a host is exposed to antigens — living or dead microbes or other proteins — antibodies are produced in the host's own body. It is slow and takes time to give its full effective response; vaccination induces it deliberately. Passive immunity is ready-made protection: preformed antibodies are directly given against foreign agents, so the defence is immediate but borrowed.
• DEFINITION — Nature runs passive immunity twice in NCERT's telling. The yellowish fluid colostrum, secreted by the mother during the initial days of lactation, has abundant antibodies — IgA — to protect the infant. And the fetus receives some antibodies from its mother through the placenta. Both are passive immunity: the receiving body manufactured nothing.
• EXAMPLE — A snakebite victim and a vaccine recipient leave the same clinic "protected", but oppositely: the victim carries borrowed antibodies that work tonight and fade within weeks; the vaccine recipient's own cells are only beginning a slow build that protects for years. The difference is entirely about who made the antibodies.
• WHY the body bothers to remember — a first response takes days because the right lymphocytes must be found and multiplied; a remembered response skips the search. Memory is the mechanism that makes the second encounter cheaper than the first — and the entire basis of vaccination, which exists to manufacture memory safely.
• WORKED PATTERN — Classify by asking two questions: who manufactured the antibody — the protected person (active) or someone else (passive)? And was memory created — yes (active) or no (passive)? Colostrum, placenta and antivenom fail both tests for active; natural infection and vaccination pass both.
✔ KEY POINT — The two responses in one line: primary is low and slow because there is no memory; secondary (anamnestic) is high and fast because memory B-cells and T-cells answer the call. Every vaccine question is this line in disguise.
• YOUR TURN — A newborn is protected against infections in its first days mainly by antibodies it never made. Name the fluid, the antibody class, and the immunity type. Answer: Colostrum; IgA; passive immunity.

The Antibody — A Y With Two Hands
• DEFINITION — The acquired response is carried out by two types of lymphocytes in our blood: B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes. The B-lymphocytes produce an army of proteins into the blood to fight pathogens — these proteins are antibodies. The T-lymphocytes do not secrete antibodies but help the B-lymphocytes produce them. Each antibody molecule has four peptide chains — two small light chains and two longer heavy chains — so an antibody is H2L2, a Y whose two arm-tips bind antigen.
• DEFINITION — On classes, NCERT assigns addresses rather than essays. IgA is the antibody abundant in the mother's colostrum. IgG is the class the fetus receives through the placenta. IgE is the class produced in allergic responses. IgM is named in the antibody family alongside these — learn the three assigned addresses cold.
• EXAMPLE — Read the Y as a tool: the stem is the constant handle every antibody of a class shares, and the two fingertips are gripping ends shaped for one antigen. A B-cell population is a warehouse of differently shaped fingertips — which is why a response needs the right clone multiplied, and why that takes days the first time.
• WHY structure explains the response curves — because each antibody's binding sites fit specific antigens, the body cannot stockpile one universal antibody; it must select and expand the matching B-cells on first encounter. That selection is the delay in the primary response, and its absence — memory cells already expanded — is the speed of the secondary.
• WORKED PATTERN — Producer questions have one safe answer: antibodies are produced by B-lymphocytes; T-lymphocytes help. Options crediting T-cells with producing antibodies invert the NCERT sentence — memorise the sentence, not the vibes.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The antibody response, being carried in the blood, is called the humoral immune response — one of the two arms of acquired immunity, the other being the cell-mediated response of the T-lymphocytes. The figure with this section draws the H2L2 molecule and sets the classes side by side.
• YOUR TURN — An antibody is described as H2L2. Expand the formula and state which cells produce the molecule. Answer: Two heavy chains and two light chains — four peptide chains in all; antibodies are produced by B-lymphocytes.

Vaccination — Training the Army Without a War
• DEFINITION — The principle of immunisation or vaccination is based on the property of memory of the immune system. In vaccination, antigenic proteins of a pathogen, or inactivated or weakened pathogens, are introduced into the body. The antibodies produced would neutralise the real pathogen during actual infection, and the vaccine also generates memory B-cells and T-cells, which on later exposure recognise the pathogen quickly and overwhelm it with massive antibody production.
• DEFINITION — Two production details NCERT insists on. Using recombinant DNA technology, the hepatitis B vaccine has been produced from yeast — a vaccine grown in a fungus, not in the pathogen. And when a quick response is needed — as when a person is infected with deadly microbes requiring quick response, for example tetanus — preformed antibodies must be injected directly: that is passive immunisation, the antivenom logic, not vaccination.
• EXAMPLE — Think of vaccination as a fire drill. The building is never set alight; the occupants rehearse the evacuation until it is automatic. Months later, if a real fire comes, nobody reads the manual — the drill answers. A vaccine is the drill; the memory cells are the rehearsed occupants.
• WHY weakened or killed still teaches — the immune system learns shapes, not dangers: antigenic proteins alone carry the shapes needed to select B-cells and build memory. A pathogen need not be alive or dangerous to be recognised later — it needs only to display the right antigens once.
• WORKED PATTERN — Vaccine versus antivenom is the highest-yield contrast in this half of the chapter: vaccine delivers antigen, protection builds slowly, memory forms, immunity is active; antivenom delivers antibody, protection is instant, no memory forms, immunity is passive. Any option giving a vaccine "ready-made antibodies" has crossed the wires.
✔ KEY POINT — If a question asks why a vaccinated person survives an infection that hospitalises an unvaccinated one, the mechanism words are memory B-cells and T-cells and a massive, rapid secondary response — never "the vaccine's antibodies were still in the blood".
• YOUR TURN — A hepatitis B vaccine is described as "produced from yeast". Which technology does NCERT credit, and what does the vaccine introduce into the body? Answer: Recombinant DNA technology; it introduces antigen (antigenic preparation), not ready-made antibodies.

Allergy and Autoimmunity — Defence That Misfires
• DEFINITION — Allergy is the exaggerated response of the immune system to certain environmental antigens, called allergens — mites in dust, pollens, animal dander. The antibodies produced are of IgE type, and allergy is caused by the release of histamine and serotonin from mast cells. Symptoms include sneezing, watery eyes, running nose and difficulty in breathing; anti-histamine, adrenaline and steroids quickly reduce them.
• DEFINITION — Autoimmunity is the opposite targeting error: for genetic and other reasons still unknown, the body attacks its own self cells, resulting in damage. Rheumatoid arthritis, which affects many people in our society, is an auto-immune disease — the immune system treats the body's own joint tissues as foreign.
• EXAMPLE — Two misfires, two directions. In allergy, the system overreacts to something harmless from outside — pollen is not invading anyone, yet mast cells flood the nose with histamine. In autoimmunity, the target itself is wrong: the army fires on its own city. One is an excessive response, the other a mistaken identity.
• WHY histamine explains both symptom and cure — histamine from mast cells produces the swelling, mucus and constricted airways the patient feels; so an anti-histamine relieves symptoms at their source, and adrenaline and steroids counter the wider reaction. Symptom, chemical and cure form one chain.
• WORKED PATTERN — Allergy questions hinge on three fixed points: the antibody is IgE (not IgG, not IgA), the cells are mast cells, and the chemicals are histamine and serotonin. Autoimmunity questions hinge on one: self cells attacked, with rheumatoid arthritis as the named example. Keep the two misfires in separate boxes.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Modern-day lifestyle has lowered immunity and increased sensitivity to allergens, NCERT notes — more and more children in metro cities suffer from allergies and asthma. Do not invert this into "allergies are caused by weak immunity"; allergy is an exaggerated, over-strong response, not a weak one.
• YOUR TURN — A patient with pollen allergy is given an anti-histamine and improves within the hour. Which antibody class started the reaction, and which cells released the chemical the drug counters? Answer: IgE; the mast cells, which released histamine (and serotonin).

The Immune System — Organs of the Defence Force
• DEFINITION — The immune system consists of lymphoid organs, tissues, cells and soluble molecules like antibodies; it recognises foreign antigens, responds to them and remembers them, and plays an important role in allergic reactions, auto-immune diseases and organ transplantation. The lymphoid organs are where origin, maturation and proliferation of lymphocytes occur.
• DEFINITION — The primary lymphoid organs are where immature lymphocytes differentiate into antigen-sensitive lymphocytes. The bone marrow is the main lymphoid organ, where all blood cells, including lymphocytes, are produced, and where B-lymphocytes mature. The thymus is a lobed organ near the heart and beneath the breastbone, providing the micro-environment for T-lymphocytes to mature; quite large at birth, it keeps reducing with age until by puberty it is very small.
• DEFINITION — After maturation, lymphocytes migrate to the secondary lymphoid organs — sites where they interact with antigen and proliferate into effector cells. The spleen is a large bean-shaped organ of lymphocytes and phagocytes; it filters the blood, trapping blood-borne microorganisms, and holds a large reservoir of erythrocytes. Lymph nodes are small solid structures along the lymphatic system that trap antigens in lymph and tissue fluid, activating the lymphocytes there. Tonsils, Peyer's patches and appendix are other lymphoid tissues; lymphoid tissue lining the respiratory, digestive and urinogenital tracts is mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue, MALT — about 50 percent of the body's lymphoid tissue.
• EXAMPLE — A lymph node is a checkpoint on a highway: the lymph is stopped and inspected, and if antigen is found, the lymphocytes multiply on the spot. That is why nodes swell during infection — the checkpoint is at war.
• WHY two tiers of organs — maturation and battle are different jobs needing different buildings. Primary organs are schools, kept antigen-quiet so lymphocytes can mature; secondary organs are front lines, placed where antigens arrive — blood for the spleen, lymph for the nodes, gut and airway linings for MALT.
• WORKED PATTERN — Organ questions ask one of three things: where a cell type matures (B — bone marrow; T — thymus), what an organ traps (spleen — blood-borne microbes; nodes — lymph antigens), or the one number in the section (MALT is about 50 percent). Answer from that short list.
✔ KEY POINT — The thymus shrinking with age is a favourite one-liner: large at birth, very small by puberty — and it belongs to T-lymphocyte maturation, never to B.
• YOUR TURN — Which lymphoid tissue constitutes about 50 percent of the body's lymphoid tissue, and where is it located? Answer: MALT — mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue — within the lining of the major tracts: respiratory, digestive and urinogenital.

AIDS — The Virus That Attacks the Defenders
• DEFINITION — AIDS — Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome — is a deficiency of the immune system acquired during the lifetime of an individual, not a congenital disease; "syndrome" means a group of symptoms. It is caused by HIV, the Human Immunodeficiency Virus, a retrovirus with an envelope enclosing an RNA genome. There is always a time lag between infection and symptoms — usually five to ten years.
• DEFINITION — The replication mechanism is this chapter's centrepiece. After entering the body, the virus enters macrophages, where its RNA genome replicates to form viral DNA with the help of the enzyme reverse transcriptase. This viral DNA gets incorporated into the host cell's DNA and directs it to produce virus particles; the macrophages continue producing virus and so act like an HIV factory. Simultaneously, HIV enters helper T-lymphocytes and replicates there, and their number progressively decreases.
• DEFINITION — Transmission and diagnosis are equally fixed. HIV spreads through sexual contact with an infected person, transfusion of contaminated blood and blood products, sharing infected needles — as in intravenous drug abuse — and from an infected mother to her child through the placenta. Diagnosis is by ELISA. And the fact NCERT underlines: HIV does not spread by mere touch or physical contact; it spreads only through body fluids.
• EXAMPLE — Why does an AIDS patient die of infections others shrug off? Because the missing soldiers are the coordinators: with helper T-lymphocytes gone, diseases caused by Mycobacterium, viruses, fungi and even Toxoplasma take hold — organisms an intact acquired immunity holds in check for a lifetime. The figure with this section draws the whole mechanism.
• WHY the factory choice is so damaging — macrophages are long-lived patrol cells, not disposable targets. By converting them into virus factories and embedding its DNA in host DNA, HIV gains a permanent production line inside the system sent to destroy it — which is why the infection is not cleared, and why NCERT's practical conclusion is prevention.
• WORKED PATTERN — AIDS questions chain in one order: retrovirus and RNA genome, reverse transcriptase, viral DNA incorporated in macrophages, HIV factory, helper T-cell decline, opportunistic infections, ELISA for diagnosis. Learn the chain as a single sentence.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — There is no permanent cure for AIDS in NCERT's account: anti-retroviral drugs are only partially effective and can only prolong life, not prevent death. Any option claiming a cure, a vaccine, or spread by touch contradicts the text.
• YOUR TURN — A rumour claims a student caught HIV by sharing a classroom bench with an infected classmate. Correct the rumour with NCERT's rule. Answer: HIV spreads only through body fluids — sexual contact, contaminated blood, shared infected needles, or mother to child through the placenta; mere touch or physical contact does not spread it.

Cancer — Cells That Forget the Rules
• DEFINITION — Cancer is one of the most dreaded diseases of human beings and a major cause of death all over the globe. Its root is a lost property: in our body, cell growth and differentiation are highly controlled, and normal cells show contact inhibition — contact with other cells inhibits their uncontrolled growth. Cancer cells have lost this property, so they continue to divide, giving rise to masses of cells called tumors.
• DEFINITION — Tumors are of two types. Benign tumors remain confined to their original location and do not spread, causing only limited damage. Malignant tumors are masses of proliferating neoplastic cells: they grow very rapidly, invading and damaging surrounding normal tissues and competing with normal cells for nutrients. Cells sloughed from such tumors reach distant sites through blood and, wherever they lodge, start a new tumor there — metastasis, the most feared property of malignant tumors.
• DEFINITION — Causes and detection: agents that induce transformation of normal cells into cancerous ones are carcinogens — physical (ionising radiations like X-rays and gamma rays; non-ionising UV rays), chemical (tobacco-smoke carcinogens, a major cause of lung cancer) and biological (oncogenic viruses, carrying viral oncogenes). Normal cells also carry cellular oncogenes — c-onc or proto-oncogenes — which, when activated, can lead to oncogenic transformation. Detection rests on biopsy and histopathological study, blood and bone marrow tests (as in leukaemias), radiography, CT (X-rays, three-dimensional image), MRI (strong magnetic fields, non-ionising radiations), antibodies against cancer-specific antigens, and molecular techniques.
• EXAMPLE — A benign skin growth sits in one place for a decade, a tenant who never leaves his room. A malignant tumor breaks into neighbouring flats and sends associates across the city via the road network — the blood. Same origin story, opposite behaviour — and behaviour, not size, decides the class.
• WHY metastasis is the feared word — a confined lump can be excised because it has an address; metastasised cells have many addresses, most invisible. That is why treatment combines surgery with radiation, chemotherapy and immunotherapy — biological response modifiers such as alpha-interferon, which activate the immune system and help destroy the tumor — and why early detection dominates cancer outcomes.
• WORKED PATTERN — Cancer questions sort on one axis: confined or spreading? Confined, limited damage — benign. Invading, nutrient-stealing, blood-borne new tumors — malignant, and the spreading word is metastasis. A second axis separates the machines: CT builds its three-dimensional image with X-rays; MRI uses strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiations.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Contact inhibition belongs to normal cells; cancer is defined by its loss. Options that give cancer cells "strong contact inhibition" have the chapter's founding sentence exactly backwards.
• YOUR TURN — A tumor is removed from the colon, but months later tumors appear in the liver although the colon growth never touched it. Name the property and the route. Answer: Metastasis — cells sloughed from the malignant tumor travelled through the blood and lodged in the liver, starting a new tumor there.

Drugs and Alcohol — The Chemistry of Abuse
• DEFINITION — Opioids bind to specific opioid receptors in our central nervous system and gastrointestinal tract. Morphine, extracted from the latex of the poppy plant Papaver somniferum, is a very effective sedative and painkiller, useful after surgery. Heroin — smack — is a white, odourless, bitter, crystalline compound, chemically diacetylmorphine, obtained by acetylation of morphine; taken by snorting and injection, it is a depressant and slows down body functions.
• DEFINITION — Cannabinoids interact with cannabinoid receptors present principally in the brain. The flower tops, leaves and resin of Cannabis sativa yield bhang, marijuana, hashish and ganja; taken by inhalation or oral ingestion, they are known for their effects on the cardiovascular system. Cocaine, from Erythroxylum coca, interferes with the transport of the neurotransmitter dopamine and potently stimulates the central nervous system, producing euphoria and increased energy; excessive dosage causes hallucinations.
• DEFINITION — Tobacco contains nicotine, an alkaloid which stimulates the adrenal gland to release adrenaline and nor-adrenaline into the blood, both raising blood pressure and heart rate. Smoking is associated with cancers of the lung, urinary bladder and throat, bronchitis, emphysema, coronary heart disease and gastric ulcer; chewing raises the risk of oral cancer; smoking also raises blood carbon monoxide, reducing haemoglobin-bound oxygen.
• EXAMPLE — NCERT's prevention list is practical: avoid undue peer pressure; take guidance from parents and peers and help from medical professionals for stress; look for danger signs and alert parents and teachers; and if a person shows withdrawal symptoms, seek proper medical supervision, since abrupt unmanaged withdrawal can be dangerous.
• WHY addiction is a trap with a mechanism — adolescence (roughly 12 to 18 years) is when the first moves towards drugs and alcohol are commonly made: curiosity, adventure and excitement, experimentation, stress, peer pressure and media influence. Repeated use raises the tolerance level of the body's receptors, so ever higher doses are needed, and dependence sets in: abrupt discontinuation brings the withdrawal syndrome — anxiety, shakiness, nausea and sweating. Warning signs include falling academic performance, unexplained absence, neglect of hygiene, withdrawal, depression and changed sleeping and eating habits.
• WORKED PATTERN — Drug questions are source-and-signature matching: morphine and heroin point to Papaver somniferum; cannabinoids to Cannabis sativa; cocaine to Erythroxylum coca and dopamine; nicotine to the adrenal gland and adrenaline. Treat each drug as a labelled arrow from source to target, and no option can cross the arrows.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Heroin depresses and cocaine stimulates: the chapter's two headline effects run in opposite directions, and options swap them deliberately. Smack slows body functions down; coke speeds the central nervous system up.
• YOUR TURN — A drug is described as chemically diacetylmorphine, obtained by acetylation of morphine. Name it and its plant source of origin. Answer: Heroin (smack); the morphine it is made from is extracted from the latex of the poppy plant Papaver somniferum.

Key Points Sheet — The Whole Chapter on One Screen
✔ KEY POINT — Health (WHO): complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely absence of disease. Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens — bacteria, viruses, protozoans, helminths, fungi; cancer is the great non-infectious disease.
✔ KEY POINT — Typhoid: Salmonella typhi (bacterium); enters the small intestine via contaminated food and water, migrates through blood; sustained fever 39 to 40 degrees Celsius, weakness, stomach pain, constipation; confirmed by the Widal test; Mary Mallon is the classic carrier.
✔ KEY POINT — Pneumonia: Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae fill the alveoli with fluid; fever, chills, cough; lips and nails grey to bluish in severe cases; spread by droplets and shared utensils. Dysentery, plague and diphtheria are the other named bacterial diseases.
✔ KEY POINT — Common cold: rhinoviruses infect the nose and respiratory passage, not the lungs; congestion, discharge, sore throat, cough; lasts three to seven days. Dengue and chikungunya are the mosquito-borne viral diseases.
✔ KEY POINT — Malaria: Plasmodium vivax, malariae and falciparum (falciparum most serious); female Anopheles vector; sporozoites infect liver cells, then RBCs; RBC rupture releases haemozoin — chill and fever every three to four days; gametocytes taken up by the mosquito, fertilisation in her gut, sporozoites to her salivary glands.
✔ KEY POINT — Amoebiasis: Entamoeba histolytica in the large intestine; stools with mucus and blood clots; houseflies are mechanical carriers. Ascariasis: Ascaris in the intestine; eggs spread via contaminated soil, water and plants. Filariasis: Wuchereria bancrofti and malayi in lymphatic vessels, via mosquito bite (classically Culex); elephantiasis.
✔ KEY POINT — Ringworm: fungi Microsporum, Trichophyton and Epidermophyton; dry, scaly, itchy lesions on skin, nails and scalp; favoured by heat and moisture; spread from soil and the towels, clothes and combs of infected persons.
✔ KEY POINT — Prevention: hygiene against food-and-water diseases; distance from infected persons against air-borne ones; vector control against mosquito-borne ones — no stagnant water, insecticides in ditches and swamps, Gambusia in ponds, wire mesh on windows.
✔ KEY POINT — Innate immunity (from birth, non-specific), four barriers: physical — skin and mucus coatings; physiological — stomach acid, saliva, tears; cellular — PMNL-neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, natural killer cells; cytokine — interferons protecting non-infected cells.
✔ KEY POINT — Acquired immunity is pathogen-specific with memory: primary response low-intensity; secondary (anamnestic) response highly intensified via memory B-cells and T-cells. B-cells produce antibodies, T-cells help. Active: own antibodies after antigen — slow, lasting (vaccination). Passive: ready-made antibodies — immediate, brief; colostrum IgA, placental IgG.
✔ KEY POINT — Antibody: four peptide chains, two light and two heavy — H2L2 — a Y with antigen-binding sites at the arm tips. Class addresses: IgA in colostrum, IgG across the placenta, IgE in allergy; IgM is the fourth class named.
✔ KEY POINT — Vaccination introduces antigenic proteins or inactivated or weakened pathogens; the body makes antibodies and memory B-cells and T-cells. Hepatitis B vaccine is produced from yeast by recombinant DNA technology. Antivenom is passive immunisation, not vaccination.
✔ KEY POINT — Allergy: exaggerated response to allergens; IgE antibodies; mast cells release histamine and serotonin; sneezing, running nose, breathing difficulty; anti-histamine, adrenaline and steroids relieve it. Autoimmunity: body attacks self cells; example rheumatoid arthritis.
✔ KEY POINT — Lymphoid organs: primary — bone marrow (all blood cells produced, B-cells mature) and thymus (T-cells mature, shrinks with age); secondary — spleen (blood filter, erythrocyte reservoir), lymph nodes (trap lymph antigens), tonsils, Peyer's patches, appendix; MALT is about 50 percent of lymphoid tissue.
✔ KEY POINT — AIDS: HIV, a retrovirus with RNA genome; in macrophages, reverse transcriptase copies RNA into viral DNA, which joins the host genome — the macrophage becomes an HIV factory; helper T-lymphocytes progressively decrease; diagnosed by ELISA; spreads only via body fluids — sex, contaminated blood, shared needles, placenta — never by touch; no permanent cure.
✔ KEY POINT — Cancer: loss of contact inhibition; benign tumors stay confined, malignant tumors invade and metastasise via blood; carcinogens are ionising radiations and UV, tobacco-smoke chemicals, oncogenic viruses; normal cells carry proto-oncogenes (c-onc); detection by biopsy, blood counts, radiography, CT (X-rays, 3D) and MRI (magnetic fields, non-ionising); treatment includes surgery, radiation, chemotherapy and alpha-interferon.
✔ KEY POINT — Drugs: opioids bind receptors in the CNS and gut — morphine from poppy latex (Papaver somniferum), heroin is diacetylmorphine, a depressant; cannabinoids from Cannabis sativa, known for cardiovascular effects; cocaine from Erythroxylum coca interferes with dopamine transport; nicotine makes the adrenal gland release adrenaline and nor-adrenaline; dependence brings tolerance and withdrawal.

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1: Naming the male Anopheles, or Culex, as the malaria vector. Why it is wrong: only female mosquitoes take blood meals, and Plasmodium's vector is the female Anopheles; Culex is associated with filariasis. Correct principle: vector pairs are fixed — female Anopheles for malaria, Culex for Wuchereria.
• Mistake 2: Attributing the malarial fever to the liver stage or to gametocytes. Why it is wrong: the fever is produced by haemozoin, released when infected red blood cells rupture; the liver stage is silent and gametocytes only wait for a mosquito. Correct principle: fever equals RBC rupture plus haemozoin, every three to four days.
• Mistake 3: Swapping the Widal test and ELISA. Why it is wrong: Widal confirms typhoid; ELISA detects HIV infection. Correct principle: each flagship disease owns its test — typhoid owns Widal, AIDS owns ELISA.
• Mistake 4: Saying interferons kill viruses, or calling them antibodies. Why it is wrong: interferons are cytokines secreted by virus-infected cells that protect non-infected cells; they destroy nothing directly. Correct principle: interferons warn and shield the neighbours; antibodies are a separate weapon.
• Mistake 5: Calling the newborn's colostrum protection active immunity. Why it is wrong: the infant's own system produced nothing — the IgA was made by the mother and received ready-made. Correct principle: classify from the receiver's side; donated antibodies are passive immunity.
• Mistake 6: Making T-lymphocytes the producers of antibodies. Why it is wrong: antibodies are produced by B-lymphocytes; T-lymphocytes help the B cells. Correct principle: B makes, T helps.
• Mistake 7: Saying a vaccine injects ready-made antibodies. Why it is wrong: a vaccine introduces antigen — antigenic proteins or inactivated or weakened pathogens — and the receiver's own system manufactures the antibodies and memory cells. Correct principle: antigen in a vaccine (active); antibodies in an antivenom (passive).
• Mistake 8: Assigning allergy to IgG or IgA. Why it is wrong: the antibodies produced against allergens are of IgE type, acting through mast cells and histamine; IgA belongs to colostrum and IgG to the placenta story. Correct principle: allergy runs on IgE.
• Mistake 9: Claiming HIV spreads by touch, hugging or sharing meals. Why it is wrong: NCERT states HIV spreads only through body fluids — sexual contact, contaminated blood, shared infected needles, and mother to child through the placenta. Correct principle: no body fluid, no transmission.
• Mistake 10: Allowing a benign tumor to metastasise, or crediting cancer cells with contact inhibition. Why it is wrong: benign tumors remain confined; metastasis defines malignant tumors, and cancer arises because contact inhibition is lost. Correct principle: confined is benign, spreading is malignant.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 — Problem: A patient has run a sustained fever of 39 to 40 degrees Celsius for a week, with weakness, stomach pain and constipation. A Widal test comes back positive. Name the disease, the pathogen and its type, and the route of entry.
• Given: Sustained high fever in the 39 to 40 degree band; abdominal symptoms; positive Widal test.
• Principle: Typhoid is the only disease in this chapter confirmed by the Widal test, and its pathogen enters through contaminated food and water.
• Steps: The test name identifies the disease: Widal means typhoid. Its pathogen is Salmonella typhi, a bacterium, entering the small intestine through contaminated food and water and migrating through the blood.
• Answer: Typhoid, caused by the bacterium Salmonella typhi, acquired through contaminated food and water.
• Reasoning: The signature slot — the named test — is decisive on its own, and the sustained fever pattern and intestinal symptoms agree.
• Mistake to avoid: Choosing malaria because the fever is high — malaria recurs every three to four days with chills and is not Widal-positive.
• Example 2 — Problem: Arrange in order: (a) sporozoites reach the mosquito's salivary glands; (b) gametocytes are taken up by a female Anopheles; (c) sporozoites infect liver cells; (d) RBCs rupture, releasing haemozoin; (e) fertilisation occurs in the mosquito's gut.
• Given: Five events spanning both hosts, scrambled.
• Principle: The cycle runs human liver, then human blood, then mosquito gut, then mosquito salivary glands.
• Steps: In the human: sporozoites infect liver cells (c) first. Then RBCs multiply and rupture with haemozoin release (d). Gametocytes are taken up by the mosquito (b). In the mosquito: fertilisation in the gut (e) precedes sporozoites reaching the salivary glands (a).
• Answer: c, d, b, e, a.
• Reasoning: Anchor the ends — a human infection opens with liver cells and closes, for the next victim, at the salivary glands — then sort the middle by host.
• Mistake to avoid: Placing fertilisation in human blood; it is strictly a mosquito-gut event following gametocyte uptake.
• Example 3 — Problem: Assign each event to its barrier of innate immunity: (i) stomach acid destroys swallowed bacteria; (ii) a macrophage engulfs a bacterium in tissue fluid; (iii) skin blocks a fungus spore; (iv) a virus-infected cell releases a protein protecting its uninfected neighbours.
• Given: Four events, one of each barrier type.
• Principle: Physical barriers block or trap; physiological barriers are chemical secretions; cellular barriers are phagocytic or killer cells; cytokine barriers are signalling proteins from infected cells.
• Steps: (i) Acid is a chemical secretion — physiological. (ii) A macrophage eating a microbe — cellular. (iii) Skin blocking entry — physical. (iv) The protein is interferon — cytokine.
• Answer: (i) physiological, (ii) cellular, (iii) physical, (iv) cytokine.
• Reasoning: Ask what does the work: a surface (physical), a chemical (physiological), a living cell (cellular), or a messenger protein (cytokine).
• Mistake to avoid: Calling stomach acid physical because the stomach is an organ — classification follows the mechanism, not the anatomy.
• Example 4 — Problem: Classify as active or passive immunity: (a) a baby receives IgA in colostrum; (b) a traveller is vaccinated with hepatitis B antigen produced in yeast; (c) a snakebite victim receives antivenom; (d) a fetus receives IgG through the placenta.
• Given: Four stories differing in who made the antibody and what was administered.
• Principle: If the protected person's own system manufactured the antibodies after meeting antigen, it is active; if ready-made antibodies were supplied, passive.
• Steps: (a) Mother-made IgA, received ready-made — passive. (b) The traveller's own system responds to antigen and builds memory — active. (c) Antivenom is preformed antibody — passive. (d) Mother-made IgG crossing the placenta — passive.
• Answer: (a) passive, (b) active, (c) passive, (d) passive.
• Reasoning: One test sorts all four: whose B-lymphocytes did the work? Only in the vaccination case was it the protected person's own.
• Mistake to avoid: Calling the vaccine passive because it was "given" by injection — what matters is whether antigen or antibody was delivered.
• Example 5 — Problem: A child exposed to a pathogen in March falls mildly ill; antibody levels rise slowly and stay low. The same pathogen enters in November; antibody levels shoot up rapidly to a far higher peak and the child stays well. Explain both observations.
• Given: First exposure — slow, low rise with mild illness. Second — rapid, high rise, no illness.
• Principle: The first encounter produces the low-intensity primary response and generates memory B-cells and T-cells; re-encounter produces the highly intensified secondary (anamnestic) response powered by that memory.
• Steps: March: no memory exists, so lymphocytes must be selected and multiplied from scratch — slow, weak, and the pathogen gets a head start; but memory cells are banked. November: memory cells respond at once, antibody production is massive and fast, and the pathogen is neutralised before symptoms.
• Answer: March shows the primary response; November the secondary or anamnestic response — faster and stronger because of immunological memory.
• Reasoning: This is the whole basis of vaccination: arrange the first encounter safely, and the real encounter meets the second, stronger response.
• Mistake to avoid: Crediting innate immunity with learning — innate barriers do not remember; memory defines acquired immunity.
• Example 6 — Problem: An HIV-positive man's helper T-lymphocyte count has fallen steadily for years. He now suffers repeated infections by Mycobacterium, fungi and Toxoplasma, organisms he once controlled easily. Trace the chain from viral entry into a macrophage to this state.
• Given: HIV infection; progressive helper T-cell decline; opportunistic infections.
• Principle: HIV replicates in macrophages (a continuing HIV factory) and in helper T-lymphocytes, whose numbers progressively decrease; helper T cells coordinate the acquired response, so their loss produces immunodeficiency.
• Steps: Step 1: HIV enters macrophages; reverse transcriptase copies its RNA genome into viral DNA. Step 2: The viral DNA is incorporated into the host DNA and directs production of new virus particles — the macrophage becomes a factory. Step 3: HIV also replicates in helper T-lymphocytes, so their count falls yearly. Step 4: With the coordinators gone, responses to other microbes fail and opportunistic organisms cause disease.
• Answer: The man has AIDS: viral replication centred on macrophages and helper T cells has dismantled his acquired immunity.
• Reasoning: Every link is checkable — enzyme, factory cell, declining cell type, consequence — which is why examiners ask this mechanism as a chain.
• Mistake to avoid: Saying HIV kills by destroying red blood cells — the lethal agents are the opportunistic infections that arrive after helper T cells fall.
• Example 7 — Problem: One tumor has remained at its original site for years causing limited damage. Another, initially in one organ, is later found as new growths in distant organs reached through the blood. Classify both, name the property shown by the second, and state which imaging method uses strong magnetic fields.
• Given: Tumor one — confined, limited damage. Tumor two — distant growths via blood.
• Principle: Benign tumors remain confined; malignant tumors invade and metastasise through blood to distant sites. MRI uses strong magnetic fields and non-ionising radiations; CT uses X-rays.
• Steps: Tumor one fits the benign pattern: confined, limited damage. Tumor two shows spread through blood — metastasis, the property of malignant tumors. The magnetic-field imaging method is MRI, not CT.
• Answer: The first tumor is benign; the second is malignant and shows metastasis; the imaging method is MRI.
• Reasoning: Two axes decide everything — confinement versus spread for the tumors, radiation type for the machines.
• Mistake to avoid: Assigning X-rays to MRI — X-ray-based three-dimensional imaging is CT by definition.

• A5. Because the first encounter is remembered: memory B-cells and T-cells generated during the primary response recognise the same pathogen on re-entry and mount a massive, rapid antibody production. Its other name is the anamnestic response. Misconception watch: the primary response is the low-intensity one; a "slow secondary response" inverts the graph.
• A6. Passive immunity — the antivenom supplies ready-made antibodies, so protection is immediate, but no memory cells form and the borrowed antibodies are soon cleared. • A7. HIV spreads through sexual contact with an infected person and through transfusion of contaminated blood (also acceptable: sharing infected needles; mother to child through the placenta). • A8. Misconception watch: metastasis is performed only by malignant tumors; a growth that has metastasised is malignant by definition, whatever its size.
