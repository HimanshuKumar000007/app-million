Introduction — One Oxygen, Three Families
• Here is the puzzle this chapter solves: take three liquids, all built only from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. Ethanol fizzes gently with sodium metal. Phenol dissolves quietly in aqueous NaOH, as if it were an acid. Diethyl ether ignores both sodium and NaOH completely. One element — oxygen — three totally different personalities. The difference is not the oxygen itself but the company it keeps: what it is attached to decides everything it does.
• DEFINITION — An alcohol carries its OH group on an sp3 carbon, as in CH3-CH2-OH. A phenol carries its OH group directly on an aromatic ring carbon, as in C6H5-OH. An ether has no OH at all: its oxygen bridges two carbon groups, C-O-C, as in CH3-O-CH3 or CH3CH2-O-CH2CH3. Spot which of the three you are holding and half the chapter's questions answer themselves.
• WHY this chapter pays rent in the IAT — it is conversion and test territory. Alkenes become alcohols by two opposite routes, alcohols become carbonyls by a ladder with different top rungs for each class, phenols announce themselves with bromine water and a pKa ladder, and ethers are won or lost at the design stage of one reaction, the Williamson synthesis. Almost every question is a product prediction, an acidity order, or a "which reagent, which test" discrimination.
• EXAMPLE — The three families at work around you: ethanol in sanitisers and fuel blends, methanol in industrial solvents, phenol derivatives in antiseptics (the active ingredient of Dettol, chloroxylenol, is a substituted phenol), ethoxyethane (diethyl ether) as the classic first anaesthetic, and glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) in everything from cough syrups to cosmetics.
• The map of the chapter: classify alcohols and feel their physical properties, build them two ways (from alkenes, then from carbonyls), measure their acidity, watch them react (HX, Lucas, dehydration), climb the oxidation ladder, then cross to phenols — preparation, the acidity ladder, ring substitution, Kolbe and Reimer-Tiemann — and close with ethers: Williamson design, cleavage by HX, and the anisole ring.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The whole chapter in one breath: find what the oxygen is attached to. An sp3 carbon means alcohol rules (class 1, 2 or 3 degree sets the ladder heights), a ring carbon means phenol rules (resonance runs acidity and substitution), and two carbons means ether rules (design the Williamson pairing, and remember the aryl-oxygen bond never breaks).

Alcohols — Classification and Physical Properties
• DEFINITION — By OH count, alcohols are monohydric (one OH, as in ethanol), dihydric (two OH groups, as in ethane-1,2-diol, the antifreeze glycol) or trihydric (three, as in propane-1,2,3-triol, glycerol). By the carbon carrying the OH — the carbinol carbon — an alcohol is primary (1 degree) if that carbon is attached to one other carbon, secondary (2 degree) if attached to two, and tertiary (3 degree) if attached to three.
• DEFINITION — Special addresses matter: an allylic alcohol has OH on an sp3 carbon beside a C=C bond, a benzylic alcohol has it beside a benzene ring, and a phenolic OH sits directly on the ring (that is phenol itself, next family). An OH on a double-bond carbon would be an enol, and vinyl alcohol is so unstable it rearranges at once to ethanal — which is why "vinylic alcohol" is essentially a label for a compound that refuses to exist.
• EXAMPLE — Classify these: CH3-CH2-OH is primary; CH3-CH(OH)-CH3 (propan-2-ol) is secondary; (CH3)3C-OH (2-methylpropan-2-ol) is tertiary; C6H5-CH2-OH is benzylic and primary; cyclohexanol is secondary. The class label you assign here is the same label that will set the oxidation ladder and the Lucas stopwatch later.
• WHY alcohols boil so high — between alcohol molecules there are hydrogen bonds: the H of one OH is attracted to the O of the next, and breaking that network costs serious thermal energy. Compare three molecules of similar size: ethane boils at 184 K, ethoxyethane at 308 K, and ethanol at 351 K. Ethanol beats even its ether isomer family because ether oxygens have no OH hydrogen of their own to donate into hydrogen bonds.
• EXAMPLE — Branching lowers the boiling point by shrinking surface contact, exactly as with the haloalkanes of Chapter 15: butan-1-ol boils at 391 K, butan-2-ol at about 372 K, and the compact, near-spherical 2-methylpropan-2-ol at about 355 K. More OH groups push the other way: glycerol, with three OH groups per molecule, is a viscous syrup boiling near 563 K.
• WHY solubility fades along the series — the OH head loves water (it hydrogen-bonds to water molecules), but the alkyl tail hates it. Methanol, ethanol and propan-1-ol, with short tails, are miscible with water in all proportions; by butan-1-ol the tail is winning and only about 8 g dissolves in 100 mL of water; longer-chain alcohols are effectively insoluble. Solubility is a tug-of-war between one OH and an ever-longer hydrocarbon tail.
• WORKED PATTERN — Predict any physical property in two questions: (1) Can it hydrogen-bond with itself? Alcohols yes, ethers no — that single answer orders boiling points of isomers. (2) How big and how branched is the carbon skeleton? Bigger raises boiling point, branching lowers it, and a bigger tail always cuts water solubility.
• YOUR TURN — (a) Arrange by boiling point: ethanol, propan-1-ol, butan-1-ol. (b) Which is more soluble in water, butan-1-ol or pentan-1-ol? Answers: (a) butan-1-ol highest, then propan-1-ol, then ethanol — same family, bigger molecule, stronger van der Waals grip. (b) Butan-1-ol — the shorter hydrophobic tail loses the tug-of-war less badly.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Ethoxyethane and butan-1-ol are functional isomers (same formula C4H10O) with a boiling-point gap of over 80 K: 308 K versus 391 K. Options that treat isomer boiling points as similar are wrong whenever one isomer can hydrogen-bond and the other cannot.

Preparing Alcohols I — From Alkenes, Two Opposite Forks
• DEFINITION — Acid-catalysed hydration adds water across a C=C double bond: alkene + H2O, with H3PO4 or dilute H2SO4 as catalyst, gives an alcohol, and the OH lands on the MORE substituted carbon — Markovnikov addition. Industrially, ethene and steam pass over phosphoric acid at 573 K and 60 to 70 atmospheres to make ethanol.
• WHY Markovnikov here — the mechanism walks through a carbocation, in three steps: Step 1, the pi bond grabs a proton and the cation forms on the more substituted carbon (the more stable one). Step 2, a water molecule attacks the cation. Step 3, the protonated alcohol loses a proton to another water molecule, regenerating the acid catalyst. Because a real carbocation exists, rearrangements can ambush unsymmetrical cases — the same hydride-shift logic as Chapter 15.
• EXAMPLE — Propene hydrates to propan-2-ol (OH on the middle carbon, through the secondary cation), and 2-methylpropene hydrates straight to 2-methylpropan-2-ol through its tertiary cation. In both cases the OH address was chosen by cation stability, not by where the proton first landed.
• DEFINITION — Hydroboration-oxidation is the mirror route: Step 1, the alkene reacts with diborane, B2H6 (in THF), and boron adds to the LESS substituted carbon of the double bond while hydrogen adds to the other. Step 2, oxidation with alkaline hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, NaOH) replaces the boron by OH, in the same position, with the same stereochemistry. The net result is water added across the double bond the anti-Markovnikov way, with no carbocation anywhere.
• WHY boron picks the less crowded carbon — the addition is concerted through a four-centre transition state, and boron, the larger and more electropositive partner, settles where there is more room and more partial positive character suits hydrogen. Sterics and electronics point the same way: B to the less substituted carbon, so OH finishes there after oxidation.
• WORKED PATTERN — One alkene, two alcohols, reagent is the switch: propene with H2O and acid gives propan-2-ol; propene with (i) B2H6, (ii) H2O2 and NaOH gives propan-1-ol. Whenever a question shows the same alkene giving two different alcohols, look for this exact fork — the products are the mechanism's signature.
• YOUR TURN — Predict both ways for but-1-ene, CH2=CH-CH2-CH3: (a) dilute acid, heat; (b) hydroboration-oxidation. Answers: (a) butan-2-ol — the secondary cation forms, water captures it. (b) butan-1-ol — boron, then OH, takes the terminal carbon. Same starting alkene, regioisomeric alcohols, chosen purely by reagent.
✔ KEY RULE — The hydration fork: H2O with acid catalyst -> Markovnikov alcohol (through a carbocation, rearrangement possible). (i) B2H6, (ii) H2O2/NaOH -> anti-Markovnikov alcohol (concerted, syn addition, no rearrangement). Learn the fork as a pair; examiners always test both arms.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — A rearranged alcohol can only come from the acid route. If hydroboration-oxidation appears in a question, delete every rearranged product from your thinking: no cation forms, so the skeleton cannot shuffle. Conversely, if the acid-hydration product in the options keeps an unlikely secondary skeleton next to a tertiary neighbour, check for the shift.

Preparing Alcohols II — Grignard, Reduction and Hydrolysis
• DEFINITION — Grignard reagents (Chapter 15) add to the carbonyl carbon of aldehydes and ketones: the nucleophilic alkyl group bonds to the carbon, the pi electrons move onto oxygen, and an acid workup protonates the resulting alkoxide to the alcohol. The carbonyl carbon keeps its oxygen and gains a whole alkyl group — the chain grows.
✔ KEY RULE — The Grignard table, the most productive five lines in alcohol synthesis: methanal (formaldehyde, CH2O) + R-MgX gives a PRIMARY alcohol (R-CH2-OH). Any other aldehyde (R-CHO) gives a SECONDARY alcohol. A ketone gives a TERTIARY alcohol. An ester, with two equivalents of Grignard reagent, gives a TERTIARY alcohol carrying two identical groups from the reagent. Ethylene oxide (oxirane) opens to give a PRIMARY alcohol extended by exactly two carbons.
• EXAMPLE — One reagent, three alcohols: CH3MgBr with methanal gives ethanol; CH3MgBr with ethanal gives propan-2-ol; CH3MgBr with propanone gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol. Same methyl group delivered each time — what changes is how many carbons the carbonyl partner already had around its C=O carbon, and that count is the class of the product.
• DEFINITION — Direct reduction also climbs down from carbonyls: catalytic hydrogenation (H2 with Ni or Pt) or the hydride reagents NaBH4 and LiAlH4 convert aldehydes to primary alcohols and ketones to secondary alcohols. Carbonyl reductions are the workhorse route when the alcohol you want has exactly the skeleton of an available aldehyde or ketone.
• WHY the hydride choice matters — NaBH4 is mild and choosy: it reduces aldehydes and ketones but leaves carboxylic acids and esters untouched. LiAlH4 is the strong, unselective one: it reduces acids all the way to primary alcohols (ethanoic acid -> ethanol) and esters to TWO alcohols, cleaving at the acyl-oxygen bond (methyl propanoate gives propan-1-ol from the acid side plus methanol from the alkoxy side). Examiners set this fork constantly: the reagent name in the question decides how far the reduction runs.
• DEFINITION — Hydrolysis of haloalkanes closes the triangle from Chapter 15: aqueous KOH or NaOH converts R-X to R-OH, by SN2 for primary halides and SN1 (with rearrangement and elimination competition) for secondary and tertiary ones. Moist silver oxide, Ag2O, does the same swap more gently. This route is real but rarely the best design choice — it is the reverse of the HX reaction coming in Section 5.
• WORKED PATTERN — Reverse-engineer any target alcohol: to build a tertiary alcohol, split it mentally into a ketone plus a Grignard reagent (three ways, one per alkyl group). To extend a chain by two carbons ending in a primary OH, reach for ethylene oxide plus a Grignard. To keep the skeleton untouched, reduce the matching carbonyl — and pick LiAlH4 if the starting material is an acid or ester, NaBH4 if it is an aldehyde or ketone.
• YOUR TURN — 2-Methylbutan-2-ol, (CH3)2C(OH)-CH2-CH3, can be made two Grignard ways: name both carbonyl partners. Answers: (a) propanone + ethylmagnesium bromide (the ethyl group arrives with the reagent), (b) butan-2-one + methylmagnesium bromide (the methyl group arrives instead). Any of the three groups on the carbinol carbon can be the delivered one — that freedom is what makes Grignard planning so powerful.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two assassins from Chapter 15 still guard this route: a Grignard reagent is destroyed by any proton source, so the carbonyl partner must not carry OH, COOH or NH groups of its own; and count carbons before you commit — the product skeleton is carbonyl skeleton plus the reagent's R group, and options that keep the starting carbon count are bait.

Acidity of Alcohols — Weak Acids with a Famous Order
• DEFINITION — Alcohols are very weak acids: R-OH can lose its OH proton to a strong enough base, leaving an alkoxide ion, R-O-. Their pKa values sit near water's: methanol about 15.5, water 15.7, ethanol about 16, and larger alcohols higher still. So alcohols are comparable to water in acidity, most of them slightly weaker — which is why sodium ethoxide dropped into water grabs a proton and becomes ethanol plus hydroxide: the alkoxide is the stronger base in that pair.
✔ KEY RULE — The acidity order in solution: CH3OH > 1 degree > 2 degree > 3 degree. Methanol is the most acidic common alcohol, ethanol next, then propan-2-ol, and 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tert-butanol) is the least acidic. The conjugate bases run exactly backwards: tert-butoxide is the strongest base of the family, which is precisely why it is sold as a bulky strong base for Hofmann eliminations.
• WHY alkyl groups weaken the acid — acidity is decided by the stability of the anion left behind. Alkyl groups push electron density towards oxygen (the +I effect), intensifying the negative charge on an already negative oxygen and destabilising the alkoxide. More alkyl groups on the carbinol carbon means a less stable anion, a less willing proton loss, a weaker acid. Bulky alkoxides are also poorly solvated by water, which piles a second penalty on the tertiary case.
• EXAMPLE — The gas-phase flip, and the lesson inside it: strip away the solvent and the order reverses to 3 degree > 2 degree > 1 degree, because in vacuum the large, polarisable alkyl groups can spread and stabilise the negative charge instead. Orders are measured in a medium, not imagined in the abstract — in solution, solvation and the +I effect write the 1 > 2 > 3 order that every syllabus question uses.
• EXAMPLE — Reaction with sodium metal makes the order visible: 2 R-OH + 2 Na gives 2 R-ONa + H2 gas. The fizz is brisk with methanol and ethanol, slower with propan-2-ol, and sluggish with tert-butanol — the stopwatch tracks the acidity order exactly. Phenol, coming in Section 8, reacts much faster still, your first hint that the ring changes everything.
• WORKED PATTERN — To compare any two OH compounds as acids, draw the anion and ask one question: what happens to the negative charge on oxygen? Anything that spreads it (resonance), withdraws from it (electron-withdrawing groups), or solvates it well makes a stronger acid. Anything that donates into it (alkyl +I) makes a weaker acid. This one question answers the alcohol order here and the whole phenol ladder in Section 8.
• YOUR TURN — Rank as acids: propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol, methanol — then name the strongest base among their conjugate bases. Answers: methanol > propan-1-ol > propan-2-ol > 2-methylpropan-2-ol as acids; the strongest base is the conjugate base of the weakest acid, tert-butoxide, (CH3)3C-O-.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Keep the twins apart: acidity runs 1 degree > 2 degree > 3 degree (alkoxide stability), but reactivity towards HX in the next section runs 3 degree > 2 degree > 1 degree (carbocation stability). Same three labels, opposite orders, different intermediates. If a question does not name its reaction, it is testing whether you invent the wrong order.

Alcohols Under Attack — HX, the Lucas Test and Dehydration
• DEFINITION — With hydrogen halides, an alcohol swaps OH for X: R-OH + HX gives R-X + H2O. The OH is first protonated to -OH2+, turning a hopeless leaving group into water, an excellent one; then either the halide attacks (SN2, primary) or the water leaves and a carbocation is captured (SN1, secondary and tertiary). This is Chapter 15 read backwards.
✔ KEY RULE — Two orders ride on this reaction: HX reactivity is HI > HBr > HCl (HCl is so sluggish with primary and secondary alcohols that anhydrous ZnCl2 is added to activate the OH), and alcohol reactivity is 3 degree > 2 degree > 1 degree, because the transition state has carbocation character. Tertiary alcohols react with concentrated HCl by simple shaking; primary alcohols need HBr, or PBr3, or the SOCl2 route of Chapter 15 for a clean job.
• EXAMPLE — The Lucas test turns the order into a stopwatch, exactly as Chapter 15 previewed: the reagent is concentrated HCl with anhydrous ZnCl2. Add it to the alcohol and watch for turbidity — the insoluble alkyl chloride clouding the mixture. Tertiary: turbid immediately. Secondary: turbid in about five minutes. Primary: stays clear at room temperature and reacts only on heating. One reagent, three timings, and you have classified an unknown alcohol without touching a spectrometer.
• DEFINITION — Dehydration removes water from within the alcohol itself: heated with concentrated H2SO4 or H3PO4, an alcohol loses OH from one carbon and H from the next, forming an alkene. The ease order is 3 degree > 2 degree > 1 degree, again carbocation-driven, and the conditions soften as the class rises: a primary alcohol needs concentrated H2SO4 at 443 K, a secondary one uses 85% H3PO4 at about 440 K, and a tertiary one dehydrates with just 20% H3PO4 at 358 K.
• WHY Saytzeff rules the product — where two different beta carbons carry hydrogens, the major alkene is the more substituted one, because alkyl groups stabilise a double bond: butan-2-ol dehydrates mainly to but-2-ene (roughly four parts in five) and only a little to but-1-ene. Count substituents on each candidate double bond before choosing — this is Chapter 15's rule wearing an OH costume.
• EXAMPLE — The rearrangement ambush, worked in full: dehydrate 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol, CH3-CH(OH)-C(CH3)3, with hot acid. Step 1: OH is protonated and leaves as water, giving a SECONDARY carbocation at C2. Step 2: a methyl group on the neighbouring quaternary carbon shifts over with its bonding pair (a methyl shift), moving the positive charge to C3, now a TERTIARY carbocation — the upgrade is irresistible. Step 3: a proton is lost from the new cation: from a methyl beside it, giving 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene, the tetrasubstituted alkene and the MAJOR product; the direct, unrearranged alkene 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene is only a trace. Neopentyl alcohol, (CH3)3C-CH2-OH, plays the same trick one carbon earlier and dehydrates mainly to 2-methylbut-2-ene.
• WORKED PATTERN — Dehydration in four moves: (1) protonate OH, water leaves, draw the carbocation. (2) Interrogate the neighbours: would a hydride or methyl shift from the next carbon upgrade the cation (1 to 2 degree, or 2 to 3 degree)? If yes, shift first — the product skeleton is the REARRANGED one. (3) Remove a beta proton from the final cation. (4) If two alkenes compete, award the major product to the more substituted double bond (Saytzeff).
• YOUR TURN — Predict: (a) cyclohexanol with concentrated H2SO4, heat. (b) 2-methylbutan-2-ol with dilute acid, warm. Answers: (a) cyclohexene — symmetrical, one alkene only, no rearrangement available or needed. (b) 2-methylbut-2-ene as the major product (trisubstituted, Saytzeff) with some 2-methylbut-1-ene; the cation is already tertiary, so no shift occurs.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Ethanol plus concentrated H2SO4 has two famous answers and the temperature is the whole question: at 413 K the product is ethoxyethane (an ether, Section 11); at 443 K it is ethene. Options swap these numbers deliberately. Second trap: the Lucas reagent is a test for ALCOHOLS (this chapter), built on the same reactivity order Chapter 15 quoted for making halides — identical timings, one less thing to memorise.

The Oxidation Ladder — How Far Each Alcohol Climbs
• DEFINITION — Oxidising an alcohol means removing hydrogen from the carbinol carbon (or, for the strongest reagents, breaking further). The destination depends on how many hydrogens that carbon carries: a primary alcohol climbs two rungs, to an aldehyde and then a carboxylic acid; a secondary alcohol climbs one rung, to a ketone, and stops; a tertiary alcohol, whose carbinol carbon carries no hydrogen at all, cannot climb — it resists oxidation under normal conditions.
• Read the figure with this section (Fig. 16.1) lane by lane: the top lane is the two-rung primary climb with a reagent gate between the rungs; the middle lane is the single secondary step ending at a stop sign; the bottom lane shows the tertiary case refusing the ladder and exiting sideways, as an alkene, under hot copper or acid conditions.
✔ KEY RULE — The reagent gates: PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate, in dry CH2Cl2) is the gentle gatekeeper — it oxidises a primary alcohol to the aldehyde and stops there. Strong oxidants — acidified KMnO4, K2Cr2O7 with H2SO4, or concentrated HNO3 — push primary alcohols all the way to the carboxylic acid, and convert secondary alcohols to ketones. Ketones resist further oxidation under normal conditions; only drastic conditions cleave their C-C bonds.
✔ KEY RULE — Copper at 573 K (vapour-phase dehydrogenation) is the class test in disguise: pass the alcohol vapour over heated copper and a primary alcohol gives an aldehyde, a secondary alcohol gives a ketone, and a tertiary alcohol gives an ALKENE — with no hydrogen on its carbinol carbon it cannot dehydrogenate, so it dehydrates instead. Three classes, three product families, one hot metal.
• EXAMPLE — Ethanol three ways: with PCC it stops at ethanal; with excess acidified KMnO4 and heat it runs to ethanoic acid; over copper at 573 K it gives ethanal again (dehydrogenation, H2 released). Propan-2-ol is boring by design: PCC, KMnO4 or copper all deliver the same propanone. 2-Methylpropan-2-ol ignores alkaline KMnO4 entirely — the classic observation that a tertiary alcohol has been spotted.
• WHY PCC stops — in dry dichloromethane there is no water available to hydrate the aldehyde into the gem-diol form that further oxidation attacks, and PCC is too mild to force the issue. Strong aqueous oxidants keep the aldehyde hydrated and keep pulling: aldehyde in water is a halfway house, not a destination. Choosing PCC versus KMnO4 is choosing which rung to stop at — a pure reagent-reading skill.
• WORKED PATTERN — Answer any oxidation question in three beats: (1) name the class of the alcohol (count hydrogens on the carbinol carbon: two means primary, one means secondary, none means tertiary). (2) name the reagent's strength (PCC = stop at first rung; strong aqueous = top rung; Cu/573 K = dehydrogenation, with tertiary diverted to alkene). (3) write the product with the same carbon skeleton — oxidation never rearranges the chain.
• YOUR TURN — Name the product: (a) butan-1-ol + PCC. (b) butan-1-ol + excess acidified K2Cr2O7, heat. (c) butan-2-ol over Cu at 573 K. (d) 2-methylpropan-2-ol over Cu at 573 K. Answers: (a) butanal. (b) butanoic acid. (c) butan-2-one. (d) 2-methylpropene — dehydration, not oxidation.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Tertiary alcohols resist oxidation" carries a footnote: under strongly acidic, hot, forcing conditions they first dehydrate to alkenes, which strong oxidants then cleave. Options offering a "tertiary ketone" or a carboxylic acid with the same carbon count from a tertiary alcohol are doubly wrong — and a ketone from a tertiary alcohol without losing a carbon is impossible, since the carbinol carbon has no hydrogen to give up.

Phenols — Preparation: Cumene, Diazonium and Dow
• DEFINITION — Phenol, C6H5-OH, is the simplest phenol: a colourless, needle-crystalline solid (melting point 314 K) with a characteristic hospital smell, sparingly soluble in water, poisonous and corrosive to skin. Left in air and light it slowly oxidises and turns pink — your first evidence that phenols oxidise far more easily than alcohols.
• DEFINITION — The cumene process is how the world actually makes phenol, in three steps. Step 1: benzene is alkylated with propene over a phosphoric acid catalyst (a Friedel-Crafts step) to give cumene, isopropylbenzene, C6H5-CH(CH3)2. Step 2: air oxidation attacks the benzylic C-H — the one weak point — converting cumene into cumene hydroperoxide, C6H5-C(CH3)2-OOH. Step 3: warm dilute sulphuric acid rearranges and cleaves the hydroperoxide into phenol plus propanone (acetone).
• Read the figure with this section (Fig. 16.3) as the whole business plan: two cheap feedstocks enter on the left, and two saleable products leave on the right. Step 3 hides a rearrangement — the phenyl group migrates from carbon to the electron-hungry oxygen before the cleavage — which is why the ring ends up holding the OH.
• WHY industry loves cumene — the co-product is not waste: acetone is itself a major industrial solvent, so one plant sells both ends of the cleavage. Almost all the world's phenol is made this way; the older routes below survive as laboratory methods and exam questions.
• DEFINITION — From diazonium salts: benzene diazonium chloride, warmed with water (or boiled with dilute acid), hydrolyses to phenol with nitrogen gas bubbling off. This is the clean laboratory preparation and your bridge to the amines chapter, where diazonium chemistry lives. From benzene sulphonic acid: fusion with molten NaOH at about 573 K gives sodium phenoxide, and acidification releases phenol — the classical industrial route.
• EXAMPLE — The Dow process completes the set, and you have met it before: chlorobenzene with aqueous NaOH at 623 K and 300 atmospheres gives sodium phenoxide, then phenol on acidification — Chapter 15's brute-force door into haloarene chemistry is, from this side, a phenol preparation. Four routes, four starting materials: benzene (cumene), aniline (diazonium), benzenesulphonic acid (fusion), chlorobenzene (Dow).
• WORKED PATTERN — Choose the route by the starting material the question hands you, not by habit: hydrocarbon feedstock and industrial scale means cumene; a diazonium salt already drawn means just add warm water; SO3H on the ring means alkali fusion; Cl on the ring with violent conditions quoted means Dow.
• YOUR TURN — (a) In the cumene process, what is oxidised in step 2 and what two products form in step 3? (b) Which catalyst runs step 1? Answers: (a) the benzylic tertiary C-H of cumene is oxidised to the hydroperoxide group; step 3 gives phenol and acetone. (b) Phosphoric acid, H3PO4.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — In step 3 it is the PHENYL group that migrates to oxygen, not a methyl group — that migration is exactly what puts OH on the ring instead of on the side chain. And the co-product is acetone: options offering propan-2-ol (the hydroperoxide fully reduced, a different reaction) or propene are traps.

Phenol Acidity — Why the Ring Changes Everything
• DEFINITION — Phenol is a genuine, if weak, acid: pKa about 10, which makes it roughly a million times more acidic than ethanol (pKa about 16). It turns blue litmus red, dissolves in aqueous NaOH to form sodium phenoxide, and reacts briskly with sodium metal — but it does NOT react with sodium hydrogencarbonate and gives no effervescence with carbonates beyond forming the phenoxide with bicarbonate left behind. That one observation separates phenols from carboxylic acids (pKa about 4 to 5), which fizz with NaHCO3 freely.
• WHY the ring makes the difference — draw the phenoxide ion: its negative charge is not stuck on oxygen but delocalised by resonance onto the ortho and para carbons of the ring, spread over four atoms in five resonance structures. Now draw phenol itself: its resonance structures carry charge separation, with a positive charge on oxygen — comparatively uncomfortable. Ionisation thus trades an uncomfortable neutral molecule for a comfortable, delocalised anion, and the equilibrium leans towards the ions. An alkoxide from ethanol has no ring to spread into: the charge sits fully on oxygen, the anion is unstable, and ethanol holds its proton a million times more tightly. The sp2 ring carbon is also more electronegative than an sp3 carbon, pulling electron density away from oxygen and helping the anion further.
✔ KEY RULE — Substituents tune the ladder by one principle: anything that stabilises the phenoxide ion strengthens the acid. Electron-withdrawing groups (-NO2, -CN, halogens) pull charge away and stabilise it — stronger acid. Electron-donating groups (-CH3, -OCH3) push charge in and destabilise it — weaker acid. The effect is strongest from the ortho and para positions, where resonance can carry the charge directly onto the substituent, and weaker from meta, where only the inductive effect operates.
• Read the ladder in the figure with this section (Fig. 16.2) from the top: picric acid, 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, pKa 0.38 — three nitro groups drag so hard on the phenoxide charge that this "phenol" out-acidifies acetic acid. Then p-nitrophenol, 7.15; o-nitrophenol, 7.23; m-nitrophenol, 8.40 — note meta lagging, resonance-locked out. Then phenol itself, 10.0; p-cresol, 10.2, its methyl group donating it slightly weaker; and far below, ethanol at about 16, the no-ring baseline.
• EXAMPLE — The ortho quirk: o-nitrophenol is a hair weaker than p-nitrophenol (7.23 versus 7.15) despite identical groups, because the ortho isomer ties itself up with an intramolecular hydrogen bond between its OH and its NO2, stabilising the neutral molecule and making it steam-volatile into the bargain. That volatility is a gift: after nitrating phenol with dilute acid (next section), the ortho product is separated from the para product by steam distillation — ortho flies over with the steam, para stays behind.
• EXAMPLE — Two quick verdicts using the principle: p-chlorophenol is a stronger acid than phenol (chlorine withdraws inductively more than it donates by resonance, net stabilisation of the anion). p-Methoxyphenol is weaker than phenol (the OCH3 group donates strongly by resonance from the para position, flooding the ring and the anion with charge). If your reasoning names the anion's stability, the answer follows.
• WORKED PATTERN — Rank any set of phenols in three passes: (1) count nitro groups and other strong withdrawers at ortho/para — most wins. (2) among equals, a withdrawer at meta beats no substituent, which beats a donor anywhere. (3) check the ortho anomalies: intramolecular hydrogen bonding (o-nitrophenol) or bulky ortho groups slightly blunt acidity. Apply the passes in order and the ladder rebuilds itself for any option set.
• YOUR TURN — Rank as acids: phenol, o-cresol, m-nitrophenol, 2,4-dinitrophenol. Answers: 2,4-dinitrophenol first by a mile (pKa about 4.1 — two nitro groups at resonance positions), then m-nitrophenol (8.40), then phenol (10.0), and o-cresol last (about 10.2 — a methyl donor, ortho or not, still donates).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two carbonate traps guard this section. First: phenol plus Na2CO3 gives sodium phenoxide plus NaHCO3 — no CO2 effervescence, because phenol is weaker than carbonic acid's first ionisation; students who write "CO2 evolved" have promoted phenol to carboxylic-acid strength. Second: picric acid has no COOH group anywhere — its acidity is pure phenoxide stabilisation, three nitro groups strong.

Phenols React — Substitution on an Activated Ring
• DEFINITION — The OH group pours electron density into the ring by resonance and is a powerful activator and ortho/para director for electrophilic substitution: phenol reacts with electrophiles far faster than benzene, often with no catalyst at all. Meanwhile the OH itself keeps phenol's own diary of reactions — with metals, with Zn dust, with oxidants — so this section splits into ring reactions and OH reactions.
✔ KEY RULE — The bromination fork, decided by solvent: Br2 in CS2 (or CCl4) at 273 K gives monobromination — a mixture of o-bromophenol and p-bromophenol, p-isomer major. Bromine WATER, in excess, gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol as a white precipitate, and the orange colour of bromine vanishes on contact. In water, phenol ionises slightly and the solvent polarises Br2, so the ring is attacked at all three activated positions before it can stop. The white precipitate plus decolourisation doubles as the standard test for a phenol (or an enol).
✔ KEY RULE — The nitration fork, decided by acid strength: dilute HNO3 at about 293 K gives mononitration — o-nitrophenol plus p-nitrophenol, separated afterwards by steam distillation (Section 8's volatility trick). Concentrated HNO3 with concentrated H2SO4 does not stop: it races to 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, picric acid, the yellow solid once used as an explosive. (Industry actually sulphonates first and nitrates the disulphonic acid, because direct nitration oxidises and wastes much of the phenol — examiners accept the direct equation.)
• EXAMPLE — Sulphonation shows temperature control: concentrated H2SO4 at room temperature gives mainly the ortho sulphonic acid; heating shifts the major product to the para isomer, because sulphonation is reversible and the para product is the thermodynamic survivor. A minor reaction, but it stocks the "reversible, thermodynamic versus kinetic" pattern you will meet again.
• DEFINITION — The OH group's own reactions: with zinc dust on strong heating, phenol is reduced to benzene (the OH is stripped entirely). With chromic acid (Na2Cr2O7 and H2SO4), phenol is oxidised to benzoquinone, a dark-coloured conjugated diketone — the same eagerness to oxidise that turns stored phenol pink in air. With acid chlorides in aqueous NaOH (Schotten-Baumann conditions), phenol gives phenyl esters such as phenyl ethanoate; with carboxylic acids directly it barely reacts, being a far poorer nucleophile than an alcohol.
• EXAMPLE — Two bench tests for phenol to file alongside bromine water: neutral iron(III) chloride solution gives an intense violet colouration with phenol (a complex with the phenoxide), and phenol couples with ice-cold benzene diazonium chloride in alkali to give p-hydroxyazobenzene, a bright orange azo dye — a forward bridge to the amines chapter, and proof of how hungry the phenol ring is for even a weak electrophile like a diazonium ion.
• WORKED PATTERN — Read phenol questions by asking which partner is named: a halogen or nitrating mix means the RING reacts (activated, ortho/para, watch the fork conditions); Na, NaOH or FeCl3 means the OH is being probed as an acid or ligand; Zn dust or chromic acid means the OH is being removed or oxidised. The reagent tells you which of phenol's two personalities is on stage.
• YOUR TURN — Name the major product: phenol with (a) Br2 in CS2 at 273 K, (b) excess bromine water, (c) dilute HNO3 at 293 K (two products — name both), (d) Zn dust, strong heat. Answers: (a) p-bromophenol (with some ortho isomer). (b) 2,4,6-tribromophenol, white precipitate. (c) o-nitrophenol and p-nitrophenol. (d) benzene.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Writing monobromophenol as the bromine-water product is the most repeated phenol error in existence: water as solvent plus excess reagent means all three open ortho/para seats are taken. The mirror trap is offering tribromophenol for Br2 in CS2 — no water, cold, controlled, one bromine only.

Two Signature Reactions — Kolbe-Schmitt and Reimer-Tiemann
• DEFINITION — The Kolbe-Schmitt reaction installs a carboxyl group on the ring: phenol is first converted to sodium phenoxide with NaOH, then treated with CO2 under pressure (4 to 7 bar) at 400 K, giving sodium salicylate; acidification releases salicylic acid, 2-hydroxybenzoic acid, with the COOH ortho to the OH. Carbon dioxide is a feeble electrophile, and only the fiercely activated phenoxide ring can catch it — free phenol will not react, which is why the phenoxide is made first.
• WHY salicylic acid matters — it is the parent of a medicine cabinet: acetylate its OH with ethanoic anhydride and you get aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid); esterify its COOH with methanol and you get methyl salicylate, the oil of wintergreen in pain balms. One Kolbe product, two famous descendants — the reason this reaction is never skipped by examiners.
• EXAMPLE — The potassium variation rewards attention: run Kolbe with potassium phenoxide at a higher temperature and the major product swings to the PARA hydroxybenzoic acid — the larger potassium ion chelates the ortho position less tightly, and thermodynamics takes the less crowded seat. Sodium and mild heat give ortho; potassium and stronger heat give para.
• DEFINITION — The Reimer-Tiemann reaction installs an aldehyde group instead: phenol heated with chloroform and aqueous KOH (or NaOH) at about 340 K gives salicylaldehyde, o-hydroxybenzaldehyde, as the major product, with a little of the para isomer. The groups installed differ by one oxygen: Kolbe gives -COOH, Reimer-Tiemann gives -CHO, and the reagents are the whole difference.
• WHY a carbene does the work — chloroform plus hydroxide generates the electrophile in three small steps: hydroxide removes chloroform's proton, the resulting CCl3- anion ejects chloride, and what remains is dichlorocarbene, :CCl2, a neutral species with only six electrons, ravenously electrophilic. The phenoxide ring attacks it at the ortho position; the installed -CHCl2 group is then hydrolysed by the aqueous alkali to -CHO. Swap chloroform for carbon tetrachloride and the same reaction delivers salicylic acid instead (the -CCl3 group hydrolyses to -COOH).
• WORKED PATTERN — Tell the twins apart by reagent alone: CO2 plus pressure on a phenoxide means Kolbe, product an ortho-COOH (salicylic acid family). CHCl3 plus aqueous alkali means Reimer-Tiemann, product an ortho-CHO (salicylaldehyde). If CCl4 replaces CHCl3 in the second, upgrade the product to salicylic acid. Three reagent strings, three products — drill them as a set.
• YOUR TURN — Outline two steps from phenol to aspirin, naming each reaction. Answers: Step 1, Kolbe-Schmitt: phenol to sodium phenoxide (NaOH), then CO2 at 400 K and 4 to 7 bar, then dilute acid gives salicylic acid. Step 2, acetylation: salicylic acid with ethanoic anhydride gives acetylsalicylic acid — aspirin.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Kolbe's substrate is sodium PHENOXIDE, not phenol — writing "phenol + CO2" as the reacting pair loses the logic mark, because neutral phenol is not activated enough for so weak an electrophile. And Reimer-Tiemann's electrophile is dichlorocarbene, not chloroform itself: CHCl3 is only the carbene's shipping container.

Ethers — Preparation: Williamson and the Temperature Fork
• DEFINITION — Ethers are the C-O-C family: simple or symmetrical when both alkyl groups match (ethoxyethane), mixed when they differ (methoxybenzene, tert-butyl methyl ether). They are functional isomers of alcohols — same formulas, utterly different physics, as Section 1's boiling-point gap showed: no OH means no hydrogen bonding between ether molecules, so boiling points sit far below the isomeric alcohols, though the oxygen lone pairs still accept hydrogen bonds from water, giving lower ethers a modest solubility.
✔ KEY RULE — The Williamson synthesis builds ethers by SN2: a sodium alkoxide or phenoxide attacks an alkyl halide, R-O- Na+ + R'-X gives R-O-R' + NaX. It obeys two design laws. Law 1: the HALIDE partner must be primary (methyl is best) — secondary halides give poor yields, and tertiary halides give NO ether at all, because the alkoxide, a strong base, simply eliminates them to alkenes. Law 2: the halide cannot be an aryl halide — the ring C-X bond of Chapter 15 will not undergo SN2. The alkoxide partner, by contrast, may be anything at all: primary, secondary, tertiary, even a phenoxide.
• EXAMPLE — Chapter 15's design classic: tert-butyl ethyl ether. Route A, sodium tert-butoxide plus bromoethane — the bulky group rides on the alkoxide, the halide is primary, SN2 succeeds: ether formed. Route B, sodium ethoxide plus tert-butyl bromide — the halide is tertiary, the ethoxide acts as a base, and the only product is isobutylene (2-methylpropene) plus ethanol. Same target, opposite verdicts: an ether is designed, not just mixed.
• EXAMPLE — Phenol ethers come from the phenoxide side: sodium phenoxide plus iodomethane gives anisole (methoxybenzene); sodium phenoxide plus bromoethane gives phenetole (ethoxybenzene). Note the direction of construction — the ring always arrives as the nucleophile, never as the halide, exactly as Law 2 demands. Anisole returns in the next section as the star of ether reactions.
✔ KEY RULE — The dehydration fork: concentrated H2SO4 dehydrates ethanol INTERmolecularly at 413 K — one ethanol molecule, protonated, is displaced by a second, giving ethoxyethane — but INTRAMolecularly at 443 K, giving ethene. Same flask, 30 degrees apart, different reaction class. The method only serves symmetrical ethers from primary alcohols: secondary and tertiary alcohols under hot acid eliminate to alkenes (E1) long before they can be displaced.
• EXAMPLE — Diethyl ether's darker footnote: ethers exposed to air and light slowly form peroxides, which are violently explosive when concentrated by distillation — old ether bottles are treated as hazards, not stock. Its volatility and flammability (boiling point about 308 K, ignites readily) explain both its anaesthetic heyday and its retirement.
• WORKED PATTERN — Design any ether in three moves: (1) draw the C-O-C skeleton and snap one C-O bond on paper. (2) Assign the oxygen side to whichever partner is bulkier, tertiary, or aromatic — that side will be the alkoxide or phenoxide. (3) Assign the other side to the halide, and audit it: primary or methyl, never tertiary, never aryl. If the audit fails, snap the other bond instead.
• YOUR TURN — Design syntheses of (a) tert-butyl methyl ether, (b) ethoxybenzene (phenetole), stating both partners for each. Answers: (a) sodium tert-butoxide + iodomethane (tertiary on the alkoxide side, methyl on the halide side). (b) sodium phenoxide + bromoethane. In both, swapping the partners would fail — tertiary or aryl halides cannot be displaced.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The ether that never forms: any option synthesising an ether from a tertiary halide plus an alkoxide is drawing an elimination (the products are an alkene and an alcohol), and any option using chlorobenzene plus an alkoxide draws no reaction at all. Examiners plant both every year; the two design laws execute them on sight.

Ethers React — Cleavage by HX and the Anisole Ring
• DEFINITION — Hot, concentrated hydrogen halides cut ethers apart: R-O-R' + HX gives R-OH + R'-X, with reactivity HI > HBr > HCl. The mechanism opens by protonating the ether oxygen (making -OH+- a good leaving group), then the halide either displaces at carbon (SN2) or the weaker C-O bond ionises first (SN1) — and which carbon ends up holding the halogen follows a strict set of rules.
✔ KEY RULE — The cleavage rules: with two primary or secondary alkyl groups, the halide attacks the LESS hindered carbon (SN2), so the smaller group takes the halogen and the larger leaves as the alcohol. If one group is tertiary (or benzylic), the SN1 road opens and the TERTIARY halide forms instead — the stable cation wins over the small-carbon rule. With excess HX and strong heat, the alcohol formed is itself converted, and both alkyl groups finish as halides.
✔ KEY RULE — The aryl rule, which outranks all others: in an alkyl aryl ether, the ALKYL-oxygen bond always breaks and the aryl-oxygen bond never does. Anisole plus HI gives phenol plus iodomethane — never iodobenzene plus methanol — because the ring C-O bond carries partial double-bond character from resonance (the same argument as haloarenes in Chapter 15), and a phenyl cation is far too unstable to form. Excess acid does not change this: the phenol produced simply stays phenol.
• EXAMPLE — Ethyl tert-butyl ether with HI: protonate, ionise at the tertiary side, capture — the products are tert-butyl iodide plus ethanol. Note the verdict contradicts the naive "smaller group takes the halogen" instinct; the tertiary group's cation stability outranks the size rule, which applies only when both sides must go by SN2. Methoxyethane with one equivalent of HI, both sides SN2-only, obeys the size rule instead: iodomethane plus ethanol.
• DEFINITION — Anisole's ring is the other theatre: the -OCH3 group is a strong activator and ortho/para director, so anisole outruns benzene in every electrophilic substitution. Bromination with Br2 in ethanoic acid needs no iron catalyst and gives p-bromoanisole as the major product. Nitration with the usual mixed acid gives p-nitroanisole plus the ortho isomer. Friedel-Crafts reactions (CH3Cl or CH3COCl with AlCl3) install alkyl or acyl groups mainly at the para position — 4-methoxyacetophenone is the classic acylation product.
• WORKED PATTERN — Predict any ether cleavage in four moves: (1) protonate the oxygen mentally. (2) Check for an aryl group: if present, it keeps the oxygen — write phenol plus alkyl halide and stop. (3) Check for a tertiary or benzylic group: if present, it takes the halogen (SN1). (4) Otherwise the smaller alkyl group takes the halogen (SN2), and if excess HX and heat are stated, convert the alcohol product into its halide too.
• YOUR TURN — Predict: (a) methoxyethane + HI, one equivalent. (b) anisole + excess HBr, strong heat. (c) ethoxyethane + excess HI, heat. Answers: (a) iodomethane + ethanol. (b) phenol + bromomethane — the methyl group still leaves with the halogen, and phenol survives even forcing conditions. (c) two molecules of ethyl iodide (plus water) — the ethanol formed in the first cut is converted by the excess acid.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Iodobenzene plus methanol, offered for anisole plus HI, is the single most repeated ether error in print: it breaks the bond that resonance protects and keeps the bond that actually breaks. Second trap: ether cleavage demands concentrated HX and heat — dilute acid or cold conditions give no reaction, however tempting the products look.

Reaction Map and Rules Sheet
✔ Hydration fork: alkene + H2O/acid -> Markovnikov alcohol through a carbocation (rearrangement possible); alkene + (i) B2H6, (ii) H2O2/NaOH -> anti-Markovnikov alcohol, concerted, no rearrangement; industrial ethanol: ethene + steam, H3PO4, 573 K, 60 to 70 atm
✔ Grignard table: CH2O -> 1 degree alcohol; other aldehydes -> 2 degree; ketones -> 3 degree; esters (2 equivalents) -> 3 degree with two identical reagent groups; ethylene oxide -> 1 degree alcohol with two extra carbons; LiAlH4 reduces acids and esters, NaBH4 only aldehydes and ketones
✔ Acidity of alcohols in solution: CH3OH > 1 degree > 2 degree > 3 degree (alkoxide destabilised by +I and poor solvation); alkoxide basicity reversed, tert-butoxide strongest; gas-phase acidity order flips to 3 > 2 > 1 degree
✔ HX with alcohols: reactivity HI > HBr > HCl; alcohol order 3 > 2 > 1 degree; Lucas reagent (conc. HCl + anhydrous ZnCl2): 3 degree turbid instantly, 2 degree in about 5 minutes, 1 degree only on heating
✔ Dehydration: ease 3 > 2 > 1 degree; Saytzeff major alkene is the more substituted one; check for hydride or methyl shifts that upgrade the cation before removing a beta proton; ethanol forks at 413 K (ethoxyethane) versus 443 K (ethene)
✔ Oxidation ladder: PCC stops a 1 degree alcohol at the aldehyde; strong aqueous oxidants (KMnO4/H+, K2Cr2O7/H2SO4) take 1 degree to the carboxylic acid and 2 degree to the ketone; ketones stop there under normal conditions; 3 degree alcohols resist (no H on the carbinol carbon)
✔ Copper at 573 K: 1 degree alcohol -> aldehyde; 2 degree -> ketone; 3 degree -> alkene by dehydration — a one-line class test
✔ Phenol preparation: cumene process (benzene + propene, H3PO4; then O2 to the hydroperoxide; then dilute H2SO4 to phenol + acetone); diazonium salt + warm water; benzene sulphonic acid fused with NaOH; Dow process, chlorobenzene + aqueous NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm
✔ Phenol acidity ladder (pKa): picric acid 0.38 < p-nitrophenol 7.15 < o-nitrophenol 7.23 < m-nitrophenol 8.40 < phenol 10.0 < p-cresol 10.2 << ethanol about 16; phenol reacts with NaOH and Na but NOT with NaHCO3 (no effervescence) — the test that separates phenols from carboxylic acids
✔ Phenol substitution forks: Br2 in CS2 at 273 K -> mono (o- and p-bromophenol); bromine water -> 2,4,6-tribromophenol, white precipitate; dilute HNO3 -> o- plus p-nitrophenol (separate by steam distillation, ortho is volatile); conc. HNO3/H2SO4 -> picric acid; Zn dust -> benzene; chromic acid -> benzoquinone; neutral FeCl3 -> violet colour
✔ Kolbe-Schmitt: sodium phenoxide + CO2, 400 K, 4 to 7 bar, then H+ -> salicylic acid (potassium salt, hotter, gives the para acid); salicylic acid + ethanoic anhydride -> aspirin; Reimer-Tiemann: phenol + CHCl3 + aqueous KOH at about 340 K -> salicylaldehyde, electrophile is dichlorocarbene :CCl2; with CCl4 the product is salicylic acid
✔ Williamson laws: alkoxide or phenoxide + alkyl halide (SN2); the halide must be 1 degree or methyl, never 3 degree (elimination instead), never aryl (no reaction); bulky and aryl groups belong on the alkoxide side — tert-butoxide + C2H5Br works, ethoxide + tert-butyl bromide gives isobutylene
✔ Ether cleavage by HX (HI > HBr > HCl, concentrated, hot): smaller alkyl takes the halogen by SN2; a tertiary or benzylic group takes it by SN1; alkyl aryl ethers always break the alkyl-O bond (anisole + HI -> phenol + CH3I); excess HX converts the alcohol product too, but phenol is never cleaved further
✔ Anisole ring: -OCH3 strongly activating, ortho/para directing; bromination in ethanoic acid without FeBr3 -> p-bromoanisole major; nitration and Friedel-Crafts -> para major products

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1 — Quoting 3 degree > 2 degree > 1 degree as the acidity order of alcohols → Why it fails: that is the HX reactivity order, which rides carbocation stability; acidity rides alkoxide stability, and alkyl groups destabilise the alkoxide → Correct principle: acidity in solution is CH3OH > 1 > 2 > 3 degree; HX reactivity and dehydration ease are 3 > 2 > 1 degree; name the reaction before reciting the order
• Mistake 2 — Showing phenol fizzing with NaHCO3, or evolving CO2 from Na2CO3 → Why it fails: phenol (pKa about 10) is a weaker acid than carbonic acid, so bicarbonate and carbonate cannot strip its proton with gas evolution → Correct principle: phenol reacts with NaOH (and with Na metal) but not with NaHCO3; effervescence with NaHCO3 belongs to carboxylic acids — it is the standard phenol-versus-acid distinction
• Mistake 3 — Oxidising a primary alcohol to the carboxylic acid when the reagent is PCC → Why it fails: PCC in dry solvent is a deliberately mild oxidant and the aldehyde cannot hydrate without water, so oxidation halts at the first rung → Correct principle: PCC (or anhydrous CrO3) stops at the aldehyde; only strong aqueous oxidants (KMnO4/H+, K2Cr2O7/H2SO4) run the full ladder to the acid
• Mistake 4 — Giving a ketone as the product of a tertiary alcohol over copper at 573 K → Why it fails: dehydrogenation needs a hydrogen on the carbinol carbon, and a tertiary alcohol has none; forming a carbonyl would require breaking a C-C bond → Correct principle: over Cu at 573 K, 1 degree gives aldehyde, 2 degree gives ketone, 3 degree dehydrates to the alkene
• Mistake 5 — Predicting the Markovnikov alcohol from hydroboration-oxidation → Why it fails: the boron adds to the less substituted carbon in a concerted step, and OH inherits boron's seat → Correct principle: B2H6 then H2O2/NaOH is the anti-Markovnikov route — propene gives propan-1-ol; acid hydration gives propan-2-ol; also remember no rearrangement is possible without a carbocation
• Mistake 6 — Reporting 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene as the dehydration product of 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol → Why it fails: the secondary carbocation formed first rearranges by a methyl shift to a tertiary carbocation before losing a proton → Correct principle: in every acid-catalysed alcohol reaction, audit the cation for an upgrading shift; the major alkene here is 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene, from the rearranged skeleton
• Mistake 7 — Designing a Williamson ether with the tertiary group on the halide side, or with an aryl halide → Why it fails: alkoxides are strong bases, so a tertiary halide is eliminated to an alkene; an aryl C-X bond cannot be displaced at all → Correct principle: bulky and aryl groups ride on the alkoxide or phenoxide side; the halide partner must be methyl or primary — sodium tert-butoxide + bromoethane succeeds, sodium ethoxide + tert-butyl bromide gives isobutylene
• Mistake 8 — Writing p-bromophenol as the product of phenol with bromine water → Why it fails: in water the ring is fully activated and bromine is in excess, so all three ortho/para positions are brominated → Correct principle: bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate, a test for phenol); Br2 in CS2 at 273 K is the controlled monobromination
• Mistake 9 — Cleaving anisole with HI to iodobenzene and methanol → Why it fails: the aryl-oxygen bond has partial double-bond character and the phenyl cation is inaccessible, so that bond never breaks → Correct principle: alkyl aryl ethers always split at the alkyl-O bond — anisole + HI gives phenol + CH3I, and the phenol resists further cleavage even with excess acid
• Mistake 10 — Swapping the ethanol dehydration temperatures, giving ethene at 413 K or ether at 443 K → Why it fails: intermolecular dehydration (ether) wins at the lower temperature; the hotter conditions drive the intramolecular elimination (ethene) → Correct principle: 413 K -> ethoxyethane; 443 K -> ethene; the fork is 30 degrees wide and purely thermal, so read the temperature in the question twice
★ HIGHLIGHT — Notice the pattern across all ten: every mistake is a skipped fork. Hydration has two arms, oxidation has gates, bromination and nitration have solvent and strength arms, Williamson has two sides, cleavage has a protected bond. Find the fork, read its conditions, and the product writes itself.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• Example 1 (Basic) — Problem: starting from propene, prepare (a) propan-2-ol and (b) propan-1-ol
• Given: one unsymmetrical alkene; two regioisomeric alcohols as targets
• Principle: acid hydration is Markovnikov through a carbocation; hydroboration-oxidation is anti-Markovnikov and concerted — the reagent selects the regioisomer
• Steps: (a) propene + water with a phosphoric or sulphuric acid catalyst: the proton adds to give the secondary cation at C2, water captures it, deprotonation gives propan-2-ol. (b) propene + diborane (B2H6, THF): boron takes the terminal carbon; then H2O2 with NaOH replaces boron by OH at that same carbon, giving propan-1-ol
• Answer: (a) propan-2-ol by acid-catalysed hydration. (b) propan-1-ol by hydroboration-oxidation
• Reasoning: the carbon skeleton never changes; only the address of the OH moves, and the mechanism — cation versus concerted — is what chooses the address
• Mistake to avoid: swapping the two reagents between the targets; anchor the pair as "acid = Markovnikov = more substituted OH" and "boron = anti = terminal OH"
• Example 2 (Basic) — Problem: arrange ethanol, propan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol and phenol in order of acidity, and state which of the four dissolve in aqueous NaOH and which effervesce with NaHCO3 solution
• Given: three alcohols of rising class and one phenol; pKa guideposts: phenol about 10, ethanol about 16, and the alcohol order 1 > 2 > 3 degree
• Principle: acidity follows the stability of the anion: resonance-delocalised phenoxide is vastly more comfortable than any alkoxide, and among alkoxides alkyl donation destabilises in the order 3 > 2 > 1 degree
• Steps: phenol first (resonance). Then ethanol (primary), then propan-2-ol (secondary), then 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tertiary, least acidic). NaOH is a strong enough base to deprotonate phenol only — the alcohols need sodium metal or a stronger base. NaHCO3 is weaker still: none of the four is a carboxylic acid, so none effervesces
• Answer: phenol > ethanol > propan-2-ol > 2-methylpropan-2-ol; only phenol dissolves in NaOH (as sodium phenoxide); none of the four effervesces with NaHCO3
• Reasoning: two different thresholds are being tested — NaOH catches everything stronger than water's conjugate chemistry at the phenol level, while NaHCO3 catches only true carboxylic acids; phenol sits between the two thresholds, which is exactly why it is the examiner's favourite discriminator
• Mistake to avoid: ranking the alcohols 3 > 2 > 1 degree — that is the HX order imported into the wrong reaction (Mistake 1)
• Example 3 (Moderate) — Problem: an alcohol of formula C4H10O turns Lucas reagent turbid immediately, gives no reaction with PCC, and passed over copper at 573 K gives a gas of formula C4H8. Identify the alcohol and justify each clue
• Given: four candidate skeletons fit C4H10O (butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol); three independent observations to satisfy
• Principle: Lucas timing reads the class; PCC silence excludes 1 and 2 degree alcohols (both would oxidise); copper at 573 K converts a tertiary alcohol to an alkene, not a carbonyl
• Steps: instant Lucas turbidity means tertiary — only 2-methylpropan-2-ol qualifies. PCC check: a tertiary alcohol has no carbinol hydrogen, so no oxidation — consistent. Copper check: the tertiary alcohol dehydrates over hot Cu to 2-methylpropene, C4H8 — consistent. Butan-2-ol fails clue one (about 5 minutes) and clue two (PCC gives butan-2-one); the two primary alcohols fail all three
• Answer: 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tert-butyl alcohol)
• Reasoning: each test interrogates a different face of the same structural fact — no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon — so three cheap observations converge on one structure with no spectroscopy
• Mistake to avoid: treating "resists PCC" as proof of an ether; the formula's single oxygen plus instant Lucas turbidity pins a tertiary alcohol, and ethers do not cloud the Lucas reagent at all
• Example 4 (Moderate) — Problem: predict the major product when 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol is warmed with concentrated sulphuric acid, and account for every step
• Given: CH3-CH(OH)-C(CH3)3, a secondary alcohol whose neighbouring carbon is quaternary — the classic rearrangement trap geometry
• Principle: dehydration runs through a carbocation; a cation that can be upgraded by a 1,2-shift will be, before any proton is lost; the final alkene follows Saytzeff
• Steps: Step 1: the OH is protonated and leaves as water, giving the secondary cation CH3-CH+-C(CH3)3. Step 2: a methyl group migrates from C3 with its electron pair; the cation lands on C3, now tertiary: (CH3)2C+-CH(CH3)2. Step 3: lose a proton — from either equivalent set of methyls around the cation there are two alkenes; the tetrasubstituted 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene dominates over 2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene
• Answer: 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene (major), with 2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene minor; the direct product 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene is barely formed
• Reasoning: the product's skeleton (2,3-dimethyl) is not the reactant's skeleton (3,3-dimethyl) — a methyl group has physically moved, which is only possible because an intermediate with an empty orbital invited the shift
• Mistake to avoid: drawing the direct Saytzeff product and stopping — whenever a secondary cation sits beside a quaternary or tertiary carbon, the shift audit (Worked Pattern, Section 5) must run before any alkene is drawn
• Example 5 (Moderate) — Problem: a student needs tert-butyl ethyl ether. She proposes (A) sodium ethoxide + tert-butyl bromide, and (B) sodium tert-butoxide + bromoethane. Grade both proposals and give the actual product of the failing one
• Given: the target ether (CH3)3C-O-CH2CH3; the two possible Williamson pairings of its two C-O bonds
• Principle: Williamson is SN2 on the halide partner; the alkoxide partner is also a strong base, so a tertiary halide is eliminated, never substituted
• Steps: Proposal A puts the tertiary group on the halide: tert-butyl bromide cannot be attacked from the back, ethoxide removes a beta proton instead, and E2 runs to completion. Proposal B puts the tertiary group on the alkoxide: bromoethane is primary and wide open to backside attack, so SN2 proceeds cleanly to the ether
• Answer: B succeeds — tert-butyl ethyl ether. A fails, giving 2-methylpropene (isobutylene) plus ethanol (and NaBr)
• Reasoning: the ether's two C-O bonds are not equivalent in synthesis: one side must survive being an electrophile under basic conditions, and only an unhindered primary carbon can
• Mistake to avoid: treating the two pairings as interchangeable because they contain the same atoms — in Williamson design the assignment of sides IS the reaction
• Example 6 (Challenging) — Problem: convert phenol into (a) picric acid, and (b) aspirin, giving reagents and conditions for each step
• Given: phenol as the single starting material; target (a) is 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, target (b) is acetylsalicylic acid
• Principle: concentrated nitrating mixture exploits the ring's activation to substitute all three open positions; aspirin needs a COOH installed ortho to the OH first (Kolbe), then acetylation of the phenolic OH
• Steps: (a) phenol + concentrated HNO3 with concentrated H2SO4: nitration proceeds past the mononitro stage to 2,4,6-trinitrophenol — picric acid, a yellow solid. (b) Step 1 (Kolbe-Schmitt): phenol + NaOH to sodium phenoxide, then CO2 at 400 K under 4 to 7 bar, then dilute acid, giving salicylic acid. Step 2: salicylic acid + ethanoic anhydride acetylates the phenolic OH (the carboxyl group survives), giving acetylsalicylic acid
• Answer: (a) picric acid by exhaustive nitration. (b) salicylic acid by Kolbe-Schmitt, then aspirin by acetylation with ethanoic anhydride
• Reasoning: the two targets use opposite faces of phenol: (a) is pure ring reactivity (activation makes three substitutions easy), while (b) needs the OH first as an acid (to make phenoxide for Kolbe) and then as the group acetylated — sequencing is the whole problem
• Mistake to avoid: attempting (b) by acetylating phenol first — phenyl ethanoate has no ortho COOH and no route to aspirin; the Kolbe step must come before the acetylation
• Example 7 (Challenging) — Problem: predict and explain the products when anisole (methoxybenzene) is treated with (a) concentrated HI and heat, (b) Br2 in ethanoic acid. Then state what does NOT happen in each case
• Given: an alkyl aryl ether with two theatres of reaction: the side-chain C-O bonds and the activated ring
• Principle: cleavage follows the aryl rule (the alkyl-O bond breaks; the ring-O bond is resonance-strengthened and protected), while electrophilic substitution follows the -OCH3 group's strong ortho/para direction
• Steps: (a) HI protonates the oxygen; iodide attacks the small methyl carbon (SN2); the ring keeps the oxygen. Even with excess HI and heat, the story ends there, because the phenol formed cannot be cleaved further. (b) In ethanoic acid, Br2 is polarised enough by the activated ring itself — no FeBr3 is needed — and substitution lands mainly at the para position (ortho is more crowded beside OCH3)
• Answer: (a) phenol + iodomethane. (b) p-bromoanisole (major). NOT formed: in (a), iodobenzene + methanol — the aryl-O bond never breaks; in (b), no ring bromination occurs without activation the way benzene would demand FeBr3, and no cleavage occurs because no HX is present
• Reasoning: one molecule, two rule sets — the examiner is testing whether you select the theatre by the reagent: HX means cleavage rules, Br2 without acid means ring rules
• Mistake to avoid: answering (a) with the "products" iodobenzene and methanol (Mistake 9), or importing the phenol bromine-water result (tribromo) into the anisole case — the methoxy ring monobrominates under these controlled conditions

• Q7. Anisole on treatment with HI gives: (A) iodobenzene + methanol (B) phenol + iodomethane (C) benzene + iodomethane (D) o-iodophenol + methane
• Q8. The major product of dehydrating 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol with hot acid is: (A) 3,3-dimethylbut-1-ene (B) 2,3-dimethylbut-1-ene (C) 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene (D) 2-methylbut-2-ene

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. (B) butan-1-ol — boron adds to the terminal, less substituted carbon, and oxidation replaces it with OH in place: anti-Markovnikov hydration. Misconception: choosing (A) butan-2-ol, which is the acid-catalysed (Markovnikov) product; the two reagents are a fork, not synonyms
• A2. (D) p-nitrophenol — the para NO2 group stabilises the phenoxide ion by resonance, dropping the pKa to 7.15, well below phenol (10.0); p-cresol's methyl group donates and weakens it (10.2), and ethanol (about 16) has no ring at all. Misconception: picking phenol because it is "the acid of the chapter" while ignoring substituents — the ladder is built by substituent effects
• A3. (C) 2-methylpropan-2-ol — it is the tertiary alcohol, and Lucas timing follows the 3 > 2 > 1 degree reactivity order: tertiary instant, secondary about 5 minutes, primary not at room temperature. Misconception: answering with the acidity order reversed — Lucas is a carbocation race, not an acidity test
• A4. (B) ethoxyethane — at 413 K intermolecular dehydration wins and one ethanol displaces water from a protonated second ethanol; ethene (A) needs 443 K. Misconception: memorising "alcohol + conc. H2SO4 = alkene" without the temperature — the 413/443 fork is the entire question
• A5. (C) 2,4,6-tribromophenol — in water, with bromine in excess, all three activated ortho/para positions are substituted, and the product precipitates white (this is also the confirmatory test for phenol). Misconception: choosing (B) p-bromophenol, which is the Br2-in-CS2 controlled product; the solvent named in the question decides
• A6. (A) sodium tert-butoxide + iodomethane — the tertiary group rides safely on the alkoxide side while the methyl halide, the ideal SN2 substrate, takes the attack. Misconception: choosing (B) — sodium methoxide plus tert-butyl bromide eliminates to 2-methylpropene; a tertiary halide can never be the Williamson electrophile
• A7. (B) phenol + iodomethane — cleavage always breaks the alkyl-oxygen bond; the aryl-oxygen bond is resonance-strengthened and the phenyl cation is inaccessible. Misconception: choosing (A), the most repeated ether error in print; check which side of the oxygen the ring sits on before writing any cleavage product
• A8. (C) 2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene — the secondary carbocation first rearranges by a methyl shift to the tertiary carbocation, and Saytzeff deprotonation then gives the tetrasubstituted alkene. Misconception: choosing (A), the direct product, which ignores the shift; (D) has only five carbons and fails the carbon count before any mechanism is needed

Chapter Summary, Revision Checklist and Self-Assessment
• SUMMARY — This chapter is three families sharing one oxygen. Alcohols are organised by the class of the carbinol carbon: hydration of alkenes forks between Markovnikov (acid, cation, rearrangements) and anti-Markovnikov (hydroboration, concerted); Grignard plus carbonyl builds any class on demand; acidity runs 1 > 2 > 3 degree while HX reactivity, Lucas timings and dehydration ease run 3 > 2 > 1 degree; and the oxidation ladder has a gate (PCC) and a ceiling that depends on class — aldehyde then acid for primary, ketone for secondary, refusal for tertiary, with hot copper reporting the class in one pass.
• SUMMARY — Phenols are alcohols rewritten by resonance: the ring delocalises the phenoxide charge, buying a million-fold acidity boost (pKa 10 versus 16), a tunable ladder (picric acid 0.38 at the summit), ferocious ortho/para substitution with solvent and strength forks (Br2/CS2 versus bromine water; dilute versus concentrated HNO3), and two named one-carbon installations — Kolbe (COOH from CO2 on the phenoxide) and Reimer-Tiemann (CHO from CHCl3 via dichlorocarbene). Ethers invert the design problem: Williamson succeeds only when the halide side is methyl or primary and the bulky or aryl side rides as alkoxide, and HX cleaves every ether by rules in which the aryl-oxygen bond is untouchable.
✔ REVISION CHECKLIST — Recite the forks as pairs: acid hydration versus hydroboration; PCC versus KMnO4; Br2 in CS2 versus bromine water; dilute versus concentrated HNO3; ethanol at 413 K versus 443 K; KCN-style side assignment in every Williamson design
✔ REVISION CHECKLIST — Keep the two alcohol orders in separate boxes with their intermediates labelled: acidity 1 > 2 > 3 degree (alkoxide), HX and dehydration 3 > 2 > 1 degree (carbocation) — and never answer an order question before naming its reaction
✔ REVISION CHECKLIST — Quote the phenol numbers cold: pKa phenol 10.0, p-nitrophenol 7.15, picric acid 0.38, ethanol about 16; Lucas: tertiary instant, secondary about 5 minutes; cumene's co-product is acetone; Dow needs 623 K and 300 atm (from Chapter 15)
✔ REVISION CHECKLIST — Run the cation audit on every acid-catalysed alcohol reaction: protonate, ionise, check for a hydride or methyl shift that upgrades the cation, then finish with Saytzeff
✔ REVISION CHECKLIST — For every ether, ask the two design questions: which side was the halide (must be methyl or primary, never tertiary, never aryl), and if HX appears, which C-O bond breaks (aryl-O never; tertiary takes X by SN1; otherwise the smaller group takes X by SN2)
✔ REVISION CHECKLIST — Test yourself on the named reactions by reagent string alone: CO2/pressure on phenoxide = Kolbe; CHCl3/KOH = Reimer-Tiemann (CCl4 swaps CHO for COOH); CH2O/RCHO/ketone/ester plus Grignard = the class table
• SELF-ASSESSMENT — Rate yourself honestly: if Worked Examples 1 and 2 feel automatic, your forks and acidity ladder are set. If Examples 3 to 5 are comfortable, class tests, rearrangements and Williamson design are exam-safe. Examples 6 and 7 are the ceiling — conversion sequencing and theatre selection on a two-faced molecule. Finish by redoing the practice set under a timer: eight questions, eight minutes, no notes.
• ONE LAST THING — in this chapter the reagent line and the conditions ARE the question: the same ethanol gives an ether or an alkene across a 30-degree gap, the same phenol takes one bromine or three across a solvent change, and the same ether target lives or dies by which side of the oxygen you build from. Read the conditions twice, find the fork, and the products write themselves.
