Introduction — Amines and the Diazonium Bridge
• Amines are the nitrogen chapter of organic chemistry, and they punch far above their weight in the IAT. The amine group -NH2 looks modest, but it is the most versatile handle in the syllabus: it is a base, a nucleophile, a directing group for the ring, and — once converted to a diazonium salt — a trapdoor through which almost any other group can be installed on a benzene ring. Master this chapter and you own the last big reaction map of Class 12 organic chemistry.
• DEFINITION — An amine is an organic derivative of ammonia in which one or more hydrogen atoms of NH3 are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups. The nitrogen carries a lone pair, and that lone pair is the whole story: it accepts protons (basicity), attacks electrophiles (nucleophilicity), and in aniline it leaks into the benzene ring and changes the ring's chemistry completely.
• EXAMPLE — Nitrogen chemistry runs your body and your pharmacy. Adrenaline, histamine and serotonin are amines; amino acids carry -NH2 beside -COOH; the local anaesthetic benzocaine and the dye industry (aniline dyes, azo colours) are built on aryl amines. When the exam asks about amines, it is asking about the chemistry of life and colour at once.
• The map of the chapter. First, classification and nomenclature — with the trap that the degree of an amine counts substituents on NITROGEN, not carbons. Then preparation: reduction routes, ammonolysis, Gabriel phthalimide and Hoffmann bromamide. Then the physical properties, then the chapter's core — BASICITY, where a three-factor tug-of-war (inductive push, solvation of the cation, steric crowding) produces aqueous orders that defy naive intuition. Then distinguishing tests (carbylamine, Hinsberg, nitrous acid), aniline's over-reactive ring, and finally the diazonium salts — the single most useful intermediate in aromatic synthesis — ending with azo coupling and dyes.
• WHY examiners love amines — the questions run on reasoning, not memory. Rank four bases using the three-factor balance; identify 1°, 2° and 3° amines from test outcomes; choose the preparation that gives ONE pure product; convert aniline into a compound no direct route can reach. Every one of those is a 60-second question with the logic internalised, and a coin flip without it.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Hold one sentence and the chapter organises itself: the lone pair on nitrogen does everything — it is protonated (basicity), alkylated and acylated (nucleophilicity), delocalised in aniline (ring activation), and removed by nitrous acid to install the diazonium group (the universal leaving group). Four behaviours, one pair of electrons.
• YOUR TURN — Predict before reading on: in water, which is the stronger base, trimethylamine (CH3)3N or dimethylamine (CH3)2NH? (Answer: dimethylamine — pKb 3.27 against 4.22. Pure electron-pushing says 3° should win, but in water the 3° cation is badly solvated and sterically crowded, so the secondary amine takes the crown. Section 5 runs the full tug-of-war.)

Classification and Nomenclature of Amines
• DEFINITION — Amines are classified as primary (1°), secondary (2°) or tertiary (3°) by the number of carbon groups attached to the NITROGEN atom. One group on N: 1°, RNH2. Two groups: 2°, R2NH. Three groups: 3°, R3N. The classification counts what nitrogen carries — it says nothing about the carbon skeleton the groups belong to.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The degree of an amine is NOT the degree of its carbon. tert-Butylamine, (CH3)3C-NH2, is a PRIMARY amine: its nitrogen carries exactly one carbon group, even though that group is a tertiary butyl group. The same trap runs in reverse: (CH3)3N is a tertiary amine built entirely from primary methyl groups. Alcohol classification counts C-OH carbon class; amine classification counts N substituents. Keep the two systems in separate boxes.
• DEFINITION — Simple amines are named as ALKANAMINES: the longest chain carrying -NH2 gives the parent, the suffix is -amine, and the locant of -NH2 is kept as low as possible. CH3-CH2-NH2 is ethanamine; CH3-CH(NH2)-CH3 is propan-2-amine; H2N-CH2-CH2-NH2 is ethane-1,2-diamine. The common-name system names them as alkylamines: ethylamine, isopropylamine, tert-butylamine — exam papers use both, so read both fluently.
• Mixed and aromatic naming: in a secondary or tertiary amine with different groups, name them alphabetically as N-substituents — CH3-NH-CH2CH3 is N-methylethanamine; (CH3)2N-CH2CH3 is N,N-dimethylethanamine. The letter N (not a number) marks substitution ON nitrogen. C6H5-NH2 is benzenamine, universally called aniline; CH3-C6H4-NH2 is toluidine (para isomer: p-toluidine, 4-methylaniline); N-methylaniline is C6H5-NH-CH3, and N,N-dimethylaniline is C6H5-N(CH3)2 — both secondary and tertiary ARYL amines respectively.
• When a senior functional group is present, -NH2 demotes to the prefix amino- (seniority from Chapter 13: -COOH > ... > -NH2 > ether). NH2-CH2-CH2-COOH is 3-aminopropanoic acid; the amino acid glycine is 2-aminoacetic acid. -NHR and -NR2 become alkylamino- and dialkylamino- prefixes.
• Quaternary ammonium salts complete the family portrait: four carbon groups on nitrogen leave no lone pair at all, giving a permanent positive charge — (CH3)4N+ Cl- is tetramethylammonium chloride. No lone pair means NO basicity and no nucleophilicity: the salt is a spectator cation, a useful contrast option in ranking questions.
• EXAMPLE — Classify in one pass: (a) CH3NH2 (b) (C2H5)2NH (c) (CH3)3N (d) C6H5NHCH3 (e) (CH3)3CNH2. Answers: (a) 1°, (b) 2°, (c) 3°, (d) 2° aryl-alkyl, (e) 1° despite the tertiary carbon — the nitrogen census is the only census that counts.
• WORKED PATTERN — naming drill, structure to name and back: (CH3)2CH-NH2 reads isopropylamine or propan-2-amine. C6H5-CH2-NH2 is benzylamine (phenylmethanamine) — note carefully: the NH2 sits on a CH2 OUTSIDE the ring, so benzylamine behaves as an ALIPHATIC amine. That single observation decides its basicity in Section 6.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Benzylamine C6H5-CH2-NH2 is an aliphatic amine in aryl clothing: the -CH2- spacer insulates the lone pair from the ring, so its basicity (pKb about 4.70) is ammonia-like, a hundred thousand times stronger than aniline. Examiners plant it beside aniline precisely to test whether you check WHERE the nitrogen is attached.
• YOUR TURN — Name and classify: CH3-CH2-N(CH3)2. (Answer: N,N-dimethylethanamine, a tertiary amine — nitrogen carries three carbon groups: one ethyl, two methyl.)

Preparation I — Reduction Routes to Amines
• The preparation map has a logic: nitrogen enters organic molecules at high oxidation level (nitro, nitrile) or as ammonia, and REDUCTION walks it down to the amine. Four reduction routes and two substitution routes cover the syllabus — this section takes the reductions, the next takes the substitutions and rearrangements.
• ROUTE 1 — Reduction of nitro compounds. DEFINITION — A nitro compound R-NO2 reduced with a strong reducing system takes up hydrogen and loses its oxygens as water, landing on the primary amine R-NH2. Standard reagents: Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl or Zn/HCl, or catalytic hydrogenation H2 over Pt, Pd or Ni. The nitro group is the standard doorway INTO aromatic amine chemistry because nitration installs -NO2 on a ring easily.
• WORKED PATTERN — benzene to aniline, the industrial sequence: benzene is nitrated (conc. HNO3 + conc. H2SO4, 323-333 K) to nitrobenzene, then nitrobenzene is reduced with Fe and dilute HCl (scrap iron and acid — the cheap industrial choice; Sn/HCl is the laboratory standard) to aniline. In industry Fe/HCl wins on cost; in exam answers either metal-acid pair scores. Rule of thumb: to make an aryl amine, nitrate first, reduce second — there is no direct amination of benzene.
• ROUTE 2 — Reduction of nitriles. R-C≡N reduced by H2/Ni or by LiAlH4 gives a primary amine with ONE EXTRA CARBON: R-CH2-NH2. The nitrile carbon becomes the new CH2. This is a chain-ASCENDING route — the standard trick when the target amine has one more carbon than your starting halide (halide -> cyanide with KCN -> reduce).
• ROUTE 3 — Reduction of amides. R-CONH2 with LiAlH4 (or H2/catalyst) gives R-CH2-NH2: the carbonyl carbon survives as a CH2. Note the difference from its famous cousin — LiAlH4 REDUCES the amide keeping all carbons, while Br2/NaOH (Hoffmann, next section) DEGRADES it, throwing one carbon away. Same starting amide, two different amines — a classic options trap.
• Reduction intermediates worth recognising: nitro reduction in neutral medium (Zn dust + NH4Cl) stops at the HYDROXYLAMINE stage (N-phenylhydroxylamine from nitrobenzene); in alkaline medium nitrobenzene couples into azoxybenzene, azobenzene and hydrazobenzene before reaching aniline. The exam rarely goes deeper than naming these stages, but a "Zn/NH4Cl" reagent pair should ring the hydroxylamine bell instantly.
✔ KEY RULE — Reduction route picker: -NO2 to -NH2 keeps the skeleton and suits aromatic amines (nitrate, then Sn/HCl or Fe/HCl); -C≡N to -CH2-NH2 ADDS one carbon; -CONH2 with LiAlH4 keeps all carbons as -CH2-NH2; -CONH2 with Br2/NaOH (Hoffmann) LOSES one carbon. Read the carbon count of the target before choosing the route.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — LiAlH4 is the strong, expensive reducer for nitriles and amides; NaBH4 does NOT reduce amides or nitriles under normal conditions, and H2/Ni does not reduce amides cleanly. Options pairing NaBH4 with an amide reduction are planted errors.
• EXAMPLE — Choose the route: make butan-1-amine from bromopropane. The target has four carbons, the halide three — you need chain ascent. Step 1: CH3CH2CH2Br + KCN -> CH3CH2CH2CN (butanenitrile). Step 2: H2/Ni or LiAlH4 -> CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2, butan-1-amine. Carbon bookkeeping: 3 + 1 (from CN) = 4.
• YOUR TURN — What does CH3-C≡N give on LiAlH4 reduction, and how many carbons does the product have? (Answer: ethanamine CH3-CH2-NH2, two carbons — the nitrile carbon becomes the CH2, so the chain grows by one.)

Preparation II — Ammonolysis, Gabriel and Hoffmann
• ROUTE 4 — Ammonolysis of alkyl halides. DEFINITION — Ammonolysis is the cleavage of a C-X bond by ammonia: R-X + NH3 first gives the 1° amine, but the product amine is itself nucleophilic and attacks more R-X, so the reaction cascades — 1° amine -> 2° amine -> 3° amine -> quaternary ammonium salt R4N+ X-. You get a MIXTURE of all four, plus NH4X. With a large excess of ammonia the 1° amine dominates; with excess halide the quaternary salt does. Either way, separation is a chore.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Ammonolysis is the honest answer to "which preparation gives a mixture?" Excess NH3 improves the 1° yield but never makes ammonolysis a clean route to a single amine. If a question demands a PURE primary amine, ammonolysis and amide reduction compete — and for aliphatic 1° amines the cleanest answer is Gabriel, coming next.
• ROUTE 5 — Gabriel phthalimide synthesis, the precision instrument. Mechanism in words: phthalimide's N-H is acidic; KOH removes it, giving potassium phthalimide, whose nitrogen is a clean nucleophile. It attacks ONE alkyl halide R-X (SN2) to give N-alkylphthalimide — and now the nitrogen is blocked, bonded into the imide, so NO further alkylation can occur. Hydrolysis (or hydrazinolysis with NH2-NH2) releases the primary amine R-NH2, pure, with phthalic acid as the by-product.
✔ KEY RULE — Gabriel makes ONLY pure primary ALIPHATIC amines. It CANNOT make: aniline or any aryl amine (the aryl halide C-X bond refuses SN2 attack — the same inertness as Chapter 15), secondary or tertiary amines (the method installs exactly one R group by design), and it struggles with tertiary alkyl halides (elimination beats substitution). The question "which amine cannot be prepared by Gabriel synthesis?" has one of three answers: aniline, a 2° amine, or a 3° amine.
• ROUTE 6 — Hoffmann bromamide degradation. DEFINITION — A primary amide R-CONH2 treated with Br2 in aqueous or ethanolic NaOH (4 equivalents of base) gives a primary amine R-NH2 with ONE CARBON FEWER — the carbonyl carbon is expelled as carbonate. Mechanism in words: Br2 brominates the amide nitrogen, base strips protons, the R group migrates from carbon to nitrogen as bromide leaves (an isocyanate R-N=C=O forms), and hydrolysis of the isocyanate releases R-NH2 plus CO2. Memorise the ledger: amide carbon count minus one equals amine carbon count.
• WORKED PATTERN — Hoffmann arithmetic: CH3CONH2 (acetamide, 2 C) -> CH3NH2 (methylamine, 1 C). CH3CH2CONH2 (propanamide, 3 C) -> CH3CH2NH2 (ethanamine, 2 C). C6H5CONH2 (benzamide) -> C6H5NH2 (aniline) — yes, Hoffmann is a legitimate route to aniline, running opposite to Gabriel, which cannot make it. The reagent pair Br2/NaOH is sometimes generated in situ from NaOBr; treat NaOBr as the same reaction wearing a name tag.
• Choosing among routes — the decision table in words: want a pure 1° aliphatic amine, one R group, no contamination — Gabriel. Want a 1° amine and can tolerate a carbon loss, or want aniline from benzamide — Hoffmann. Want aniline industrially — nitrate benzene, reduce with Fe/HCl. Want chain +1 — go through the nitrile. Want a 2° or 3° amine — controlled alkylation of a 1° amine (accepting mixture risk) or reduction of the corresponding amide (N-substituted amides reduce to 2° and 3° amines with LiAlH4, keeping carbons).
• EXAMPLE — A synthesis with two defensible answers: prepare ethanamine. Route A (Gabriel): phthalimide + KOH, then CH3CH2Br, then hydrolyse — pure ethanamine. Route B (Hoffmann): propanamide + Br2/NaOH — ethanamine with one carbon thrown away. Route C (nitrile reduction) fails the carbon count: it would ascend, not descend. Choosing between A and B is about atom economy; both score, ammonolysis does not.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The two named syntheses are mirror-image specialists: Gabriel ADDS nothing and protects nitrogen so exactly one aliphatic group lands (pure 1°, aliphatic only); Hoffmann REMOVES one carbon from an amide (any 1° amine, including aniline). Between them, almost every "prepare this amine" question has a one-line answer.
• YOUR TURN — Which named method prepares (a) aniline and (b) pure butan-1-amine from butyl bromide? (Answers: (a) Hoffmann bromamide on benzamide — or nitrate-and-reduce; Gabriel is impossible for aryl halides. (b) Gabriel phthalimide — ammonolysis would contaminate it with di- and tributylamine.)

Physical Properties of Amines
• DEFINITION — The physical personality of an amine comes from one fact: the N-H bond is polar and nitrogen has a lone pair, so primary and secondary amines form HYDROGEN BONDS with each other and with water. Tertiary amines have no N-H bond and cannot hydrogen-bond among themselves — though their lone pair still accepts H-bonds from water.
• Boiling points, the three-way comparison the exam loves. For comparable molar mass: alcohol > 1° amine > 3° amine, and 1° > 2° > 3° within amines. WHY: O-H hydrogen bonds are stronger than N-H ones (oxygen is more electronegative), so alcohols boil highest. Among amines, H-bonding needs N-H bonds: primary has two per molecule, secondary one, tertiary none — so boiling point falls as substitution rises. Data anchors: ethanamine boils at 290 K, N-methylethanamine (2°) lower per H-bond count, and the isomeric alcohol ethanol at 351 K towers over all three.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not rank boiling points by molar mass alone, and do not assume the tertiary amine wins because it is "biggest". (CH3)3N boils at about 276 K, BELOW its isomer propan-1-amine (322 K) — hydrogen bonding beats size. The order asked is almost always: for the same formula, 1° > 2° > 3°.
• Solubility in water: the lower aliphatic amines (up to about four carbons) are miscible or freely soluble — the lone pair accepts a hydrogen bond from water and N-H donates one back. Solubility falls as the hydrocarbon part grows: the hydrophobic tail eventually wins. Aniline is only sparingly soluble (about 3.6 g per 100 mL) — its ring is a large hydrophobic block and its lone pair is busy in the ring (Section 6 again).
• Amines smell fishy, form salts with acids instantly (R-NH2 + HCl -> R-NH3+ Cl-, water-soluble), and that salt formation is a practical purification handle: extract the amine into dilute acid, separate, then release it with NaOH. The ammonium salt is ionic — higher melting, odourless, soluble — the opposite personality of the free amine.
• EXAMPLE — Ethylamine is a gas at room temperature (bp 290 K) while ethanol is a liquid (bp 351 K) and ethane a much colder gas (bp 185 K). One row of data, three ideas: no H-bond (alkane) lowest, N-H bonding (amine) middle, O-H bonding (alcohol) highest. Quote this trio whenever a boiling-point justification is needed.
• YOUR TURN — Arrange by boiling point: (CH3)3N, CH3CH2CH2NH2, CH3CH2NHCH3 (all C3H9N). (Answer: CH3CH2CH2NH2 (1°, two N-H, strongest network) > CH3CH2NHCH3 (2°, one N-H) > (CH3)3N (3°, no N-H, no self H-bonding).)

Basicity I — Aliphatic Amines and the Three-Factor Balance
• DEFINITION — Basicity measures how willingly the lone pair accepts a proton: R-NH2 + H+ -> R-NH3+. It is quantified by the pKb of the base (smaller pKb = stronger base) or the pKa of its conjugate acid (larger = stronger base). Anchors: NH3 has pKb 4.75; aliphatic amines sit near 3.0-3.4; aniline sits at 9.38 — six orders of magnitude weaker. This section explains the aliphatic numbers; the next explains aniline's crash.
• FACTOR 1 — the inductive effect (+I). Alkyl groups push electrons towards nitrogen, raising lone-pair availability and stabilising the positive charge of R-NH3+. Pure +I logic predicts the gas-phase and naive order: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH3. In the GAS PHASE this order is exactly what is observed: (CH3)3N > (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > NH3. ✔ This is the order to quote whenever a question says "in the gaseous phase" or "in chlorobenzene" (a non-solvating solvent).
• FACTOR 2 — solvation of the cation. In WATER the conjugate acid is stabilised by hydrogen bonding to solvent, and the stabilisation scales with the number of N-H bonds the cation carries: R-NH3+ (from a 1° amine) has three H-bonding hydrogens, R2NH2+ has two, R3NH+ has one. Solvation therefore favours the order 1° > 2° > 3° — the exact reverse of the inductive order. Water is not a spectator; it grades the cations.
• FACTOR 3 — steric hindrance. Three bulky groups crowd the nitrogen: they block the approaching proton a little and, more importantly, block the solvent shell that must pack around R3NH+. Sterics tax the tertiary amine twice — less solvation room, more crowding — which is why the 3° amine slides down the aqueous order despite the strongest +I push.
✔ KEY RULE — In WATER, the observed orders are compromises of +I (favours 3°) against solvation and sterics (favour 1°). For the ETHYL set: (C2H5)2NH (pKb 3.00) > (C2H5)3N (3.25) > C2H5NH2 (3.29) > NH3 (4.75). For the METHYL set: (CH3)2NH (3.27) > CH3NH2 (3.38) > (CH3)3N (4.22) > NH3 (4.75). The secondary amine wins both sets; note the ethyl set puts 3° above 1° while the methyl set puts 1° above 3° — the methyl 3° cation is the most poorly solvated of all.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The two sets swap the 1°/3° positions. Methyl: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH3. Ethyl: 2° > 3° > 1° > NH3. A question naming ethylamines wants the ethyl order; answering with the methyl pattern loses the mark. And any question that forgets to mention water expects the GAS-phase order 3° > 2° > 1° — read the phase before ranking.
• WHY the secondary amine wins — it holds the best hand of all three: two +I donors (nearly the inductive strength of 3°), two N-H bonds on its cation (good solvation), and only moderate crowding. Basicity questions are won by arguing the balance, not by reciting a single effect. When an option claims "3° is strongest in water because of +I", that single-factor reasoning is exactly the planted error.
• WORKED PATTERN — ranking any aliphatic set in water: (1) put the 2° amine first (safe anchor); (2) every aliphatic amine beats NH3 (+I always helps against ammonia); (3) split 1° vs 3° by family — ethyl set: 3° > 1°; methyl set: 1° > 3°; (4) mixed alkyl sets follow the same compromise, e.g. (CH3)2NH > (C2H5)2NH comparisons are not asked, but anything vs NH3 is decided by +I.
• EXAMPLE — The numbers behind the methyl set: (CH3)2NH pKb 3.27, CH3NH2 3.38, (CH3)3N 4.22, NH3 4.75. The gap between 2° and 3° is nearly a full pKb unit (about tenfold in Kb) — solvation of (CH3)2NH2+ (two N-H bonds, little crowding) beats the third methyl's +I push. That tenfold gap is the solvation factor measured, not theorised.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Basicity in water is a three-way tug-of-war and the winner is always the compromise candidate: the secondary amine. Learn the two aqueous orders as photographs — methyl: 2 > 1 > 3; ethyl: 2 > 3 > 1; both end with NH3 last — and attach the three reasons (+I, solvation, steric) so you can defend any ranking the paper invents.
• YOUR TURN — Rank in water: NH3, C2H5NH2, (C2H5)2NH, (C2H5)3N. (Answer: (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > NH3 — the ethyl order: secondary first, then tertiary edging primary on stronger +I with still-tolerable solvation, ammonia last.)

Basicity II — Aniline and the Aromatic Crash
• DEFINITION — An aromatic amine has nitrogen attached DIRECTLY to the ring (aniline, toluidines, N-alkylanilines). Its basicity collapses: aniline's pKb is 9.38 against about 3.3 for aliphatic amines — aniline is roughly a MILLION times weaker as a base. Understanding that crash, and how ring substituents soften or deepen it, is this chapter's signature question.
• WHY the crash — resonance. In aniline the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the ring's pi system: contributing structures place the pair in the ring with positive charge on nitrogen and negative charge at the ortho and para positions. The pair that should grab a proton is spread over four atoms — barely available. Worse, protonation DESTROYS the resonance: the anilinium ion C6H5-NH3+ has no lone pair left to delocalise, so the base form is resonance-stabilised while the acid form is not. Both effects — pair delocalised, resonance lost on protonation — tax basicity, and aniline pays twice.
• The contrast that proves the mechanism: benzylamine C6H5-CH2-NH2 has pKb about 4.70 (ammonia-like) because the CH2 spacer cuts the conjugation — same ring, same NH2, one carbon of insulation, a hundred-thousand-fold difference in Kb. And cyclohexylamine (no pi system at all) is a normal strong aliphatic base near pKb 3.3. Whenever ring-plus-nitrogen appears, ask: is the lone pair conjugated with the ring? Attached directly = conjugated = weak.
• Substituent effects on aniline — read them through the lone pair. Electron-DONATING groups (+I, +R: -CH3, -OCH3) push electron density towards the ring and the nitrogen, making the pair slightly more available: p-toluidine is a STRONGER base than aniline; p-anisidine (p-methoxyaniline) is stronger still. Electron-WITHDRAWING groups (-I, -R: -NO2, halogens, -CN) drain the ring and the pair: p-nitroaniline is far weaker than aniline (pKb about 13.00), weaker by nearly four orders of magnitude — the -NO2 group withdraws by both -I and resonance, the heavy anchor of Chapter 13.
✔ KEY RULE — The grand basicity ladder, strongest to weakest: aliphatic 2° > aliphatic 3°/1° (set-dependent) > NH3 approximately equal to benzylamine > p-toluidine > aniline > p-nitroaniline. In exam shorthand: aliphatic amines > ammonia > substituted anilines (donor-substituted first) > aniline > acceptor-substituted anilines. Amides sit below everything — an amide nitrogen's pair is delocalised into C=O, so amides are essentially non-basic; never rank an amide above any amine.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two classics. First: N,N-dimethylaniline vs aniline — the two N-methyls add +I, so it IS a stronger base than aniline, but it remains an aromatic amine, still far weaker than any aliphatic amine; options promoting it above ethylamine are traps. Second: "aniline does not undergo Friedel-Crafts" and "aniline is a weak base" are the SAME fact — the lone pair is committed to the ring and is grabbed by Lewis acids and protons alike.
• The anilinium twist — protonation flips the ring's personality. Free aniline's -NH2 is a powerful ACTIVATING, ortho/para-directing group. In strong acid, aniline becomes the anilinium ion -NH3+, which is DEACTIVATING and META-directing (a positively charged group withdraws hard). Practical fallout: nitration of aniline in strong acid gives a large share (about 47 per cent) of m-nitroaniline alongside the para product — the meta product is the fingerprint of the anilinium ion, and examiners ask for exactly this explanation.
• WORKED PATTERN — to rank aromatic bases: (1) any aliphatic amine beats any aromatic one; (2) among anilines, donor groups at para raise, acceptor groups lower; (3) para effects are strongest (resonance reaches para), meta effects are inductive-only; (4) the more -NO2 groups, the weaker — picramide (2,4,6-trinitroaniline) is barely a base at all.
• EXAMPLE — Full ranking drill: (C2H5)2NH, NH3, p-toluidine, aniline, p-nitroaniline. Answer: (C2H5)2NH (pKb 3.00, aliphatic 2°) > NH3 (4.75) > p-toluidine (methyl donates, aniline softened) > aniline (9.38) > p-nitroaniline (about 13.00). Five compounds, three decisions: aliphatic first, ammonia next, then the aniline family sorted by substituent.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Aniline's weakness is not a defect to memorise but a trade to understand: the lone pair is INVESTED in the ring. That investment buys a hyper-reactive ring (Section 9: bromine water instantly gives the tribromo product) and sells basicity. Every aniline question — basicity, nitration's meta product, Friedel-Crafts failure — is this one trade viewed from a different window.
• YOUR TURN — Which is the stronger base, p-anisidine (p-CH3O-C6H4-NH2) or p-chloroaniline, and why? (Answer: p-anisidine — methoxy donates by resonance (+R) into the ring, enriching the nitrogen; chlorine withdraws (-I dominates for basicity here) and impoverishes it.)

Reactions I — Alkylation, Acylation and the Carbylamine Test
• The lone pair makes every amine a nucleophile, and the first three reactions are the lone pair at work: it attacks alkyl halides (alkylation), acyl groups (acylation), and chloroform/base in the carbylamine test. Watch what each reaction does to the degree of the amine — that is how the exam interrogates them.
• ALKYLATION. DEFINITION — An amine reacts with an alkyl halide by SN2: the lone pair attacks carbon, halide leaves, and the amine gains one alkyl group — 1° becomes 2°, 2° becomes 3°, 3° becomes the quaternary ammonium salt. It is ammonolysis run from the amine side, with the same cascade problem: stopping at one alkylation is hard because each product is still nucleophilic (often more than the starting amine). With a controlled single equivalent and care, mono-alkylation can be favoured, but the exam's default verdict is "mixture, quaternary salt with excess".
• ACYLATION. DEFINITION — Amines react with acid chlorides and anhydrides, replacing one N-H hydrogen with an acyl group to give an AMIDE. Aniline + acetic anhydride -> acetanilide (plus acetic acid); aniline + benzoyl chloride in aqueous NaOH -> benzanilide — this second one carries a name: the SCHOTTEN-BAUMANN reaction (benzoylation of amines and phenols in alkali). Aliphatic amines acylate just as readily: ethanamine + acetyl chloride -> N-ethylacetamide.
• WHY acylation matters strategically — the acyl group tames the amine. An amide nitrogen's lone pair is delocalised into the carbonyl, so acetanilide is (a) barely basic, (b) far less activating towards ring substitution than aniline, and (c) protected against oxidation. Converting -NH2 to -NHCOCH3, doing ring chemistry at controlled speed, then hydrolysing the amide back to -NH2 is THE standard protection strategy of aromatic synthesis — Section 9 uses it to monobrominate aniline.
✔ KEY RULE — Alkylation raises the amine's degree and refuses to stop (quaternary salt is the end of the line); acylation converts the amine into an amide and switches its reactivity OFF. A tertiary amine cannot be acylated (no N-H to replace, and no stable product) — it can only be alkylated, to the quaternary salt.
• CARBYLAMINE REACTION — the smell test for primary amines. DEFINITION — A primary amine (aliphatic OR aromatic) warmed with chloroform CHCl3 and alcoholic KOH gives an isocyanide (carbylamine), R-N≡C, with an unbearably foul smell. In words: base strips chloroform to dichlorocarbene :CCl2, the amine nitrogen attacks it twice with HCl eliminations, landing on the isocyanide. Only 1° amines can play — the mechanism needs two N-H hydrogens to eliminate. Secondary and tertiary amines give NO reaction.
• The carbylamine test is therefore a binary classifier: foul smell = primary amine (aniline passes, ethanamine passes); no smell = 2°, 3°, or not an amine. It is also the standard synthesis-neutral way to PROVE an -NH2 group on a ring before diazotisation enters the story. Bonus fact for one-mark questions: the product's correct name is an isocyanide (isonitrile), carbon attached to nitrogen, NOT a cyanide.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Carbylamine needs the chloroform-ALCOHOLIC KOH pair; aqueous KOH or CHCl3 alone does nothing testable. And the reaction FAILS for 2°/3° amines by mechanism, not by sluggishness — an option claiming N-methylaniline gives carbylamine slowly is wrong in kind, not degree.
• EXAMPLE — Distinguish aniline, N-methylaniline and N,N-dimethylaniline with carbylamine alone? It separates only the first: aniline (1°) gives the foul isocyanide; the other two (2° and 3°) stay silent — and carbylamine cannot tell those two apart. For the full three-way split you need Hinsberg, next section. Knowing a test's blind spot is worth as much as knowing the test.
• YOUR TURN — CH3NH2, (CH3)2NH and (CH3)3N are treated separately with CHCl3 and alcoholic KOH. Which produce a foul smell? (Answer: only CH3NH2 — the sole primary amine. The 2° and 3° amines have too few N-H hydrogens for the double elimination.)

Reactions II — Hinsberg's Test and Nitrous Acid
• Two tests complete the identification toolkit: Hinsberg's sorts 1°, 2° and 3° in a single shake; nitrous acid sorts them by GAS and by a famous coloured sequel for aniline. Together with carbylamine (1° only), they answer every "identify the amine class" question in the syllabus.
• HINSBERG'S TEST. DEFINITION — The amine is shaken with benzene sulphonyl chloride, C6H5SO2Cl (Hinsberg's reagent), in aqueous KOH. The sulphonamide that forms carries the verdict. PRIMARY amine: forms N-alkylbenzene sulphonamide, which still has an acidic N-H (the sulphonyl group makes it acidic) — it DISSOLVES in KOH as its salt. SECONDARY amine: forms N,N-dialkylbenzene sulphonamide with NO N-H — it does NOT dissolve in KOH and stays a solid precipitate. TERTIARY amine: does not react at all — no sulphonamide forms, and the amine itself dissolves in dilute acid on workup.
✔ KEY RULE — Hinsberg verdict table in one line each: 1° — sulphonamide forms AND dissolves in KOH (acidic N-H present); 2° — sulphonamide forms but does NOT dissolve (no N-H); 3° — no reaction. The solubility difference is the whole test: it exists because the 1° sulphonamide's remaining N-H is acidified by the -SO2- group's electron withdrawal.
• Workup logic worth knowing: after the KOH shake, acidifying separates the cast — the 1° sulphonamide's salt stays in the alkaline layer until acidification precipitates it, the 2° sulphonamide never dissolved, and the 3° amine is recovered from the acid wash. Modern variant: p-toluenesulphonyl chloride (tosyl chloride, TsCl) is used instead of benzenesulphonyl chloride — same logic, same verdicts; the exam accepts both names.
• NITROUS ACID — the gas test. DEFINITION — Nitrous acid (generated in situ: NaNO2 + dilute HCl) reacts completely differently with each class. PRIMARY ALIPHATIC amine: the amine is diazotised but the alkyl diazonium is hopelessly unstable — it loses NITROGEN GAS immediately (visible effervescence) leaving a carbocation that lands as a messy mixture of alcohols (plus some alkene and rearranged products). R-NH2 + HNO2 -> R-OH (mixture) + N2 up + H2O.
• PRIMARY AROMATIC amine — aniline at 273-278 K does something far more valuable: it forms the BENZENEDIAZONIUM salt, stable at that temperature (next sections live here). Secondary amines (aliphatic and aromatic): give a yellow OILY nitrosamine, R2N-N=O, which separates as an oil and gives Liebermann's nitroso test (green-blue colour with phenol and conc. H2SO4). Tertiary ALIPHATIC amines: merely form a soluble nitrite salt — no gas, no oil. Tertiary AROMATIC amines like N,N-dimethylaniline: undergo ring substitution — para-nitroso product, a green solid (the ring, not the nitrogen, reacts).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Effervescence with NaNO2/HCl" identifies a primary ALIPHATIC amine. Aniline also has -NH2 but gives NO effervescence at 0-5 °C — its diazonium holds together. Students who answer "nitrogen gas" for aniline at ice temperature have merged two different reactions. Temperature is part of the reagent: warm the aniline mixture up and the diazonium DOES decompose — to phenol, with N2 (Section 11).
• WORKED PATTERN — three test tubes, one reagent set: Tube A gives brisk N2 effervescence with NaNO2/HCl at room temperature, Tube B a yellow oil, Tube C nothing visible. Verdicts: A = 1° aliphatic amine (e.g. ethanamine); B = 2° amine (e.g. diethylamine); C = 3° amine (e.g. triethylamine) — or a 1° aromatic amine if kept at 0 °C, which is why the protocol specifies temperature. Tests are read as a panel: carbylamine flags 1°, nitrous acid splits aliphatic 1° from aromatic 1°, Hinsberg splits all three cleanly.
• EXAMPLE — An unknown C3H9N amine gives a sulphonamide with Hinsberg reagent that is insoluble in KOH. Carbylamine: silent. Nitrous acid: yellow oil. All three tests agree: N-H count is one — the compound is a secondary amine, CH3-NH-C2H5 (N-methylethanamine) or its isomer... note C3H9N secondary can only be methylethylamine; the primary alternatives (propan-1-amine, propan-2-amine) are excluded by the Hinsberg insolubility alone.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The three tests triangulate the N-H census: carbylamine counts "two or more N-H on a primary centre" by smell, Hinsberg counts N-H on the SULPHONAMIDE by solubility, nitrous acid counts by outcome — gas, oil, salt or diazonium. One census, three instruments; the exam rotates which instrument it hands you.
• YOUR TURN — An amine forms a sulphonamide that dissolves in KOH, gives the carbylamine smell, and with NaNO2/HCl at 273 K forms a solution that dyes beta-naphthol orange. Classify it. (Answer: primary AROMATIC amine — aniline itself. The dye test is the giveaway: only an aryl diazonium survives to couple.)

Reactions III — Electrophilic Substitution on Aniline
• Aniline's ring is the most reactive aromatic ring in the syllabus: the -NH2 group pours its lone pair into the ring (+R), making ortho and para positions electron-rich and hungry for electrophiles. That sounds like a gift; in practice it is a control problem — the ring reacts too fast and too many times, and this section is about taming it.
• BROMINATION — the emblematic reaction. Aniline + bromine water -> instant white precipitate of 2,4,6-TRIBROMOANILINE: all three activated positions (both ortho, one para) are brominated at once and the bromine water is decolourised. No catalyst, no moderation. This is simultaneously a TEST for aniline (white ppt, decolourisation) and the demonstration that direct substitution cannot stop at mono. Compare phenol, which does the same — the two most activated rings share the trick.
• NITRATION — the anilinium ambush. Direct nitration of aniline with mixed acid misbehaves twice over. First, the strong acid protonates much of the aniline to ANILINIUM, whose -NH3+ group is meta-directing and deactivating — so about 47 per cent of the product is m-nitroaniline, a shocking share for an ortho/para director's ring. Second, nitric acid is an oxidant and aniline is easily oxidised: tarry oxidation products eat the yield. Direct nitration is therefore the wrong road to pure o- or p-nitroaniline.
• SULPHONATION — aniline with concentrated H2SO4 gives sulphanilic acid (p-aminobenzene sulphonic acid) as the major product, through the anilinium hydrogen sulphate baked at 453-473 K. Sulphanilic acid exists as a ZWITTERION (internal salt, +H3N-C6H4-SO3-) — a favourite one-line fact. Friedel-Crafts is the forbidden door: aniline FAILS Friedel-Crafts alkylation and acylation because the lone pair coordinates the AlCl3 catalyst, forming a salt that deactivates the ring — the Lewis acid-base adduct eats the catalyst and the ring's activation together.
✔ KEY RULE — The protection protocol for controlled substitution on aniline: (1) ACETYLATE — aniline + acetic anhydride -> acetanilide. The -NHCOCH3 group is still ortho/para-directing but only moderately activating, and the nitrogen is shielded from oxidation and protonation. (2) SUBSTITUTE — bromination now gives mainly the PARA monobromo product (sterics favour para, reactivity is tamed to one substitution). (3) HYDROLYSE — dilute acid or alkali removes the acetyl group, restoring -NH2. Product: p-bromoaniline, cleanly — a compound direct bromination can never give.
• Why acetylation works, in the language of Chapter 13: the acetyl carbonyl competes for the nitrogen lone pair (amide resonance), so less of the pair is available to the ring. Activation drops from violent to manageable; direction stays ortho/para because the pair still donates, just less. The same trick protects phenol chemistry elsewhere — protection by acylation is a transferable strategy, not an aniline curiosity.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not write that -NH2 is meta-directing, and do not write that acetylation reverses direction. -NH2 and -NHCOCH3 are BOTH ortho/para directors; protection changes the RATE and the COUNT of substitutions, not their positions. The meta product in nitration comes from the anilinium ION, a different species entirely.
• EXAMPLE — Target synthesis: p-nitroaniline from aniline. Route: acetylate to acetanilide; nitrate (the acetamido group directs para, ring survives the acid because the nitrogen is protected and less protonated); hydrolyse the amide. Skipping protection gives the meta-contaminated, oxidised mess described above. Three steps, one idea: protect, substitute, deprotect.
• WORKED PATTERN — predicting an aniline substitution outcome: ask three questions in order. (1) Is the medium strongly acidic? If yes, a large anilinium fraction drags product towards meta. (2) Is the nitrogen protected? If yes, expect clean mono-substitution, para major. (3) Is the electrophile bromine water? If yes and nitrogen is free, expect 2,4,6-tribromo, instantly, as a white precipitate.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Aniline chemistry is a volume knob, not a switch: free -NH2 is volume eleven (tribromo, oxidation, anilinium side-products), -NHCOCH3 is volume five (clean para mono-substitution), -NH3+ is a different station altogether (meta). Every exam question about aniline substitution is really asking: what is the knob set to?
• YOUR TURN — How would you prepare o-bromoaniline as the major product from aniline? (Answer: acetylate, brominate — para dominates, so block para first: sulphonate acetanilide to fill the para position, brominate (ortho to the protected NH2), then hydrolyse both the acetyl and the sulphonic acid groups; desulphonation with steam removes the blocker. Accept the simpler verdict: ortho-major needs a para blocker — direct routes give para or tribromo.)

Diazonium Salts — Diazotisation and Stability
• DEFINITION — A diazonium salt carries the group -N2+ (written -N≡N+) attached to carbon, with a counter-anion: benzenediazonium chloride is C6H5-N2+ Cl-. The diazonium group is organic chemistry's best leaving group — molecular nitrogen, the most stable small molecule there is, departs and takes the reaction's thermodynamics with it. Everything in the next two sections is powered by N2's eagerness to leave.
• DIAZOTISATION — the preparation, with conditions that are part of the reaction's name. Aniline is dissolved in dilute HCl, cooled to 273-278 K (0-5 °C, ice bath), and treated with aqueous NaNO2. Nitrous acid generated in situ converts -NH2 into -N2+ Cl-: C6H5NH2 + NaNO2 + 2HCl -> C6H5N2+ Cl- + NaCl + 2H2O. The cold is not a detail: above about 283 K the product hydrolyses to phenol. Excess mineral acid is kept present to stop the diazonium coupling with unreacted aniline.
✔ KEY RULE — Diazotisation conditions, recited as one breath: NaNO2, dilute HCl, 273-278 K, use the solution immediately and in situ. Diazonium salts are almost never isolated — they are explosive when dry and decompose in solution on warming. The exam treats "0-5 °C" as part of the reagent; an answer that diazotises at room temperature is wrong even with the right chemicals.
• WHY aryl diazonium survives (briefly) and alkyl diazonium does not — resonance. In benzenediazonium, the ring's pi system conjugates with the -N2+ group: contributing structures spread the positive charge into the ring, giving the C-N bond partial double-bond character and buying stability at low temperature. An alkyl diazonium has no pi system to share the charge: R-N2+ simply ejects N2 the instant it forms, leaving a carbocation — which is why primary aliphatic amines plus HNO2 give alcohol mixtures with nitrogen effervescence (Section 8), and why there is no alkyl-diazonium chemistry to learn.
• The diazonium solution is a loaded spring: keep it cold and it waits; warm it, shine light, add a reducing agent or a nucleophile, and N2 leaves, installing whatever the conditions offer. That menu — the replacement reactions — is the next section, and it is the main reason aniline is the gateway to half the aromatic compounds in the syllabus.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Diazonium salts of aniline derivatives follow the same rules (toluidines, nitroanilines all diazotise), but the base must be a PRIMARY AROMATIC amine. Secondary aromatic amines (N-methylaniline) with HNO2 give N-nitroso compounds, not diazonium salts; tertiary aromatic amines give ring nitrosation. An option diazotising N-methylaniline has failed the N-H census again.
• EXAMPLE — Order of operations in a real sequence: to convert aniline into iodobenzene, you do NOT reach for I2 and aniline (no reaction — iodine does not substitute the ring's H usefully and never replaces NH2). You diazotise (NaNO2/HCl, 0-5 °C), then add KI and warm gently: C6H5N2+ Cl- + KI -> C6H5I + N2 + KCl. The amino group leaves as nitrogen gas, iodine takes its seat. Two steps, and the second step's driving force is entirely N2.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Remember the asymmetry: -NH2 is installed on a ring with difficulty (nitrate, then reduce), but once present it converts to -N2+, and -N2+ converts to almost anything. Synthetic planning flows through this bottleneck: ring -> NO2 -> NH2 -> N2+ -> target. That four-node chain is the backbone of aromatic synthesis questions.
• YOUR TURN — Why must the diazotisation bath stay at 273-278 K rather than, say, 298 K? (Answer: the product is thermally fragile — warming decomposes benzenediazonium chloride with water to phenol and N2. At 0-5 °C decomposition is slow enough that the salt survives to be used; at room temperature your yield quietly becomes phenol.)

Diazonium Salts — The Replacement Reactions
• This section is the synthetic menu: one diazonium solution, eight destinations. Learn it as a map (Figure 3 draws it) — every spoke replaces -N2+ with a new group and releases N2. The strategic point: several of these groups (I, F, CN, H) CANNOT be installed on a benzene ring by direct substitution at all — the diazonium route is not one option among many, it is the only road.
• HALOGENS. Sandmeyer reaction: CuCl/HCl gives chlorobenzene; CuBr/HBr gives bromobenzene; CuCN/KCN gives benzonitrile (a nitrile — the gateway to benzoic acid by hydrolysis, or to amines by reduction). The catalyst is cuprous (Cu(I)); freshly prepared CuCl is the textbook reagent. Gattermann reaction: copper POWDER with HCl or HBr reaches the same chloro/bromo products, usually in lower yield — know both names and which metal form each uses (Sandmeyer = Cu(I) salt, Gattermann = Cu metal).
• IODINE and FLUORINE — the specials. Iodobenzene: just warm the diazonium solution with KI — no copper needed (iodide is reducing enough to do the job alone). Fluorobenzene: add HBF4 (fluoroboric acid) — the diazonium fluoroborate precipitates, is dried and heated, decomposing to fluorobenzene + N2 + BF3: the BALZ-SCHIEMANN reaction. Fluorine and iodine, the two halogens direct halogenation handles worst, are exactly the two with bespoke diazonium routes — the exam tests this pairing relentlessly.
• HYDROGEN — deamination. Reducing agents strip the diazonium group off entirely and leave plain benzene: hypophosphorous acid H3PO2 with water, or ethanol (which is itself oxidised to ethanal). C6H5N2+ -> C6H6. Why remove a group you worked to install? Because -NH2 is a powerful DIRECTOR: use it to steer substituents into place, then erase it. Classic pattern: make 1,3,5-tribromobenzene — impossible by direct bromination of benzene (bromine directs 1,2,4 patterns wrongly) — by brominating aniline to 2,4,6-tribromoaniline, diazotising, and deaminating with H3PO2. The amino group is scaffolding: build with it, then take it down.
• HYDROXYL — phenol. Warming the aqueous diazonium solution (with dilute H2SO4) hydrolyses it: C6H5N2+ + H2O -> C6H5OH + N2 + H+. This is also the decomposition you fight during diazotisation — the same reaction, villain at 278 K, hero at 323 K when phenol is the target. And the NITRO group can be installed too: diazonium + NaNO2 with copper (or via the fluoroborate with nitrite) gives nitrobenzene — a niche spoke, worth recognising.
✔ KEY RULE — The replacement menu, reagent by spoke: Cl — CuCl/HCl (Sandmeyer); Br — CuBr/HBr (Sandmeyer); CN — CuCN/KCN (Sandmeyer); Cl or Br — Cu powder/HX (Gattermann); I — KI, warm; F — HBF4 then heat (Balz-Schiemann); H — H3PO2/H2O or ethanol; OH — warm aqueous acid (hydrolysis). Every spoke releases N2. If a target arene carries I, F, CN or a pattern only a director-then-erase can build, plan through -N2+ automatically.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Sandmeyer's CN spoke gives benzonitrile, C6H5-C≡N — count the carbon: the nitrile carbon is NEW, added by the reagent, so hydrolysis gives benzoic acid with the COOH carbon being the former nitrile carbon. Students who think the CN carbon is a ring carbon miscount every downstream formula. Also, Gattermann uses copper powder, Sandmeyer cuprous salts — swapping the names is a planted one-mark error.
• WORKED PATTERN — synthesis planning through the hub: target p-tolyl cyanide (4-methylbenzonitrile) from p-toluidine. Diazotise p-toluidine (NaNO2/HCl, 0-5 °C), then CuCN/KCN (Sandmeyer) -> p-CH3-C6H4-CN. Target fluorobenzene from aniline: diazotise, HBF4, isolate the fluoroborate, heat. Target benzene from aniline: diazotise, H3PO2. One hub, three destinations, zero hesitation — that fluency is the goal.
• EXAMPLE — The full scaffolding masterpiece: 1,3,5-tribromobenzene from benzene. Step 1: nitrate benzene, reduce (Fe/HCl) to aniline. Step 2: bromine water — 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (the ring's three activated positions fill; symmetry does the rest). Step 3: diazotise (NaNO2/HCl, 273-278 K). Step 4: H3PO2/H2O removes -N2+ as N2, leaving H. The three bromines now sit at 1,3,5 on a bare ring — a substitution pattern no direct route can draw. If an exam shows this sequence, name the strategy: director as scaffolding.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Diazonium replacement reactions are valued in proportion to what direct substitution cannot do. Chlorination and bromination of rings exist anyway (FeCl3, FeBr3); the diazonium versions matter because the SAME intermediate also delivers I, F, CN, OH and H. One intermediate, eight exits — learn the hub and the spokes come free.
• YOUR TURN — Starting from aniline, choose reagents for: (a) chlorobenzene (b) iodobenzene (c) benzene (d) phenol. (Answers: (a) diazotise, then CuCl/HCl; (b) diazotise, then KI and warm; (c) diazotise, then H3PO2/H2O; (d) diazotise cold, then warm the aqueous solution — same intermediate, four spokes.)

Coupling Reactions — Azo Dyes
• DEFINITION — Coupling is the diazonium ion's one reaction that KEEPS both nitrogens: the diazonium ion acts as an ELECTROPHILE and attacks an electron-rich aromatic ring — phenol or aniline — at its para position (ortho if para is blocked), forming an AZO compound, Ar-N=N-Ar', with the intensely coloured -N=N- (azo) linkage. Coupling is electrophilic aromatic substitution where the electrophile is weak — so it only succeeds on strongly activated rings.
• With PHENOL: benzenediazonium chloride + phenol in weakly alkaline medium (NaOH, pH about 9-10) at 273-278 K gives p-HYDROXYAZOBENZENE — an orange dye. The alkali converts phenol to phenoxide, an even stronger activator. With beta-NAPHTHOL (2-naphthol) in NaOH, the coupling lands on the position next to the -OH of the naphthol ring, giving a brilliant RED dye (the classic Sudan-type colour) — beta-naphthol coupling is the standard TEST for a diazonium solution and for primary aromatic amines (Section 8's dye verdict).
• With ANILINE: coupling in weakly ACIDIC medium (pH about 4-5) gives p-AMINOAZOBENZENE, a yellow dye. The pH windows are chemistry, not decoration: too acidic and aniline is protonated to unreactive anilinium (and phenol coupling slows); too alkaline and the diazonium ion converts to diazotate (Ar-N=N-O-), which is NOT electrophilic and cannot couple. Each partner has its window — phenol mildly alkaline, aniline mildly acidic — and exam questions quote the windows.
✔ KEY RULE — Coupling conditions card: diazonium solution kept at 273-278 K; partner ring must be strongly activated (phenol, naphthol, aniline); attack goes PARA to the -OH or -NH2 (ortho only if para is occupied); phenol couples at pH 9-10, aniline at pH 4-5. Products: azo dyes — p-hydroxyazobenzene (orange), benzenediazonium + beta-naphthol (red), p-aminoazobenzene (yellow).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Coupling does NOT occur with benzene, nitrobenzene or other ordinary/deactivated rings — the diazonium ion is too weak an electrophile. An option coupling benzenediazonium chloride with benzene itself is testing exactly this. Conversely, the diazonium component should ideally carry electron-withdrawing groups (a p-NO2 makes it a hungrier electrophile and coupling faster) — direction of effects matters on BOTH partners.
• WHY azo compounds are dyes: the -N=N- bridge conjugates the two rings into one long pi system; absorption shifts into the visible and the colours are intense. Azo dyes are the largest class of synthetic dyes — methyl orange (an acid-base indicator, itself made by coupling diazotised sulphanilic acid with N,N-dimethylaniline) and congo red are family members. Methyl orange closes a loop in this chapter: sulphanilic acid came from aniline's sulphonation in Section 9.
• EXAMPLE — The diazo test cascade, a full identification: an unknown primary aromatic amine is diazotised at 0-5 °C and the solution added to alkaline beta-naphthol — an intense red azo dye forms. Verdict chain: the compound was a 1° aromatic amine (diazotisable), its diazonium survived (cold, aryl), and it coupled (beta-naphthol activated). This two-step test is the standard laboratory proof of a primary aromatic amine — Hinsberg said "primary", the dye says "aromatic primary".
• WORKED PATTERN — predicting a coupling product: (1) confirm the diazo component (Ar-N2+) and the coupling component (activated Ar'H); (2) find the position para to the activator on the coupling ring — draw the bond there; (3) join as Ar-N=N-Ar'(para) and name the colour family: phenol partner = orange hydroxyazo, beta-naphthol = red, aniline partner = yellow aminoazo. If para is blocked, couple ortho; if both ortho and para are blocked, no coupling.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The diazonium group has two personalities: in replacement reactions it LEAVES as N2 (the best leaving group), in coupling it STAYS as the azo bridge (the best chromophore). Same intermediate — destroy it for synthesis, preserve it for colour. The conditions decide which personality shows up.
• YOUR TURN — Benzenediazonium chloride is mixed with phenol at pH 10 and 275 K, and separately with nitrobenzene under the same conditions. Outcomes? (Answer: with phenol — orange p-hydroxyazobenzene forms by para coupling; with nitrobenzene — nothing: the ring is deactivated and the weak diazonium electrophile cannot attack it.)

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• MISTAKE 1 — Calling tert-butylamine a tertiary amine. Why it is wrong: the label counts groups on nitrogen, and (CH3)3C-NH2 has exactly one. The correct principle: amine degree = number of carbon groups bonded to N; carbon class of those groups is irrelevant — (CH3)3C-NH2 is 1°, (CH3)3N is 3°.
• MISTAKE 2 — Ranking aqueous basicity by the inductive effect alone (3° > 2° > 1° in water). Why it is wrong: that is the GAS-phase order; in water the conjugate acid's solvation (N-H count) and steric crowding reverse the tail. The correct principle: in water, quote methyl set 2° > 1° > 3° > NH3 and ethyl set 2° > 3° > 1° > NH3 — secondary wins both, and the 1°/3° order swaps between the sets.
• MISTAKE 3 — Using Gabriel synthesis for aniline or for a secondary amine. Why it is wrong: Gabriel needs an SN2 attack on an alkyl halide — aryl C-X bonds refuse it — and the design installs exactly one R group. The correct principle: Gabriel = pure 1° aliphatic amines only; aniline comes from nitrobenzene reduction or Hoffmann on benzamide; 2°/3° amines come from controlled alkylation or amide reduction.
• MISTAKE 4 — Forgetting the carbon ledger in Hoffmann bromamide. Why it is wrong: the carbonyl carbon leaves as carbonate, so propanamide gives ETHANamine, not propanamine. The correct principle: amine carbons = amide carbons minus one; contrast LiAlH4 reduction of the same amide, which keeps every carbon (propanamide -> propan-1-amine).
• MISTAKE 5 — Saying aniline gives nitrogen effervescence with NaNO2/HCl. Why it is wrong: at 273-278 K aniline's diazonium salt is resonance-stabilised and stays in solution; effervescence with alcohol formation is the PRIMARY ALIPHATIC response, whose diazonium cannot survive. The correct principle: gas = alkyl 1° amine; silent cold solution (that later couples) = aryl 1° amine.
• MISTAKE 6 — Expecting mono-bromination from aniline and bromine water. Why it is wrong: free -NH2 activates all ortho and para positions simultaneously — the product is 2,4,6-tribromoaniline, a white precipitate. The correct principle: for mono-substitution, protect first (acetanilide), substitute (para major), hydrolyse back.
• MISTAKE 7 — Blaming meta-nitration of aniline on the -NH2 group. Why it is wrong: -NH2 is ortho/para-directing; the meta product comes from the ANILINIUM ion -NH3+ (deactivating, meta-directing) formed in the strong acid medium, about 47 per cent of the mixture. The correct principle: in strong acid, ask which species is actually in the flask — free base or its conjugate acid — before applying directing rules.
• MISTAKE 8 — Reading Hinsberg solubility backwards. Why it is wrong: students dissolve the wrong sulphonamide. The 1° product keeps an acidic N-H (acidified by -SO2-) and dissolves in KOH; the 2° product has no N-H and stays insoluble; the 3° amine never reacts. The correct principle: solubility reports the surviving N-H count on the sulphonamide.
• MISTAKE 9 — Attempting Friedel-Crafts on aniline. Why it is wrong: AlCl3 is a Lewis acid and the lone pair is a Lewis base — they form a salt, the ring is deactivated (the nitrogen is now positively charged), and no alkylation or acylation occurs. The correct principle: protect the nitrogen (acetanilide) or choose another route; free aniline and strong Lewis acids never share a flask in a correct answer.
• MISTAKE 10 — Isolating or warming a diazonium salt casually. Why it is wrong: dry diazonium salts are explosive and warm solutions hydrolyse to phenol with loss of N2. The correct principle: prepare at 273-278 K, keep in solution, use immediately — the exception is the diazonium fluoroborate of the Balz-Schiemann reaction, which is deliberately precipitated and heated DRY by design, the one sanctioned isolation in the syllabus.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• EXAMPLE 1 (basicity ranking, aromatic set) — Problem: arrange aniline, p-toluidine, p-nitroaniline and N,N-dimethylaniline in decreasing basic strength in water. Given: one ring, four nitrogen environments. Principle: resonance availability of the lone pair, adjusted by substituent donation/withdrawal and N-alkylation. Steps: all four are aromatic and feeble next to aliphatic amines. p-Nitroaniline's -NO2 drains the pair by -I and -R — weakest (pKb about 13). Aniline is the baseline (9.38). p-Toluidine's para methyl donates (+I, hyperconjugation) and enriches nitrogen — stronger than aniline. N,N-Dimethylaniline has two methyls donating directly ON nitrogen — the strongest of these four, though still aromatic-class weak. Answer: N,N-dimethylaniline > p-toluidine > aniline > p-nitroaniline. Reasoning: every step on the ladder is one identifiable push or pull on the lone pair. Mistake to avoid: promoting N,N-dimethylaniline above aliphatic amines in a mixed list — its pair is still ring-conjugated.
• EXAMPLE 2 (Hoffmann arithmetic) — Problem: 5.9 g of propanamide is subjected to Hoffmann bromamide degradation. What amine forms, and what mass is obtained at 100 per cent yield? Given: propanamide CH3CH2CONH2, molar mass 73 g per mol; Br2/NaOH conditions. Principle: the amine keeps the R group and loses the carbonyl carbon. Steps: product is ethanamine CH3CH2NH2 (2 carbons, molar mass 45). Moles of amide = 5.9 / 73 = 0.0808 mol; the reaction is 1:1, so moles of amine = 0.0808; mass = 0.0808 x 45 = 3.6 g. Answer: ethanamine, about 3.6 g. Reasoning: the missing 28 mass units per molecule left as carbonate/CO2 — the ledger balances only if you strike the carbonyl carbon. Mistake to avoid: answering propanamine with mass 4.8 g — that is the LiAlH4 reduction product of the same amide, a different reaction.
• EXAMPLE 3 (route selection) — Problem: prepare (a) pure pentan-1-amine, (b) aniline, (c) benzylamine from toluene-side materials, naming the key method for each. Given: purity matters for (a); (b) must not use Gabriel; (c) has the NH2 one carbon off the ring. Principle: match method to structure — Gabriel for pure 1° aliphatic; reduction of nitro for aryl; nitrile reduction or Gabriel via a benzyl halide for benzylamine. Steps: (a) Gabriel: phthalimide/KOH, then 1-bromopentane, then hydrolysis — ammonolysis would mix in di- and tripentylamine. (b) Nitrate benzene, reduce nitrobenzene with Fe/HCl (or Sn/HCl). (c) Benzyl chloride + Gabriel sequence, or toluene -> benzyl cyanide route then LiAlH4 — either way the CH2 spacer is installed before the NH2. Answer: (a) Gabriel, (b) nitration-reduction, (c) Gabriel on benzyl chloride (or nitrile reduction). Reasoning: three targets that look alike (1° amines all) demand three different methods — structure, not habit, chooses. Mistake to avoid: Gabriel for aniline — aryl halides do not undergo the SN2 the method is built on.
• EXAMPLE 4 (the three-test tribunal) — Problem: an unknown amine C4H11N gives a foul carbylamine smell, a Hinsberg sulphonamide that dissolves in KOH, and brisk N2 effervescence with NaNO2/HCl at room temperature. Identify its class and list the candidate structures. Given: three test outcomes and a formula. Principle: carbylamine positive + sulphonamide soluble = primary; effervescence (not a stable diazonium) = primary ALIPHATIC. Steps: candidates are the four primary butylamines: butan-1-amine, butan-2-amine, 2-methylpropan-1-amine and tert-butylamine. The tests fix the class; distinguishing among the four needs boiling points or further chemistry. Answer: a primary aliphatic amine — one of the four butylamine isomers. Reasoning: each test independently counts N-H or its consequences; agreement of three instruments is the identification standard. Mistake to avoid: claiming the tests identify tert-butylamine specifically — they identify the CLASS; overclaiming structure from class tests is a marking trap.
• EXAMPLE 5 (protection strategy in synthesis) — Problem: convert aniline into p-bromoaniline as the major product, and into 2,4,6-tribromoaniline. Given: the same starting material, two targets. Principle: free -NH2 over-activates; -NHCOCH3 activates moderately and directs para. Steps for p-bromoaniline: (1) acetic anhydride -> acetanilide; (2) Br2 in acetic acid -> p-bromoacetanilide (major); (3) dilute acid hydrolysis -> p-bromoaniline. For the tribromo target: skip protection — bromine water on aniline directly precipitates 2,4,6-tribromoaniline in one step. Answer: protect-substitute-deprotect for mono-para; direct bromine water for tribromo. Reasoning: the protecting group is a reactivity dial — the two targets differ only in where the dial is set. Mistake to avoid: trying to stop bromine water at one equivalent — the tribromo product is effectively instantaneous and quantitative; stoichiometry cannot tame this ring.
• EXAMPLE 6 (through the diazonium hub) — Problem: convert aniline into (a) fluorobenzene, (b) benzonitrile, (c) benzene, writing reagents for each step. Given: one common intermediate. Principle: diazotise once (NaNO2, dilute HCl, 273-278 K), then choose the spoke. Steps: (a) add HBF4, collect the diazonium fluoroborate, heat it dry — Balz-Schiemann — fluorobenzene. (b) CuCN/KCN, warm — Sandmeyer — benzonitrile. (c) H3PO2 in water — deamination — benzene, the -N2+ replaced by H. Answer: (a) HBF4, heat; (b) CuCN/KCN; (c) H3PO2/H2O — all after NaNO2/HCl at 273-278 K. Reasoning: three products share 90 per cent of their route; only the last reagent differs. Mistake to avoid: attempting fluorobenzene by direct fluorination of benzene — no such practical reaction exists; the diazonium spoke is the only road.
• EXAMPLE 7 (scaffolding — the masterpiece) — Problem: prepare 1,3,5-tribromobenzene from benzene. Given: direct bromination of benzene cannot give the 1,3,5 pattern. Principle: use -NH2 as a temporary, powerful ortho/para director, then erase it through the diazonium group. Steps: (1) HNO3/H2SO4 — nitrobenzene; (2) Fe/HCl — aniline; (3) excess bromine water — 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (positions 2, 4, 6 relative to NH2 are 1, 3, 5 relative to each other once NH2 leaves); (4) NaNO2/HCl, 273-278 K — diazonium; (5) H3PO2/H2O — the diazonium is replaced by H. Answer: 1,3,5-tribromobenzene, via aniline and diazonium scaffolding. Reasoning: the amino group is installed only to be destroyed — its directing effect is borrowed, then the loan is repaid with N2. Mistake to avoid: brominating nitrobenzene or benzene and hoping for symmetry — no direct sequence of halogenations produces the 1,3,5 pattern; the detour is the method.

• Q4. The carbylamine reaction is given by: (A) (CH3)2NH (B) (CH3)3N (C) C6H5NHCH3 (D) C6H5NH2
• Q5. Aniline on treatment with bromine water gives a white precipitate of: (A) p-bromoaniline (B) o-bromoaniline (C) 2,4,6-tribromoaniline (D) anilinium bromide
• Q6. Benzenediazonium chloride warmed with hypophosphorous acid and water gives: (A) phenol (B) benzene (C) chlorobenzene (D) benzoic acid
• Q7. An amine reacts with benzenesulphonyl chloride to give a solid that does NOT dissolve in aqueous KOH. The amine is: (A) CH3NH2 (B) (CH3)2NH (C) C6H5NH2 (D) CH3CH2NH2
• Q8. Benzenediazonium chloride couples with phenol in weakly alkaline medium to give: (A) p-hydroxyazobenzene, an orange dye (B) benzene and nitrogen (C) phenol ether (D) p-aminophenol

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1. (B) — the aqueous ethyl order is (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > NH3: the secondary amine holds the best balance of +I donation, cation solvation and low steric crowding; aniline is nowhere near this company (pKb 9.38). Misconception: (C) wins only in the gas phase, where solvation vanishes and pure +I rules.
• A2. (C) — Gabriel synthesis needs SN2 attack by phthalimide nitrogen on an alkyl halide; chlorobenzene's C-Cl bond has partial double-bond character and refuses substitution, so aniline cannot be made this way. Benzylamine is fine — its halide is benzyl chloride, an aliphatic (and very SN2-willing) halide. Misconception: reading "aromatic amine" and also rejecting benzylamine — the CH2 spacer makes its halide aliphatic in behaviour.
• A3. (B) — Hoffmann bromamide removes the carbonyl carbon as carbonate: butanamide (4 C) -> propanamine (3 C). Misconception: (A) is the product of LiAlH4 reduction of butanamide, which keeps all four carbons — the reagent pair decides whether the carbon ledger balances or drops one.
• A4. (D) — the carbylamine reaction needs a primary amine with two N-H hydrogens to eliminate; aniline qualifies (aromatic primary amines pass), while N-methylaniline is secondary and fails despite its ring -NH- group. Misconception: believing only aliphatic 1° amines respond — aniline is the classic positive example.
• A5. (C) — the free -NH2 group activates both ortho positions and the para position simultaneously; bromine water is consumed instantly and 2,4,6-tribromoaniline precipitates white. Misconception: (A) requires the protection route — acetylate to acetanilide, brominate, hydrolyse; stoichiometry cannot stop the free amine at mono.
• A6. (B) — H3PO2 is a reducing agent that replaces -N2+ by H (deamination), giving benzene. Misconception: (A) phenol is the product of warming with WATER alone (hydrolysis); the presence of the reducing agent changes the spoke entirely.
• A7. (B) — a secondary amine's sulphonamide has no N-H left, so it cannot ionise and stays insoluble in KOH; primary amines (A, C and D) all give alkali-soluble sulphonamides, and a tertiary amine would not react at all. Misconception: assuming aniline behaves specially here — Hinsberg reads the N-H census, not the ring.
• A8. (A) — coupling is electrophilic substitution by the diazonium ion on the activated phenol ring at the para position, in weakly alkaline solution at 273-278 K, giving the orange azo dye p-hydroxyazobenzene. Misconception: (B) confuses coupling with reduction/decomposition — in coupling the -N=N- bridge is preserved, and that bridge is the chromophore.

Chapter Summary, Checklist and Self-Assessment
• SUMMARY — Amines are ammonia's organic descendants, classified by the census of groups on nitrogen. They are prepared by reduction (nitro, nitrile, amide), by ammonolysis (messy), by Gabriel synthesis (pure 1° aliphatic only) and by Hoffmann bromamide (one carbon less). Their basicity in water is a three-factor compromise — inductive donation versus cation solvation versus steric crowding — crowning secondary amines, swapping 1°/3° between the methyl and ethyl sets, and crashing for aniline, whose lone pair is delocalised into the ring and modulated predictably by substituents. The identification panel — carbylamine (1°), Hinsberg (1°/2°/3° by sulphonamide solubility), nitrous acid (gas, oil, salt, diazonium) — reads the N-H census three ways. Aniline's ring is hyper-activated: bromine water tribrominates it, strong acid diverts nitration towards meta through anilinium, and acetyl protection restores control. Diazotisation at 273-278 K converts -NH2 into -N2+, the universal leaving group: Sandmeyer, Gattermann, KI, Balz-Schiemann, deamination and hydrolysis radiate from that hub, and coupling with phenols and anilines preserves the azo bridge to make dyes.
• REVISION CHECKLIST — (1) Classify any amine by its nitrogen census, including the tert-butylamine trap. (2) Recite the four reduction routes and their carbon ledgers. (3) State Gabriel's two exclusions (aryl, 2°/3°) and Hoffmann's minus-one rule with a worked mass example. (4) Rank isomeric amines by boiling point using N-H counts. (5) Produce both aqueous basicity orders (methyl and ethyl sets) with pKb values and defend them with the three factors; give the gas-phase order on demand. (6) Rank aniline derivatives by substituent and explain the anilinium meta twist in nitration. (7) Run all three identification tests on paper for any set of amines. (8) Write the protection route to p-bromoaniline. (9) Diazotise correctly (reagents, 273-278 K) and serve all eight replacement spokes plus coupling with its pH windows.
• SELF-ASSESSMENT — Score yourself honestly: (a) When I see an amine I automatically count substituents on nitrogen, not carbons. (b) Given Br2/NaOH on an amide I subtract one carbon without hesitation — and keep all carbons for LiAlH4. (c) I can explain WHY the secondary amine wins in water using all three factors, not just recite the order. (d) I never rank aniline-class bases against aliphatic ones without the million-fold warning. (e) I match each unknown-amine test to what it actually measures — and know each test's blind spot. (f) Every diazonium question starts in my head with "NaNO2, HCl, 273-278 K" and fans out to the spokes. If any letter wobbles, that section is your next revision stop.
★ CLOSING HIGHLIGHT — One lone pair, four careers: protonated (base), attacking (nucleophile), delocalised (ring activator), and expelled as N2's partner (diazonium gateway). Carry that sentence into the exam and the chapter carries you.
