Introduction — The Carbonyl Family Runs the Exam
• If the IAT organic paper had a capital city, this chapter would be it. Aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids carry more named reactions, more distinguishing tests and more two-step conversions than any other family — and nearly every reaction here grows out of one small structure, the carbonyl group, C=O.
• DEFINITION — An aldehyde carries the -CHO group: a carbonyl carbon bonded to at least one hydrogen, always at the end of a chain (CH3-CHO, C6H5-CHO). A ketone carries C=O between two carbons (CH3-CO-CH3). A carboxylic acid carries -COOH: a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on the same carbon (CH3-COOH). The three are one family on one oxidation ladder — alcohol oxidises to aldehyde, aldehyde oxidises to acid, and a ketone is the side branch that refuses to climb further.
• EXAMPLE — Your nose knows this chapter. Vanillin (vanilla), cinnamaldehyde (cinnamon) and benzaldehyde (bitter almonds) are aldehydes; acetone is the ketone in nail-polish remover; vinegar is dilute ethanoic acid, and the sting of an ant is formic acid — the simplest carboxylic acid, named after ants (formica in Latin).
• Two ideas organise everything that follows. First, the carbonyl carbon is flat, polar and electron-poor, so nucleophiles attack it — that single fact generates additions, tests and reductions. Second, the hydrogens on the carbon NEXT to the carbonyl (the alpha-hydrogens) are unusually acidic, and that generates the aldol family. Attack at the carbonyl, or action at the alpha carbon — classify any new reaction into one of those two boxes and it will make sense.
• The map: structure and naming first, then preparations — four named gateways to aldehydes are guaranteed exam material — then nucleophilic addition in full, the alpha-hydrogen reactions (aldol and Cannizzaro), the great distinguishing tests (Tollens, Fehling, iodoform), reductions, and finally the carboxylic acids with their acidity ladder and their own named reactions (HVZ, Hunsdiecker).
★ HIGHLIGHT — The chapter in one breath: nucleophiles attack the carbonyl carbon because it is delta-positive and flat; aldehydes oxidise at the slightest excuse while ketones refuse; alpha-hydrogens make enolates that build C-C bonds; and carboxylic acids are simply aldehydes that finished oxidising — with an acidity ladder ruled by anion stability.

The Carbonyl Group — Shape, Polarity and Names
• DEFINITION — The carbonyl group is a carbon-oxygen double bond, C=O. The carbon is sp2 hybridised and trigonal planar: its three attachments (two groups plus the oxygen) sit in one plane at about 120 degrees, and the C=O length is about 122 pm — shorter and stronger than a C-O single bond (143 pm). The group is usefully drawn with its resonance partner: C=O alongside C+ -O-, because oxygen drags the pi electrons towards itself.
• WHY polarity runs the show — oxygen is far more electronegative than carbon, so the carbonyl carbon carries a partial positive charge (delta+) and oxygen a partial negative (delta-). The dipole moments of aldehydes and ketones, roughly 2.3 to 2.8 D, dwarf those of ethers and hydrocarbons of similar mass. This permanent delta+ on a flat, exposed carbon is an open invitation to nucleophiles, and the delta- oxygen is ready to accept a proton or a metal ion — the two handles every reaction in this chapter grabs.
• Physical consequences worth quoting: aldehydes and ketones cannot hydrogen-bond to EACH OTHER (no O-H bond), so their boiling points sit between the alkanes/ethers below and the alcohols above. Methanal is a gas (boils near 252 K), ethanal a volatile liquid (near 293 K), propanone boils at 329 K. The small members — methanal, ethanal, propanone — mix with water in all proportions because their oxygen accepts hydrogen bonds from water; solubility then falls steadily as the hydrocarbon part grows.
• Nomenclature, IUPAC line: aldehydes are alkanals and the -CHO carbon is always carbon 1 and is counted IN the parent chain — CH3-CHO is ethanal (two carbons), never methanal-plus-one. Ketones are alkanones, numbered to give the carbonyl the lowest locant: CH3-CO-CH2-CH3 is butan-2-one; a ring ketone starts numbering at the carbonyl (cyclohexanone). Carboxylic acids are alkanoic acids, -COOH carbon again C1.
• Priority rule that examiners love: when several oxygen groups compete, -COOH outranks -CHO outranks C=O. If an aldehyde group shares a molecule with a carboxylic acid, the acid wins the suffix and the aldehyde is named with the prefix oxo- or formyl-. CH3-CO-CH2-CH2-COOH is 4-oxopentanoic acid — an acid first, with a ketone guest.
• Common names you must still recognise on sight: formaldehyde (methanal), acetaldehyde (ethanal), benzaldehyde, acetophenone (C6H5-CO-CH3, IUPAC 1-phenylethanone), benzophenone (C6H5-CO-C6H5), and acetone for propanone — a retained name IUPAC itself allows.
• WORKED PATTERN — Name by checkpoints. CH3-CH2-CO-CH(CH3)2: longest chain through the carbonyl is five carbons, C=O at position 3 from either end, methyl branch at position 2 if you count from the right: 2-methylpentan-3-one. The checkpoint order never changes: find the senior group, give it the lowest locant, then place branches.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two naming ambushes recur every year. One: the aldehyde carbon counts in the chain, so CH3CH2CHO is propanal, not "ethanal with a methyl". Two: benzaldehyde's -CHO carbon is NOT part of the ring numbering — the ring carbon carrying it is C1 and the aldehyde carbon stands outside the count entirely.
• YOUR TURN — Give IUPAC names: (a) CH3-CO-CH2-CH2-CH3, (b) OHC-CH2-CHO, (c) C6H5-CH2-COOH. (Answer: (a) pentan-2-one. (b) propanedial — both ends are aldehydes, both carbons counted. (c) phenylethanoic acid — the COOH carbon is C1, the phenyl sits on C2.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — One structure to carry forward: flat sp2 carbon, delta+ exposed on both faces, oxygen delta- and basic. Every mechanism in this chapter is something attacking that carbon or removing an alpha-hydrogen — there is no third plot.

Making Aldehydes and Ketones I — From Alcohols, Alkenes and Alkynes
• DEFINITION — Carbonyl compounds are the middle rung of the oxidation ladder, so most preparations are controlled climbs or descents: oxidise an alcohol carefully and stop, cut an alkene at its double bond, or hydrate an alkyne.
• Route 1, gentle oxidation of alcohols: a primary alcohol with PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) in dry dichloromethane stops cleanly at the aldehyde. Ethanol gives ethanal and no further. Secondary alcohols with PCC, or with any standard oxidant, give ketones — propan-2-ol gives propanone, and there the climb naturally halts because ketones resist mild oxidation.
• WHY PCC stops — the secret is water, or its absence. Strong AQUEOUS oxidants (acidified K2Cr2O7, KMnO4) do not oxidise the aldehyde directly; they oxidise its HYDRATE, the gem-diol that aldehydes form in water, so a primary alcohol runs straight through to the carboxylic acid. PCC works in a dry solvent where no hydrate forms, so the reaction dies at the aldehyde. Conditions are chemistry: dry equals stop, wet equals run through.
• Route 2, dehydrogenation: pass alcohol vapour over heated copper at 573 K and hydrogen is stripped off — a primary alcohol gives an aldehyde plus H2, a secondary alcohol gives a ketone plus H2. This is an industrial workhorse, clean and catalyst-recycled.
• Route 3, ozonolysis of alkenes (the cleavage from the hydrocarbons chapter): slice the C=C and cap each carbon with oxygen — a =CH-R carbon arrives as an aldehyde, a =C(R)2 carbon as a ketone. 2-Methylbut-2-ene gives propanone plus ethanal. If a synthesis question hands you an alkene and asks for a carbonyl, this is usually the intended knife.
• Route 4, hydration of alkynes (also from hydrocarbons): HgSO4 with dilute H2SO4 at about 333 K adds water Markovnikov-fashion and the enol tautomerises — ethyne alone gives ethanal, every other terminal alkyne gives a methyl ketone (propyne gives propanone).
• Route 5, hydrolysis of gem-dihalides: boil a gem-dichloride with aqueous KOH and the two chlorines are replaced by two OH groups on one carbon; that gem-diol sheds water at once to leave the carbonyl. CH3-CHCl2 gives ethanal; (CH3)2CCl2 gives propanone.
• WORKED PATTERN — Picking the oxidant is a two-question drill. Question one: is the target an aldehyde from a primary alcohol? Then the oxidant must be PCC (or dehydrogenation over Cu). Question two: is the target the acid? Then use excess acidified K2Cr2O7 or KMnO4 and let it run. Options in an exam differ only in this choice — read the TARGET first, then the reagent.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — A tertiary alcohol under these conditions does NOT give a carbonyl compound at all: with acid and heat it dehydrates to an alkene, and with Cu at 573 K it also eliminates rather than dehydrogenates. There is no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon to remove, so oxidation has nothing to grip.
• EXAMPLE — One alcohol, three fates, pure exam fuel. Ethanol over Cu at 573 K: ethanal. Ethanol with PCC: ethanal. Ethanol with excess hot acidified dichromate: ethanoic acid. The substrate never changed — the conditions wrote the answer.
• YOUR TURN — Butan-2-ol is treated with (a) PCC, (b) Cu at 573 K, (c) hot concentrated KMnO4 with forcing conditions. Name the organic product each time. (Answer: (a) butan-2-one. (b) butan-2-one — dehydrogenation of a secondary alcohol. (c) still essentially the ketone under normal conditions; only violent oxidation cleaves its C-C bonds into smaller acids, which is why ketones are called resistant to oxidation.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — The alcohol routes compress to one line: primary plus gentle and dry gives aldehyde, primary plus wet and strong gives acid, secondary gives ketone by every road.

Making Aldehydes and Ketones II — The Named Gateways to Aldehydes
• DEFINITION — Four named reactions exist for one purpose: delivering an ALDEHYDE without overshooting to the acid. Each parks the product at the aldehyde oxidation level by a different trick — a poisoned catalyst, a trapped intermediate, or a protected complex — and the IAT asks them by name, by reagent, and by the substrate each one demands.
• Gateway 1, ROSENMUND reduction: an acyl chloride is hydrogenated over palladium supported on barium sulphate, Pd/BaSO4, deliberately poisoned with sulphur or quinoline. CH3-COCl + H2 -> CH3-CHO + HCl. The poison is the whole point: honest palladium would keep reducing the aldehyde to the alcohol and beyond; the poisoned surface can hydrogenate the very reactive acyl chloride but lacks the strength for the aldehyde.
• Rosenmund limits to memorise: the substrate must be an ACID CHLORIDE (not the acid, not an ester); the product is always an aldehyde; and formaldehyde can never be made this way because formyl chloride, H-COCl, is unstable and does not exist as a shelf reagent.
• Gateway 2, STEPHEN reduction: a nitrile is reduced with stannous chloride and concentrated HCl, SnCl2/HCl, to an imine salt, which on hydrolysis (steam or boiling water) releases the aldehyde. CH3-CH2-C triple-bond N -> CH3-CH2-CHO. Note the bookkeeping: the nitrile carbon becomes the aldehyde carbon, and since nitriles come from alkyl halides plus KCN, the Stephen route lengthens a chain by exactly one carbon and caps it with -CHO.
• Gateway 3, ETARD reaction: toluene is oxidised by chromyl chloride, CrO2Cl2, in CS2 or CCl4. The methyl group is oxidised but the product benzaldehyde is trapped inside a brown chromium complex as it forms; hydrolysis of that complex with water releases benzaldehyde. WHY the trapping matters — free benzaldehyde in the presence of a strong oxidant would race on to benzoic acid; locked in the complex, it never meets the oxidant again.
• Gateway 4, GATTERMANN-KOCH reaction: benzene (or an alkylbenzene) is treated with carbon monoxide and HCl in the presence of anhydrous AlCl3 and a little CuCl, and a formyl group is installed directly on the ring: C6H6 -> C6H5-CHO. The effective electrophile is the formyl cation equivalent, H-C+=O, generated from CO and HCl — this is Friedel-Crafts chemistry with a one-carbon electrophile.
• Gattermann-Koch limits: strongly deactivated rings such as nitrobenzene refuse to react (the electrophile is not strong enough), and phenol/aniline-type rings fail as well. Toluene works and gives mainly the para product, p-tolualdehyde.
• A modern fifth route worth one line: esters and nitriles reduced by DIBAL-H (diisobutylaluminium hydride) at low temperature, then hydrolysed, also stop at aldehydes — the same "gentle hydride, cold flask" philosophy as Rosenmund's poisoned catalyst.
• WORKED PATTERN — Reagent-to-gateway recognition, the exact form exams use. Pd/BaSO4 with H2 means Rosenmund. SnCl2/HCl means Stephen. CrO2Cl2 means Etard. CO plus HCl plus AlCl3 means Gattermann-Koch. Say the gateway name before the product; the product then writes itself, because each gateway has exactly one product type: an aldehyde.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The four gateways are confused in pairs by design. Rosenmund eats acid chlorides; Stephen eats nitriles — swapping them is the classic error. And Etard starts from TOLUENE's methyl group; give it benzene itself and nothing happens, because there is no benzylic methyl to oxidise.
• EXAMPLE — Three-step chain, a favourite full question: bromoethane with KCN gives propanenitrile (chain plus one carbon); Stephen reduction (SnCl2/HCl, then hydrolysis) gives propanal. Two named steps, one extra carbon, aldehyde delivered — chain-extension questions are built exactly like this.
• YOUR TURN — Name the gateway and the product: (a) benzoyl chloride, C6H5-COCl, with H2 over Pd/BaSO4; (b) benzene with CO, HCl, AlCl3; (c) toluene with CrO2Cl2, then water. (Answer: all three give benzaldehyde — (a) Rosenmund, (b) Gattermann-Koch, (c) Etard. Three different doors into the same room; the exam is testing the doors, not the room.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Every aldehyde gateway is a braking device: poison the catalyst (Rosenmund), stop at the imine (Stephen), lock the product in a complex (Etard), or install the formyl group directly (Gattermann-Koch). Learn the brakes and the names stop being trivia.

Making Ketones — Grignard Routes, Friedel-Crafts and Salt Pyrolysis
• DEFINITION — Ketones are built three honest ways: let a nitrile meet a Grignard reagent and hydrolyse the result, acylate an aromatic ring, or bake the calcium salts of carboxylic acids. (Oxidation of secondary alcohols and alkyne hydration from the earlier sections are the other two roads.)
• Route 1, nitrile plus Grignard — the dial-a-ketone machine. A nitrile R-C triple-bond N adds one equivalent of a Grignard reagent R'-MgX across the C triple-bond N to give a ketimine salt; aqueous work-up hydrolyses the C=N to C=O, delivering the ketone R-CO-R'. The nitrile carbon becomes the carbonyl carbon. Choose R and R' independently and any unsymmetrical ketone is reachable — ethanenitrile plus C6H5MgBr gives acetophenone after hydrolysis.
• Route 1b, acyl chloride plus dialkylcadmium: a Grignard reagent converted to a dialkylcadmium, (R)2Cd, reacts with an acyl chloride to give a ketone and stops there, because cadmium reagents are gentler than Grignards. (A Grignard itself would attack the ketone product and run on to a tertiary alcohol — that is precisely the problem cadmium solves.)
• Route 2, Friedel-Crafts acylation: benzene plus an acyl chloride with anhydrous AlCl3 gives an aryl ketone — CH3-COCl gives acetophenone. The electrophile is the acylium ion, which cannot rearrange, and the product ring is deactivated by its own new acyl group, so the reaction stops at one substitution. This is the cleanest aryl-ketone synthesis in the syllabus, and the partner of the Clemmensen reduction you met in hydrocarbons: acylate, then reduce C=O to CH2, and straight-chain alkylbenzenes become available.
• Route 3, dry distillation of calcium carboxylates: heat the calcium salt of a carboxylic acid strongly and it sheds CaCO3 to give a carbonyl compound. The pattern runs on the formate trick: calcium acetate alone gives propanone (acetone); calcium formate alone gives methanal; a MIXTURE of calcium formate with any other calcium carboxylate gives the ALDEHYDE of that other acid — formate plus acetate gives ethanal. The formate partner always donates the hydrogen that makes the product an aldehyde.
• WORKED PATTERN — Predict a salt-pyrolysis product by pairing. Two identical non-formate salts give the symmetrical ketone of that acid. Any pair containing formate gives an aldehyde (the non-formate acid supplies the R group). Two different non-formate salts give a mixture of three ketones in practice — examiners idealise the cross product, symmetrical ones are the clean questions.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Calcium formate plus calcium benzoate gives BENZALDEHYDE, not a ketone — students see two "big" salts and reach for a ketone, forgetting the rule is about the formate's hydrogen, not the partners' size. And the Grignard-plus-nitrile product is a KETONE, not an aldehyde: the nitrile carbon keeps its R group, unlike the Stephen reduction where that same carbon ends up holding hydrogen.
• EXAMPLE — Stephen versus Grignard on one nitrile: propanenitrile with SnCl2/HCl then water gives propanal; propanenitrile with CH3MgBr then water gives butan-2-one. Same starting material, two different carbonyl classes — the reagent chooses the family because it decides what the nitrile carbon is bonded to at the end.
• YOUR TURN — Give two routes to acetophenone. (Answer: (1) Friedel-Crafts — benzene plus CH3-COCl, anhydrous AlCl3. (2) Benzonitrile plus CH3-MgBr in dry ether, then aqueous hydrolysis of the ketimine salt. Both are one-step-once-set-up and both are standard exam answers.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Ketone construction is modular: the nitrile-plus-Grignard route assembles any R-CO-R' from two chosen fragments, Friedel-Crafts owns the aryl ketones, and the calcium salts survive as pattern questions with the formate rule as their key.

Nucleophilic Addition — The Two-Step Mechanism
• DEFINITION — Nucleophilic addition is the signature reaction of the carbonyl group: a nucleophile (Nu-, an electron-pair donor) attacks the delta-positive carbonyl carbon, the C=O pi bond breaks and its electrons settle on oxygen, and protonation of the resulting alkoxide completes the addition. Nothing leaves the molecule — the pi bond is what gets consumed.
• The mechanism in steps. Step 1, attack: the nucleophile approaches the flat carbonyl carbon from either face (both are open — there is no backside problem as in SN2) and bonds to it; the pi electrons move onto oxygen. The carbon changes hybridisation, sp2 planar to sp3 tetrahedral, and the intermediate is an ALKOXIDE — negative charge on oxygen, exactly where electronegativity wants it. This step is slow and rate-determining. Step 2, protonation: the alkoxide pulls a proton from the solvent or from H-Nu, giving the neutral addition product with both Nu and H (or OH) installed.
• WHY the intermediate matters — every addition in this chapter is this same two-step dance with a different nucleophile: CN- gives cyanohydrins, HSO3- gives bisulphite compounds, R- from a Grignard gives alcohols, RO- from an alcohol gives hemiacetals. Learn the steps once, then the menu in the next section is just a list of dancers.
• Figure 1 draws the full sequence — planar carbonyl with its delta charges, the nucleophile arriving as the pi bond breaks, the tetrahedral alkoxide in brackets, then the protonated product — with the reactivity order running along the bottom.
• Reactivity order, the most-asked ranking in the chapter: formaldehyde is the most reactive carbonyl compound of all; then other aldehydes; then ketones, with bulkier ketones trailing. Concretely: HCHO > CH3-CHO > CH3-CH2-CHO > CH3-CO-CH3 > CH3-CO-CH2-CH3. Aromatic carbonyls sit lower than aliphatic ones of similar size: benzaldehyde is less reactive than acetaldehyde, and benzophenone is the sluggard of the family.
• WHY the order — two reasons, both worth quoting. Steric: ketones carry two alkyl groups around the carbonyl carbon, crowding the nucleophile's approach and crowding the tetrahedral product even more (four groups where the flat starting material had three). Electronic: alkyl groups donate electron density (+I effect), shrinking the delta+ on carbon and stabilising the starting carbonyl; aryl groups add resonance donation on top. A group that withdraws does the opposite — chloral, CCl3-CHO, is so reactive it forms a stable hydrate with water.
• Catalysis, both directions: ACID helps weak nucleophiles by protonating the carbonyl oxygen first, making the carbon still more positive (this is how alcohols and ammonia derivatives are coaxed in). BASE helps by manufacturing a stronger nucleophile — a trace of alkali turns feeble HCN into a working supply of CN-. But acid has a limit: too much protonates nitrogen nucleophiles into non-nucleophilic ammonium ions and kills the reaction, a trap the next-but-one section cashes in.
• WORKED PATTERN — Answering any "arrange in reactivity order" question: run three checks in order. Check one, aldehyde beats ketone (one alkyl donor versus two). Check two, smaller alkyl groups beat larger (less steric crowding). Check three, aliphatic beats aromatic (no resonance donation feeding the carbonyl). Three checks, total order, no memorised list needed.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not import SN2 thinking: there is no leaving group and no inversion here, and the intermediate is an alkoxide (negative on OXYGEN), never a carbocation. Options describing nucleophilic addition as passing through a planar carbocation are describing the wrong mechanism entirely.
• YOUR TURN — Arrange towards HCN addition: benzophenone, propanone, methanal, benzaldehyde. (Answer: methanal first (no alkyl donors, no crowding), then benzaldehyde (one aryl donor), then propanone (two alkyl donors), benzophenone last (two aryl groups: maximum resonance stabilisation of the carbonyl plus maximum crowding).)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Addition is decided before it starts: the more positive and the less crowded the carbonyl carbon, the faster the nucleophile lands. Formaldehyde is naked and starving; benzophenone is armoured and fed — the entire reactivity order is those two adjectives graded.

The Addition Menu — HCN, Bisulphite, Grignard and Alcohols
• DEFINITION — Four nucleophiles cover almost every addition the exam tests: cyanide (builds carbon skeletons), bisulphite (separates and purifies), Grignard reagents (build alcohols), and alcohols themselves (build acetals). Each entry below is the same two-step mechanism wearing a different hat.
• Addition of HCN — cyanohydrins. Aldehydes and most ketones add HCN to give cyanohydrins: CH3-CHO + HCN -> CH3-CH(OH)-CN. The catch is catalysis: pure HCN reacts painfully slowly because it is barely ionised, so a trace of base (or KCN) is added to generate CN-. ACID does the opposite — it suppresses CN- formation and stops the reaction. Cyanohydrins are prized because the -CN is a handle: hydrolyse it and you hold an alpha-hydroxy acid; the acetone cyanohydrin route is industrial chemistry.
• Bisulphite addition — the purification artist. Shake an aldehyde (or a methyl ketone, or a small cyclic ketone like cyclohexanone) with saturated aqueous sodium bisulphite and a white crystalline addition compound precipitates. Treat that solid with dilute HCl or sodium carbonate and the pure carbonyl compound is regenerated. WHY it is useful: the carbonyl compound can be fished out of an impure mixture as crystals, washed clean, and released — separation by temporary capture.
• Bisulphite scope is sterics in action: benzophenone and bulky ketones fail completely because the large HSO3- nucleophile cannot reach the crowded carbonyl carbon. The failure list is as examinable as the success list.
• Grignard addition — alcohol manufacture. A Grignard reagent adds its R group to the carbonyl carbon, and aqueous work-up protonates the alkoxide. The product class is set by the carbonyl partner: formaldehyde gives a PRIMARY alcohol (one carbon longer), any other aldehyde gives a SECONDARY alcohol, a ketone gives a TERTIARY alcohol. Propanone plus CH3-MgBr, then water, gives 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tert-butyl alcohol).
• Addition of alcohols — hemiacetals and acetals. An aldehyde plus one molecule of an alcohol, with dry HCl gas as catalyst, gives a hemiacetal (one -OR and one -OH on the same carbon); a second alcohol molecule converts it to an ACETAL (two -OR groups) with loss of water. Ketones are sluggish with ordinary alcohols but react smoothly with ethylene glycol to give cyclic ketals, because the second step then happens inside the same molecule.
• WHY acetals matter beyond the test tube: an acetal is stable to bases, Grignard reagents and reducing agents but is hydrolysed back by dilute aqueous acid — so chemists park a C=O group as an acetal while they do violent chemistry elsewhere in the molecule, then unmask it. Protecting-group logic, and a guaranteed "why" question.
• Addition of water — the ghost addition. Most gem-diols (two OH on one carbon) fall apart instantly back to the carbonyl, but two exceptions are stable enough to bottle: formaldehyde in water exists almost entirely as its hydrate, methylene glycol, and chloral, CCl3-CHO, forms chloral hydrate, a stable crystalline solid — the three chlorines withdraw so strongly that the carbonyl carbon begs for the extra bond.
• WORKED PATTERN — One table to hold in your head: nucleophile CN- gives cyanohydrin; HSO3- gives crystalline bisulphite adduct (aldehydes and methyl ketones); R-MgX then water gives an alcohol (class set by the carbonyl); ROH with dry HCl gives hemiacetal then acetal; H2O gives a hydrate that usually collapses — except formaldehyde and chloral.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two mirror-image traps guard this section. HCN addition needs BASE catalysis, not acid. And the bisulphite test does NOT work for all carbonyls: benzophenone gives no crystals, so "no bisulphite precipitate, therefore no carbonyl group" is a false conclusion — steric failure is not absence.
• YOUR TURN — A chemist has impure benzaldehyde contaminated with non-carbonyl impurities. Outline the purification. (Answer: shake with saturated sodium bisulphite solution; benzaldehyde's crystalline bisulphite compound precipitates while impurities stay dissolved; filter and wash the crystals; decompose them with dilute acid or sodium carbonate solution to release pure benzaldehyde. Benzaldehyde is unhindered enough for HSO3- to add — benzophenone would refuse this treatment.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Bisulphite captures, cyanide extends, Grignard builds, alcohol masks: four nucleophiles, four jobs, one mechanism.

Nitrogen Nucleophiles — Oximes, Hydrazones and the 2,4-DNP Test
• DEFINITION — Ammonia and its derivatives, H2N-Z, add to carbonyls and then eliminate water — an addition-elimination (condensation) — giving products with a C=N double bond. The family: hydroxylamine (Z = OH) gives OXIMES; hydrazine (Z = NH2) gives HYDRAZONES; phenylhydrazine (Z = NH-C6H5) gives PHENYLHYDRAZONES; semicarbazide (Z = NH-CO-NH2) gives SEMICARBAZONES; and 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine gives 2,4-DINITROPHENYLHYDRAZONES. Each crystalline derivative has a sharp melting point, which is how a liquid carbonyl compound gets identified: make the derivative, read the melting point.
• The 2,4-DNP test — the carbonyl detector. Brady's reagent (2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine in acidic alcohol) gives an immediate yellow, orange or red crystalline precipitate with ANY aldehyde or ketone. It answers one question only: is a C=O of an aldehyde or ketone present? It cannot tell aldehyde from ketone — both precipitate — and alcohols, acids and esters stay silent.
• WHY the medium must be weakly acidic — the pH knife-edge. A little acid protonates the carbonyl oxygen and speeds the addition (good). Too much acid protonates the H2N-Z reagent itself into H3N+-Z, which has no lone pair and cannot attack (fatal). Too little acid and the dehydration step crawls. Maximum rate lives in the mildly acidic middle — a favourite "explain the optimum pH" answer.
• Ammonia itself behaves specially with formaldehyde: instead of a simple adduct, excess ammonia converts formaldehyde into hexamethylenetetramine, (CH2)6N4, a cage compound known as urotropine — used as a urinary antiseptic and in fuel tablets. Ordinary aldehydes with ammonia give simple aldehyde-ammonia addition products that trimerise; ketones with ammonia give complex condensation products not worth chasing.
• EXAMPLE — The identification assembly line, exactly as practical exams run it: unknown liquid plus 2,4-DNP gives an orange precipitate (carbonyl confirmed); the derivative melts sharply at the value tabulated for propanone's derivative; identity established without a single spectrum.
• WORKED PATTERN — Naming a derivative product in one breath: keep the carbonyl compound's name, add the reagent's product word. Propanone plus hydroxylamine gives propanone oxime. Ethanal plus semicarbazide gives ethanal semicarbazone. Benzaldehyde plus phenylhydrazine gives benzaldehyde phenylhydrazone. The carbonyl oxygen leaves as water, the =N-Z group takes its place.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The 2,4-DNP trap is overselling it: a positive DNP test does NOT prove an aldehyde. Ketones precipitate identically. To split aldehyde from ketone you must walk one section further, to Tollens or Fehling. Examiners pair these two tests precisely to catch students who stop one step early.
• YOUR TURN — An unknown gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNP but no silver mirror with Tollens reagent. What is it, and what single extra fact would pin it down? (Answer: it is a ketone — carbonyl present (DNP positive) but not an aldehyde (Tollens negative). The melting point of its 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone, matched against tables, identifies which ketone.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Nitrogen derivatives turn invisible functional groups into weighable, melt-able crystals — and 2,4-DNP is the gatekeeper test of this chapter: everything carbonyl passes it, nothing else does.

Alpha-Hydrogen Chemistry — The Aldol Condensation
• DEFINITION — The alpha-hydrogen is a hydrogen on the carbon directly beside the carbonyl. It is unusually acidic for a C-H (pKa around 19-20 against about 50 for an ordinary alkane) because removing it leaves an ENOLATE anion whose negative charge is resonance-shared between the alpha carbon and the carbonyl oxygen. Enolates are carbon nucleophiles — and that is a superpower: they attack other carbonyls and build carbon-carbon bonds.
• The aldol reaction: an aldehyde or ketone WITH at least one alpha-hydrogen, stirred with DILUTE sodium hydroxide (or Ba(OH)2), dimerises. Two molecules of ethanal give 3-hydroxybutanal — an ALDOL, one molecule carrying both an aldehyde and an alcohol group. The mechanism in steps: Step 1, hydroxide removes an alpha-H, forming the enolate. Step 2, the enolate carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule — nucleophilic addition, exactly the mechanism of the earlier section. Step 3, the alkoxide takes a proton from water, giving the aldol and returning the hydroxide catalyst.
• Dehydration completes the classic picture: warm the aldol and it loses water — OH from the beta carbon, H from the alpha carbon — to give an alpha,beta-unsaturated carbonyl. Ethanal's aldol becomes but-2-enal (crotonaldehyde). The new C=C sits conjugated with C=O, which is why dehydration is so willing: conjugation is the reward.
• Figure 3 draws this chapter's great fork: an aldehyde meeting base is sorted by a single question — does it carry an alpha-hydrogen? Left branch, yes: dilute base, enolate, aldol. Right branch, no: concentrated base forces the Cannizzaro reaction of the next section instead.
• Acetone does it too: two molecules give 4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one (diacetone alcohol), which dehydrates to 4-methylpent-3-en-2-one (mesityl oxide). Ketone aldols are slower and less complete than aldehyde aldols (ketones are poorer electrophiles), but the pattern is identical.
• Crossed aldol — two different carbonyls, both with alpha-hydrogens, give FOUR products (two self, two crossed) and a separation headache. The reaction becomes useful when one partner has NO alpha-hydrogen: it cannot form an enolate, so it can only play electrophile. Benzaldehyde plus ethanal with dilute NaOH gives, after dehydration, cinnamaldehyde (3-phenylprop-2-enal) — the cinnamon smell, made by design rather than luck.
• Intramolecular aldol in one line: a dicarbonyl compound whose enolate can reach its own second carbonyl (heptane-2,6-dione is the textbook case) cyclises to a five- or six-membered enone — the ring-building version of the same trick.
• WORKED PATTERN — Predicting any aldol product mechanically: take molecule A, mark its alpha carbon; take molecule B, mark its carbonyl carbon; join those two carbons with a single bond; B's oxygen becomes OH. That is the aldol. If heat is mentioned, delete that OH and an alpha-H from A and draw the double bond between alpha and beta carbons. Two marks, one join, optional deletion — practice it until it is reflex.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Benzaldehyde and formaldehyde CANNOT self-condense — no alpha-hydrogen, no enolate, no aldol. In a crossed aldol they appear only on the electrophile side. Any option showing benzaldehyde dimerising with dilute NaOH is wrong; with CONCENTRATED alkali it does something else entirely (next section), and the concentration printed in the question is the tell.
• EXAMPLE — Propanal with dilute NaOH, then heat. Enolate forms on C2 of one molecule and attacks the carbonyl of another: 3-hydroxy-2-methylpentanal. Dehydration removes the OH and the alpha-H on the carbon bearing the methyl branch, giving 2-methylpent-2-enal — the branched skeleton is the fingerprint of a crossed-looking product that was actually a self-aldol.
• YOUR TURN — Name the dehydrated aldol product of butan-2-one reacting with itself, in words. (Answer: the enolate forms preferentially at the methyl group (C1); joining C1 of one molecule to the carbonyl carbon of another and dehydrating gives 3,4-dimethylhex-3-en-2-one — but for exam purposes the accepted description is the alpha,beta-unsaturated ketone from self-condensation; the essential marks are the enolate carbon, the join, and the conjugated C=C beside C=O.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Aldol is the carbonyl group attacking itself: base mints an enolate from the alpha carbon, the enolate spends it on another carbonyl, and heat cashes the product out as a conjugated enal or enone. It is the chapter's one true C-C bond construction — treat it accordingly.

▸ No Alpha-Hydrogen — The Cannizzaro Reaction
• DEFINITION — The Cannizzaro reaction: an aldehyde with NO alpha-hydrogen, treated with CONCENTRATED potassium or sodium hydroxide, undergoes self oxidation-reduction (disproportionation). Of every two molecules, one is reduced to the primary alcohol and the other is oxidised to the carboxylic acid salt. No outside oxidant or reductant is involved — the aldehyde cannibalises itself.
• The cast is short and must be recognised instantly: formaldehyde (HCHO) gives methanol plus potassium formate; benzaldehyde gives benzyl alcohol plus potassium benzoate; 2,2-dimethylpropanal, (CH3)3C-CHO, also qualifies — its alpha carbon is a quaternary carbon carrying no hydrogen at all, the "hidden" member of the family that examiners use to test whether you actually checked for alpha-hydrogens.
• Mechanism in words: hydroxide adds to the carbonyl of one molecule, giving a tetrahedral adduct; that adduct transfers a HYDRIDE ion (H-) directly to the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule. The donor, having lost hydride, becomes the carboxylic acid (isolated as its salt in the alkali); the acceptor, having gained hydride, becomes the alkoxide and then the alcohol. Hydride transfer is the slow step — the proof is elegant: run the reaction in heavy water and the alcohol product contains no carbon-bound deuterium, because its new hydrogen arrived from the other aldehyde, not from the solvent.
• Stoichiometry trap-avoidance: the products come in a one-to-one pair from TWO molecules of aldehyde. Starting with 2 mol of benzaldehyde you harvest 1 mol of benzyl alcohol and 1 mol of potassium benzoate — never 2 mol of each.
• Crossed Cannizzaro — mix formaldehyde with any other no-alpha-H aldehyde and the division of labour is fixed: FORMALDEHYDE is always the one oxidised (to formate), the partner is reduced to its alcohol. Benzaldehyde plus formaldehyde plus concentrated KOH gives benzyl alcohol plus potassium formate. Formaldehyde's carbonyl is the most reactive and the best hydride donor after hydroxide adds, so it always plays the donor.
• The fork, stated as a rule you can apply half-asleep: aldehyde plus alkali — first ask "alpha-hydrogen?" If YES, aldol chemistry (dilute base in the textbook recipes). If NO, Cannizzaro (concentrated base). The two reactions share a reagent shelf and differ in everything else; the alpha-hydrogen question is the entire decision.
✔ KEY RULE — Cannizzaro needs three conditions simultaneously: an ALDEHYDE (ketones do not undergo it), NO alpha-hydrogen, and CONCENTRATED alkali. Remove any one and the reaction is off the table.
• EXAMPLE — The ambush pair, placed side by side exactly as exams place them: (a) ethanal with dilute NaOH gives the aldol, 3-hydroxybutanal. (b) ethanal with concentrated KOH still gives aldol-type chemistry, NOT Cannizzaro, because ethanal HAS alpha-hydrogens — concentration cannot rescue a substrate that fails the alpha test. The substrate decides first; concentration only confirms.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The most common Cannizzaro error is offering it to acetaldehyde or propanal (both have alpha-hydrogens and aldol instead). The second most common is forgetting that crossed Cannizzaro oxidises the formaldehyde partner whichever aldehyde it is paired with — students regularly oxidise the wrong molecule.
• YOUR TURN — Which of these undergo Cannizzaro with concentrated KOH: propanal, formaldehyde, 2-methylpropanal, benzaldehyde, trimethylacetaldehyde ((CH3)3C-CHO)? (Answer: formaldehyde, benzaldehyde and trimethylacetaldehyde — three. Propanal and 2-methylpropanal each carry at least one alpha-hydrogen; note 2-methylpropanal has exactly ONE alpha-H, which is enough to disqualify it — the test is any alpha-H at all, not the count.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Cannizzaro is disproportionation with a dress code: no alpha-hydrogen, concentrated alkali, aldehydes only — and formaldehyde, whenever present, is always the molecule that gets oxidised.

Oxidation — Tollens, Fehling and the Distinguishing Tests
• DEFINITION — Aldehydes are strong enough reducing agents to be oxidised by very mild oxidants that ketones completely ignore. That gap is the basis of the classical distinguishing tests: Tollens reagent (silver mirror), Fehling and Benedict solutions (red precipitate), and Schiff reagent (colour restored). Ketones resist all mild oxidants and cleave only under violent conditions.
• TOLLENS test — freshly prepared ammoniacal silver nitrate (the silver-ammonia complex, [Ag(NH3)2]+ OH-), warmed gently with the sample. An aldehyde reduces Ag+ to metallic silver, which plates the glass as a bright SILVER MIRROR: R-CHO becomes R-COOH while Ag+ becomes Ag. Ketones give no mirror. This is the universal aldehyde-versus-ketone test.
• Tollens nuances, each one a past trap: ALPHA-HYDROXY KETONES reduce Tollens reagent — fructose, a ketose sugar, gives a positive test because in the alkaline medium it rearranges to an aldehyde form. FORMIC ACID also gives a silver mirror, because its structure H-C(=O)-OH carries an aldehyde-like hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon. And terminal alkynes give a WHITE precipitate (silver acetylide) with the same reagent — a precipitate, not a mirror; do not let the shared reagent blur two different observations.
• FEHLING test — mix Fehling A (aqueous CuSO4) with Fehling B (alkaline sodium potassium tartrate, Rochelle salt) to get a deep blue Cu(II) complex; heat it with an ALIPHATIC aldehyde and a brick-RED precipitate of Cu2O falls as the aldehyde oxidises to its acid. Acetaldehyde and formaldehyde pass beautifully.
• THE Fehling trap, in the largest letters this chapter owns: BENZALDEHYDE DOES NOT REDUCE FEHLING'S SOLUTION. Aromatic aldehydes reduce Tollens (the stronger of the two mild oxidants in practice) but fail Fehling and Benedict. "Aldehydes reduce Fehling" is true only with the silent word ALIPHATIC in front of it — Figure 2 builds its matrix around exactly this cell, and the exam builds its questions around it too.
• BENEDICT solution (CuSO4 with sodium citrate and sodium carbonate) reads the same verdict as Fehling: aliphatic aldehydes yes, aromatic aldehydes and ketones no. Its famous job is testing urine for glucose — glucose is an aliphatic aldehyde sugar, so it reduces Benedict's to the red Cu2O.
• SCHIFF test — Schiff reagent is the magenta dye rosaniline bleached colourless by sulphur dioxide. Aldehydes restore the pink-violet colour promptly; ketones restore it extremely slowly or not at all within test time. A second aldehyde-versus-ketone vote, useful when Tollens glassware is not at hand.
• Strong oxidation, for completeness: hot KMnO4, concentrated HNO3 or acidified K2Cr2O7 take aldehydes to carboxylic acids trivially, and attack ketones only under forcing conditions, breaking a C-C bond on either side of the carbonyl to give a mixture of smaller acids. (Popoff's rule, one line for the pattern-hungry: in an unsymmetrical ketone the carbonyl tends to stay with the smaller alkyl group.) Ketones' resistance is the deep reason Tollens and Fehling can exist as tests at all.
• Figure 2 lays the four bench tests against four bench compounds — acetaldehyde, benzaldehyde, acetone, ethanol — and marks the one cell everyone gets wrong. Read it as a decision grid: 2,4-DNP finds the carbonyls, Tollens splits aldehyde from ketone, Fehling splits aliphatic from aromatic aldehyde, iodoform (next section) finds the CH3-CO fingerprint.
• WORKED PATTERN — Identifying an unknown from test results, the standard drill. Ask in order: DNP precipitate? (carbonyl yes/no). Tollens mirror? (aldehyde yes, ketone no). Fehling red precipitate? (aliphatic aldehyde yes; aromatic aldehyde no). Three questions place any simple carbonyl compound in its exact box.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Read Tollens results with the exceptions loaded: fructose (an alpha-hydroxy ketone) and formic acid both fake being aldehydes. And never report benzaldehyde as Fehling-positive — it is the single most planted false statement in this chapter's true/false fields.
• YOUR TURN — A compound gives an orange 2,4-DNP precipitate, a silver mirror with Tollens, but no reaction with Fehling solution, even on heating. Name its class and a likely identity. (Answer: an aromatic aldehyde — benzaldehyde is the model. DNP proves carbonyl, Tollens proves aldehyde, Fehling silence proves aromatic rather than aliphatic.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — The test ladder by strength: 2,4-DNP detects carbonyls, Tollens detects aldehydes (with fructose and formic acid as registered impostors), Fehling detects ALIPHATIC aldehydes only — benzaldehyde is the exception that the whole ladder leans on.

The Haloform Reaction — Iodoform as Test and as Cleavage
• DEFINITION — The haloform reaction: a compound containing the CH3-CO- group (a methyl ketone, or ethanal), or the CH3-CH(OH)- group that is oxidised into it on the spot, is warmed with iodine and sodium hydroxide (equivalently sodium hypoiodite, NaOI) and gives a YELLOW precipitate of iodoform, CHI3 (melting point 392 K), while the rest of the molecule leaves as a carboxylate with ONE carbon fewer than the starting carbonyl compound.
• Mechanism in brief: hydroxide and iodine progressively replace all three methyl hydrogens with iodine (each installed iodine makes the remaining hydrogens still more acidic, so iodination runs to completion: CH3-CO- becomes CI3-CO-). Hydroxide then attacks the carbonyl carbon, the tetrahedral intermediate collapses, and the CI3- group departs — a rare carbon leaving group, stabilised by three iodines — and collects a proton to become CHI3 while the acyl fragment becomes the carboxylate.
• THE POSITIVE LIST, which must be automatic: ethanal (the ONLY aldehyde that qualifies — it alone carries CH3 next to its carbonyl), all methyl ketones (propanone, butan-2-one, pentan-2-one, and acetophenone — the ring does not matter, the CH3-CO does), and the alcohols ethanol and secondary alcohols of the form CH3-CH(OH)-R (propan-2-ol, butan-2-ol), because hypoiodite oxidises them to the methyl carbonyl first.
• THE NEGATIVE LIST, equally automatic: methanol (no second carbon at all), propan-1-ol (oxidises to propanal — no CH3-CO unit), benzaldehyde, benzophenone, and pentan-3-one (CH3CH2-CO-CH2CH3 — its carbonyl is flanked by ethyls, no methyl group attached to C=O). Pentan-3-one versus pentan-2-one is the standard paired distractor: same formula, opposite verdicts.
• Dual identity of the reaction: as a TEST it fingerprints the CH3-CO / CH3-CH(OH) unit with an unmistakable yellow solid; as a REACTION it is an oxidative cleavage — R-CO-CH3 becomes R-COOH with one carbon surrendered to the haloform. Acetophenone plus I2/NaOH, then acidification, is a genuine preparation of benzoic acid. With Cl2/NaOH the same chemistry produces chloroform (colourless), and with Br2, bromoform.
• WORKED PATTERN — The three-second scan for any haloform question: look at the carbonyl carbon (or the carbinol carbon) and ask "is a CH3 group directly attached here?" If yes — directly as CH3-C=O or one oxidation away as CH3-CH-OH — the answer is positive, yellow precipitate. Anything else is negative, however ketone-like the molecule looks.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The slogan "iodoform tests for ketones" fails in both directions: pentan-3-one is a ketone and fails, while ethanol is not even a carbonyl compound and passes. The test reads a structural unit, CH3-CO or its alcohol precursor — never a functional-group label.
• EXAMPLE — Two C5 ketones part ways: pentan-2-one warmed with I2/NaOH clouds yellow with iodoform and leaves butanoate in solution; pentan-3-one under identical conditions stays clear. Isomer questions ("same formula, different test") are built from exactly this contrast.
• YOUR TURN — Which of these give a yellow precipitate with I2/NaOH: (a) CH3-CH2-CHO, (b) CH3-CO-C6H5, (c) CH3-CH(OH)-CH3, (d) C6H5-CHO? (Answer: (b) and (c) only. Propanal's methyl is one carbon too far from the carbonyl — it is an ethyl aldehyde, not a CH3-CO; benzaldehyde has no methyl ketone unit at all.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Iodoform is a methyl-group detector wearing a test's clothing: CH3 attached to C=O, or to CH-OH, turns yellow; everything else in the chapter is silence.

Reduction — Hydrides, Clemmensen and Wolff-Kishner
• DEFINITION — Carbonyl reduction has two depths. Shallow reduction stops at the alcohol (C=O becomes CH-OH). Deep reduction strips the oxygen entirely (C=O becomes CH2, an alkane-level carbon). Every reagent in this section is filed by which depth it delivers — confusing the depths is the standard error.
• Shallow reagents: catalytic hydrogenation (H2 over Ni, Pt or Pd) takes aldehydes to primary alcohols and ketones to secondary alcohols, though it will also reduce C=C bonds in the same molecule. Sodium borohydride, NaBH4, is the gentle specialist: it reduces ALDEHYDES AND KETONES ONLY, leaving carboxylic acids, esters and isolated C=C bonds untouched — the reagent of choice when the molecule carries other reducible-looking groups. Lithium aluminium hydride, LiAlH4, is the bruiser: it reduces aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids AND esters all the way to alcohols, and must be used in dry ether because it reacts violently with water.
• CLEMMENSEN reduction (deep, acidic): the carbonyl compound is refluxed with amalgamated zinc, Zn-Hg, and concentrated HCl; C=O becomes CH2. Acetophenone becomes ethylbenzene. Choose it when the rest of the molecule tolerates strong hot acid — its classic partnership is Friedel-Crafts acylation, where acylate-then-Clemmensen builds straight-chain alkylbenzenes without rearrangement.
• WOLFF-KISHNER reduction (deep, basic): the carbonyl is first converted to its hydrazone with hydrazine, then heated with strong KOH in high-boiling ethylene glycol (about 453 K); C=O becomes CH2 and nitrogen gas bubbles off, driving the reaction. Choose it when the molecule is ACID-SENSITIVE but base-tolerant — the mirror image of Clemmensen.
• The choice matrix, worth memorising as a sentence: acid-stable substrate, Clemmensen; acid-sensitive substrate, Wolff-Kishner; both reduce C=O all the way to CH2 and both leave most other groups (alkyl, aryl, halide on a ring) standing.
• EXAMPLE — The acetal problem, a conversion-exam staple: a molecule carrying both a ketone and an acid-labile acetal protecting group must lose its ketone oxygen. Clemmensen's concentrated HCl would hydrolyse the acetal while reducing; Wolff-Kishner's hot alkali reduces the C=O to CH2 and leaves the acetal untouched, because acetals are base-stable. Reagent choice is substrate reading, not preference.
• WORKED PATTERN — Answering "reduce this carbonyl": ask two questions. How deep? (Alcohol: hydride or catalytic. Methylene: Clemmensen or Wolff-Kishner.) What else is in the molecule? (Acid/ester present and must survive: NaBH4. Acid present and must also become an alcohol: LiAlH4. Strong-acid-sensitive group present: avoid Clemmensen.) Two questions, reagent chosen.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The depth trap in its two costumes: Clemmensen and Wolff-Kishner do NOT stop at the alcohol (students sketch R-CH2OH for both), and NaBH4 does NOT reduce carboxylic acids (students offer it for CH3COOH and get no reaction). Also note LiAlH4 takes acids to PRIMARY ALCOHOLS, never to aldehydes — there is no hydride reagent in this syllabus that parks an acid at the aldehyde; that job belongs to Rosenmund on the acid chloride.
• YOUR TURN — Select reagents: (a) butan-2-one to butane; (b) butan-2-one to butan-2-ol; (c) ethanoic acid to ethanol. (Answer: (a) Clemmensen (Zn-Hg, conc HCl) or Wolff-Kishner (NH2NH2, then KOH, heat) — deep reduction. (b) NaBH4, or H2/Ni — shallow. (c) LiAlH4 in dry ether, then aqueous work-up — only the strong hydride touches a carboxylic acid.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Depth first, compatibility second: hydrides and hydrogen stop at alcohols, the zinc and hydrazine routes burn the oxygen out entirely, and Clemmensen versus Wolff-Kishner is nothing but the question "will this molecule survive hot acid?"

Carboxylic Acids — Structure and Preparation
• DEFINITION — A carboxylic acid carries the -COOH group: a carbonyl carbon bonded to a hydroxyl, sp2 and planar, with the acidic proton on the oxygen. In the vapour phase and in nonpolar solvents, acids pair up into DIMERS held by two hydrogen bonds between the two -COOH groups — a detail with loud consequences: apparent molar masses in benzene come out doubled (the colligative-property chapters cash this in as an association factor), and boiling points soar above even alcohols of similar mass. Ethanoic acid boils at 391 K; propan-1-ol, same molar mass territory, at 370 K.
• Solubility pattern: the first four aliphatic acids mix with water in all proportions (the -COOH group hydrogen-bonds eagerly), then solubility falls steeply with chain length as the hydrocarbon tail wins. Benzoic acid is the classic demonstration: almost insoluble in cold water, comfortably soluble in hot — a recrystallisation waiting to be described.
• Preparation 1, full oxidation: primary alcohols and aldehydes with strong oxidants (acidified K2Cr2O7, KMnO4, HNO3) run to the acid — the ladder climbed to the top, no brakes. Ethanol gives ethanoic acid; this is also how wine sours.
• Preparation 2, oxidation of alkylbenzenes: hot alkaline KMnO4 followed by acidification burns ANY alkyl side chain that carries at least one benzylic hydrogen all the way down to -COOH. Ethylbenzene and propylbenzene both give benzoic acid — chain length is irrelevant, the ring keeps only the first carbon as its carboxyl carbon.
• Preparation 3, nitrile hydrolysis: a nitrile boiled with aqueous acid or alkali hydrolyses through the amide to the carboxylic acid: R-CN -> R-CONH2 -> R-COOH. Since the nitrile came from R-X plus KCN, this route lengthens the chain by one carbon — bromoethane becomes propanoic acid.
• Preparation 4, Grignard carboxylation: bubble CO2 (or pour onto crushed dry ice) a Grignard reagent and the R group bonds to the CO2 carbon; acidic work-up gives R-COOH, again one carbon longer than the halide you started from. CH3-MgBr becomes ethanoic acid.
• Preparation 5, trihalide hydrolysis: chloroform heated with concentrated KOH hydrolyses to formic acid (isolated as potassium formate) — three chlorines on one carbon are a -COOH group in disguise; benzotrichloride, C6H5-CCl3, hydrolyses with water to benzoic acid by the same logic.
• Preparation 6, derivative hydrolysis in one line: acyl chlorides and anhydrides react with water instantly to the acid, and esters hydrolyse on boiling with acid or alkali — the reverse commute of the derivative-forming reactions in two sections' time.
• WORKED PATTERN — Carbon accounting for acid syntheses: oxidation routes keep the carbon count of the alcohol/aldehyde (or slash the side chain to the ring carbon); nitrile hydrolysis and Grignard-plus-CO2 each ADD exactly one carbon (the nitrile or CO2 carbon). Asked to grow an acid by one carbon, reach for KCN or CO2; asked to keep the count, oxidise.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The tert-butylbenzene ambush: it has NO benzylic hydrogen, so hot KMnO4 leaves it essentially untouched — "all alkylbenzenes give benzoic acid" fails precisely where the benzylic position is fully substituted. Check for the benzylic H before predicting side-chain oxidation.
• EXAMPLE — Three roads to benzoic acid, a model short answer: oxidise toluene or any benzylic-H alkylbenzene with hot alkaline KMnO4; hydrolyse benzonitrile with boiling acid; or bromobenzene to its Grignard reagent, then CO2, then acid work-up. Three mechanisms, one product — synthesis questions reward exactly this kind of route fluency.
• YOUR TURN — Starting from ethanol, reach propanoic acid. (Answer: convert ethanol to bromoethane (HBr or PBr3), treat with KCN to give propanenitrile, then hydrolyse with boiling aqueous acid — propanoic acid, one carbon richer than the bromide you made.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Carboxylic acids sit at the top of the oxidation ladder, so every road either climbs (oxidation), extends (nitrile or CO2 add the carboxyl carbon), or unmasks (hydrolysis of trihalides and derivatives).

Acid Strength — The pKa Ladder
• DEFINITION — Acid strength is measured by Ka, usually quoted as pKa = -log Ka: the LOWER the pKa, the STRONGER the acid. A carboxylic acid ionises as R-COOH + H2O ⇌ R-COO- + H3O+, and everything about its strength is decided by one question — how stable is the carboxylate anion it leaves behind?
• WHY carboxylate is special: in the anion, the negative charge is shared EQUALLY over two oxygen atoms — both C-O bonds in a carboxylate ion are the same length, the resonance is perfectly symmetrical, and charge never has to sit on carbon. Compare the phenoxide ion, whose charge delocalises into the ring and must park on carbon atoms in most resonance forms (charge on carbon, less electronegative, costs energy), and the alkoxide ion, which has no delocalisation at all. The league table follows: acetic acid pKa 4.76, phenol about 10, ethanol about 16. Carboxylic acids beat phenols by roughly a million-fold in Ka, and phenols beat alcohols by as much again.
• Substituent logic, the only rule you need: a group that WITHDRAWS electron density disperses the anion's charge further, stabilises the anion, and makes the acid STRONGER. A group that DONATES electron density concentrates the charge, destabilises the anion, and makes the acid WEAKER. Every ranking question is this rule applied with a ruler for distance.
• Figure 4 draws the ladder. Follow the chlorine staircase: acetic acid 4.76; chloroacetic acid 2.87 (one Cl, -I pull); dichloroacetic acid 1.29 (two); trichloroacetic acid 0.65 (three — nearly a strong acid). Each chlorine buys roughly one and a half to two pKa units of strength. Fluorine pulls harder than chlorine: fluoroacetic acid stands at 2.59 and trifluoroacetic acid at 0.23. Benzoic acid (4.20) sits a step above acetic, and phenol (about 10) dangles far below as the cross-reference that shows what a real carboxylate is worth.
• Distance decay, the refinement that separates ranks: the -I effect fades with distance, so alpha-chlorobutanoic acid (pKa about 2.9) is far stronger than 3-chlorobutanoic (about 4.1), which beats 4-chlorobutanoic (about 4.5), which still edges the unsubstituted acid. Same substituent, four addresses, four strengths — position is part of the answer.
• The formic acid point: formic acid (pKa 3.75) is stronger than acetic acid (4.76), because acetic acid's methyl group donates (+I) into the carboxylate and destabilises it, while formic acid's hydrogen does not. Extend the chain and propanoic acid (4.87) sinks a little lower still. Alkyl groups are donors: more donation, weaker acid.
• The benzoic acid family: benzoic acid (4.20) beats acetic acid because the sp2 ring carbon attached to -COOH is more electron-withdrawing than a methyl group. Substituents then play by the rule: p-nitrobenzoic acid is stronger (3.41, withdrawer), p-methylbenzoic weaker (4.37, donor), p-hydroxybenzoic weaker still (4.58, the OH donates by resonance). And the ORTHO EFFECT overrides everything: an ortho substituent of almost any kind makes benzoic acid stronger — o-methylbenzoic acid (3.91) beats benzoic acid despite methyl being a donor, because the bulky ortho group twists the -COOH out of the ring's plane and switches off the ring's resonance donation into it; salicylic acid (o-hydroxy, 2.97) adds an intramolecular hydrogen bond that cradles its anion.
• WORKED PATTERN — Ranking any set of acids, run the checklist aloud: (1) count electron-withdrawing groups and note how many atoms away they sit — more and closer wins; (2) check for donors, especially resonance donors para on a benzoic acid — they weaken; (3) if a benzoic acid carries ANY ortho substituent, promote it (ortho effect); (4) translate through anion stability, never through "how acidic the proton feels". Four checks and the ladder builds itself.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The pKa inversion is the cheapest trap in physical-organic questions: lower pKa means STRONGER acid, and under time pressure half the hall reads it backwards. Anchor it once: trichloroacetic acid, pKa 0.65, is the bully of the ladder; phenol, pKa 10, is the weakling. Big number, feeble acid.
• YOUR TURN — Arrange in decreasing acid strength: propanoic acid, acetic acid, fluoroacetic acid, chloroacetic acid. (Answer: fluoroacetic (2.59) > chloroacetic (2.87) > acetic (4.76) > propanoic (4.87). Fluorine out-pulls chlorine; both halo acids crush the plain ones; propanoic trails acetic because its larger alkyl group donates slightly more.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Acid strength is anion stability wearing a number: spread the negative charge (two oxygens, plus withdrawers close by) and the acid strengthens; feed the charge (donors, distance) and it weakens. The whole ladder — chlorines, positions, ortho twists — is that one sentence.

Reactions of Carboxylic Acids — Salts, Esters and Acid Derivatives
• DEFINITION — Carboxylic acids react in three theatres: as ACIDS (the -OH proton is traded away), at the CARBONYL (the -OH group itself is replaced, giving the acid-derivative family), and at the ALPHA carbon and ring (next section). Keep the theatres separate and the reaction list organises itself.
• Theatre one, acid behaviour: with reactive metals (Na, Zn, Mg) acids give the salt plus hydrogen gas, briskly enough to observe but more sedately than mineral acids. With alkalis they form salts and water. With carbonates and, decisively, with sodium bicarbonate they give the salt, water, and CO2 — visible as brisk effervescence that turns limewater milky.
• The NaHCO3 test deserves its own spotlight: it distinguishes carboxylic acids from PHENOLS. Phenol is acidic enough to react with NaOH and with sodium metal, but it is WEAKER than carbonic acid, so it cannot decompose bicarbonate — no effervescence. The acid ladder you built in the previous section is working as a laboratory tool here: bicarbonate reacts only with acids stronger than carbonic acid (pKa of H2CO3 about 6.4), and phenol at pKa 10 fails the audition. (One whisper-level exception for the truly curious: picric acid, a phenol loaded with three nitro groups, is strong enough to effervesce — it is the exception that proves the ladder logic.)
• Theatre two, esterification (Fischer): a carboxylic acid warmed with an alcohol and concentrated H2SO4 (or dry HCl gas) reaches an equilibrium with the ESTER plus water: CH3-COOH + C2H5-OH ⇌ CH3-COO-C2H5 + H2O. Drive it by removing water or using excess alcohol. The mechanism's one examinable fact, proved by oxygen-18 labelling: the acid contributes the OH and the alcohol contributes the H of the water formed — labelled alcohol oxygen turns up inside the ester, not in the water.
• Theatre two continued, the derivative family: SOCl2 (cleanest — the by-products SO2 and HCl are gases that leave) or PCl5 converts the acid to its ACYL CHLORIDE. Heat two molecules of acid with P2O5 and they shed water to the ANHYDRIDE. Pass ammonia and the ammonium salt, on strong heating, dehydrates to the AMIDE. These are all the same move — replace the -OH of COOH — and the products are the reactive currency spent elsewhere in this chapter (remember: acyl chloride is the Rosenmund substrate and the Friedel-Crafts partner).
• Reduction of acids, closing the loop with the reduction section: LiAlH4 in dry ether (or diborane, B2H6, which prefers COOH even over most other groups) reduces a carboxylic acid to the PRIMARY ALCOHOL — CH3-COOH becomes CH3-CH2-OH. NaBH4 leaves acids alone. The oxidation ladder therefore has exactly one hydride elevator down from the top floor, and it is the strong one.
• WORKED PATTERN — The ethanoic acid five-reagent drill, a complete short question in itself: Na metal gives sodium ethanoate plus H2; NaHCO3 gives sodium ethanoate plus CO2 effervescence; ethanol with conc H2SO4 gives ethyl ethanoate (fruity ester); PCl5 gives ethanoyl chloride; LiAlH4 gives ethanol. Five reagents, five theatres, no overlaps — practise until the answers arrive before the question finishes.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Both phenol and carboxylic acids turn blue litmus red and react with NaOH, so options claiming those tests distinguish them are empty. The discriminators are bicarbonate effervescence (acid yes, phenol no) and neutral FeCl3 (phenol gives a violet colour, simple acids do not).
• YOUR TURN — A liquid fizzes with NaHCO3 solution and, warmed with ethanol and a drop of concentrated H2SO4, develops a fruity smell. Identify its functional group and name the smell-producing product class. (Answer: a carboxylic acid — the fizz is CO2 from bicarbonate decomposition, and the fruity product is an ester from Fischer esterification. Phenol is excluded by the first test alone.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — An acid's proton is its handshake (salts, effervescence), its hydroxyl is its currency (chlorides, anhydrides, amides, esters), and LiAlH4 is its elevator back down to alcohol — three theatres, no confusion.

▸ Decarboxylation, HVZ and the Hunsdiecker Reaction
• DEFINITION — Three named reactions finish the acid story: decarboxylation deletes the -COOH group as CO2, the Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky (HVZ) reaction brominates the alpha carbon, and the Hunsdiecker reaction swaps -COOAg for a bromine, deleting one carbon in the process. All three are chain-editing tools.
• Decarboxylation proper: heat the sodium salt of the acid with soda lime (NaOH mixed with CaO) and the carboxyl group leaves as Na2CO3 while the R group picks up a hydrogen: R-COO-Na + NaOH -> R-H + Na2CO3. Sodium ethanoate gives methane — the product always has ONE CARBON FEWER than the acid. The CaO keeps the NaOH dry and free-flowing; the chemistry is the hot alkali's.
• Two gentler decarboxylations worth their lines: beta-keto acids lose CO2 on mere warming (acetoacetic acid gives propanone plus CO2 through a snug cyclic transition state — no soda lime needed), and Kolbe electrolysis of a carboxylate solution discharges two carboxylates at the anode to give the COUPLED alkane R-R plus 2 CO2 (from the hydrocarbons chapter — decarboxylation with a partner instead of a hydrogen).
• HVZ reaction: a carboxylic acid carrying at least one ALPHA-HYDROGEN is treated with Br2 (or Cl2) in the presence of a trace of red phosphorus, and the alpha-hydrogen is replaced by halogen: CH3-CH2-COOH -> CH3-CHBr-COOH (2-bromopropanoic acid). The phosphorus first converts a little acid into its acyl bromide, which enolises far more readily than the acid itself; bromine strikes the enol, and exchange with more acid delivers the alpha-bromo acid while regenerating the acyl bromide — a catalytic loop worth narrating if asked "why red P".
• HVZ scope is the examinable edge: formic acid has no alpha carbon at all, and benzoic acid's -COOH is bolted straight onto the ring, so NEITHER undergoes HVZ. Alpha-bromo acids are gateways — treat one with concentrated ammonia and you are on the road to alpha-amino acids.
• Hunsdiecker reaction: the dry SILVER salt of the acid is refluxed with Br2 in CCl4: R-COOAg + Br2 -> R-Br + CO2 + AgBr. The product is an alkyl bromide with one carbon fewer than the acid — silver propanoate gives bromoethane. It is a radical chain reaction, and its job description is precise: descend a series by exactly one carbon and arrive holding a bromide, ready for Grignard or Wurtz chemistry.
• WORKED PATTERN — The "lose one carbon" toolbox, four tools, four destinations: soda lime gives the ALKANE R-H; Kolbe gives the COUPLED alkane R-R; Hunsdiecker gives the BROMIDE R-Br; haloform on a methyl ketone gives the ACID with one carbon fewer. Exam conversion questions are answered by matching the destination functional group to the tool.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Two scope traps guard this section: HVZ on benzoic or formic acid (no alpha-H, no reaction — the options love offering "ring bromination" as a decoy), and Hunsdiecker attempted on the sodium salt or in water (it demands the DRY SILVER salt in CCl4; the sodium salt route is Kolbe's electrolysis, a different reaction).
• EXAMPLE — Descend from propanoic acid two ways: soda lime on sodium propanoate gives ethane (hydrocarbon destination); Hunsdiecker on silver propanoate gives bromoethane (halide destination). Same one-carbon descent, different arrivals — state the destination first and the reagent follows.
• YOUR TURN — Predict: (a) 2-methylpropanoic acid with Br2 and red P; (b) silver ethanoate with Br2 in CCl4. (Answer: (a) 2-bromo-2-methylpropanoic acid — the single alpha-H is replaced. (b) Bromomethane plus CO2 plus AgBr — Hunsdiecker, one carbon down from ethanoate.)
★ HIGHLIGHT — Editing an acid is a small toolkit: delete COOH (soda lime or Kolbe), brominate beside it (HVZ, needs alpha-H), or trade it for Br with one carbon's toll (Hunsdiecker, needs the silver salt).

Reaction Map and Named-Reaction Sheet
✔ Carbonyl facts: C is sp2, planar, about 120 degrees; C=O about 122 pm; dipole 2.3-2.8 D; reactivity HCHO > aldehydes > ketones, aliphatic > aromatic
✔ PCC in dry solvent stops a primary alcohol at the aldehyde; acidified K2Cr2O7 or KMnO4 in water runs it to the acid; Cu at 573 K dehydrogenates 1 degree to aldehyde, 2 degree to ketone
✔ Rosenmund: R-COCl + H2 over Pd/BaSO4 (poisoned) -> R-CHO; never formaldehyde, never ketones
✔ Stephen: R-CN + SnCl2, conc HCl, then hydrolysis -> R-CHO (chain plus one carbon from R-X)
✔ Etard: toluene + CrO2Cl2, then H2O -> benzaldehyde (product trapped as chromium complex)
✔ Gattermann-Koch: benzene + CO + HCl, anhydrous AlCl3/CuCl -> benzaldehyde; nitrobenzene fails
✔ Ketone builds: R-CN + R prime-MgX then H3O+ -> R-CO-R prime; benzene + R-COCl/AlCl3 -> aryl ketone; (CH3COO)2Ca on heating -> propanone; any formate salt in the pair -> aldehyde
✔ Addition mechanism: Nu- attacks carbonyl C (slow), alkoxide forms, protonation finishes; acid catalysis activates C, base catalysis activates Nu
✔ Cyanohydrin needs base catalyst (acid kills CN-); bisulphite adducts form for aldehydes, methyl ketones and small cyclic ketones only — benzophenone fails
✔ Grignard plus C=O then water: HCHO -> 1 degree alcohol, R-CHO -> 2 degree alcohol, ketone -> 3 degree alcohol; aldehyde + 2 ROH (dry HCl) -> acetal; ketone + ethylene glycol -> cyclic ketal
✔ 2,4-DNP (Brady reagent): yellow-orange-red precipitate with aldehydes AND ketones; nitrogen derivatives (oxime, hydrazone, phenylhydrazone, semicarbazone) form best in weakly acidic medium
✔ Aldol: needs alpha-H and dilute NaOH; beta-hydroxy carbonyl first, alpha,beta-unsaturated carbonyl on heating; ethanal -> 3-hydroxybutanal -> but-2-enal
✔ Cannizzaro: needs NO alpha-H and concentrated KOH; 2 R-CHO -> R-CH2OH + R-COO-K+; formaldehyde is always the oxidised partner in crossed Cannizzaro
✔ Tollens: aldehydes (plus alpha-hydroxy ketones like fructose, and formic acid) -> silver mirror; ketones negative
✔ Fehling and Benedict: ALIPHATIC aldehydes -> red Cu2O; benzaldehyde NEGATIVE; Schiff reagent: aldehydes restore pink, ketones do not
✔ Iodoform (I2/NaOH): positive for CH3-CO- compounds, ethanal, ethanol and CH3-CH(OH)- alcohols -> yellow CHI3, mp 392 K; pentan-3-one, benzaldehyde, propan-1-ol, methanol negative
✔ Reduction depths: NaBH4 reduces aldehydes/ketones only; LiAlH4 reduces acids and esters too; Clemmensen (Zn-Hg, conc HCl) and Wolff-Kishner (NH2NH2, KOH, heat) both take C=O to CH2 — Clemmensen for acid-stable, Wolff-Kishner for acid-sensitive substrates
✔ Acidity ladder (pKa): CCl3COOH 0.65 < CHCl2COOH 1.29 < CH2ClCOOH 2.87 < HCOOH 3.75 < C6H5COOH 4.20 < CH3COOH 4.76 < C6H5OH about 10; withdrawers strengthen, donors weaken, distance fades the effect, ortho substituents strengthen benzoic acids
✔ Acid tests and builds: NaHCO3 effervescence for COOH but not phenol; Fischer esterification — acid gives OH, alcohol gives H; SOCl2 -> acyl chloride; LiAlH4 -> primary alcohol
✔ HVZ: alpha-H acid + Br2/red P -> alpha-bromo acid (formic and benzoic acids excluded); soda-lime decarboxylation -> alkane with one C less; Hunsdiecker: dry R-COOAg + Br2/CCl4 -> R-Br with one C less

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• MISTAKE 1 — "All aldehydes reduce Fehling's solution." WHY IT TEMPTS: the aliphatic examples dominate the textbook's demonstration bench. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: Fehling and Benedict oxidise only ALIPHATIC aldehydes; benzaldehyde reduces Tollens but leaves Fehling untouched. The aromatic ring's aldehyde is the standing exception.
• MISTAKE 2 — "Ketones never give Tollens." WHY IT TEMPTS: it is the clean rule behind the silver-mirror test. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: ordinary ketones fail, but ALPHA-HYDROXY KETONES pass — fructose gives a silver mirror because alkali rearranges it towards an aldehyde. Rule first, registered exception second.
• MISTAKE 3 — "Iodoform is the test for ketones." WHY IT TEMPTS: methyl ketones are its poster compounds. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: the test reads the CH3-CO unit or its precursor CH3-CH(OH) — so ethanal and ethanol pass, while pentan-3-one, a genuine ketone with no methyl on the carbonyl, fails.
• MISTAKE 4 — Offering Cannizzaro to acetaldehyde. WHY IT TEMPTS: aldehyde plus concentrated KOH matches the recipe superficially. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: Cannizzaro demands NO alpha-hydrogen; acetaldehyde has three and follows the enolate road (aldol) whatever the concentration. Check the alpha carbon before the reagent bottle.
• MISTAKE 5 — Self-aldol of benzaldehyde. WHY IT TEMPTS: "aldehyde plus dilute NaOH" pattern-matching. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: no alpha-H means no enolate and no aldol; benzaldehyde can only be the ELECTROPHILE partner in a crossed aldol, and with concentrated alkali alone it Cannizzaros instead.
• MISTAKE 6 — Rosenmund on the wrong substrate. WHY IT TEMPTS: "reduce something carbonyl-ish with Pd/BaSO4, get aldehyde." CORRECT PRINCIPLE: the substrate is the ACYL CHLORIDE and the catalyst is deliberately poisoned; feed it a carboxylic acid and nothing happens, and expect a ketone from it never — acyl chlorides have one carbon substituent, so the product must be an aldehyde.
• MISTAKE 7 — Stopping Clemmensen or Wolff-Kishner at the alcohol. WHY IT TEMPTS: reduction in neighbouring sections stops at alcohols. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: both named reductions take C=O ALL the way to CH2; the alcohol-stoppers are the hydrides and catalytic hydrogen. Depth is part of the reagent's identity.
• MISTAKE 8 — NaBH4 for every reduction. WHY IT TEMPTS: it is the friendly, safe hydride and students over-trust it. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: NaBH4 reduces only aldehydes and ketones; carboxylic acids and esters need LiAlH4. And LiAlH4, in turn, never stops an acid at the aldehyde.
• MISTAKE 9 — HVZ on benzoic acid or formic acid. WHY IT TEMPTS: "acid plus bromine plus red P" looks universal. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: HVZ brominates an ALPHA carbon bearing hydrogen; benzoic acid's carboxyl hangs directly on the ring and formic acid has no alpha carbon — both are outside the reaction's scope.
• MISTAKE 10 — Reading the pKa ladder upside down. WHY IT TEMPTS: bigger numbers feel stronger under pressure. CORRECT PRINCIPLE: pKa is a negative logarithm — trichloroacetic acid at 0.65 is far stronger than acetic acid at 4.76, and phenol at about 10 is weaker than every true carboxylic acid on the ladder. Anchor on the extremes and interpolate.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — One alcohol, two oxidants. Problem: predict the product of ethanol with (a) PCC in dichloromethane, (b) excess hot acidified K2Cr2O7.
• Given: a primary alcohol and two oxidant systems, one dry and mild, one aqueous and strong. Principle: aqueous strong oxidants oxidise the aldehyde's hydrate onward to the acid; dry PCC cannot, so it stops at the aldehyde. Steps: (a) ethanol loses two hydrogens and stops — ethanal. (b) ethanol climbs the whole ladder — ethanoic acid. Answer: (a) ethanal; (b) ethanoic acid.
• Reasoning: the substrate is identical; the conditions choose the floor of the ladder. Mistake: answering "ethanal" for both because "oxidation of a primary alcohol gives an aldehyde" — that slogan silently assumes a controlled oxidant.
• WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — Chain extension to an aldehyde. Problem: convert bromoethane into propanal, naming each step.
• Given: a two-carbon halide and a three-carbon aldehyde target — one carbon must be added and the chain must end in -CHO. Principle: KCN installs the extra carbon as a nitrile; Stephen reduction converts a nitrile to the aldehyde without overshooting. Steps: bromoethane plus KCN gives propanenitrile; SnCl2/concentrated HCl reduces it to the imine salt; hydrolysis releases propanal. Answer: propanal, via propanenitrile, Stephen reduction.
• Reasoning: the carbon audit (2 -> 3) selects the nitrile route, and the aldehyde target selects Stephen over nitrile hydrolysis (which would give propanoic acid). Mistake: reaching for Rosenmund — there is no acyl chloride in this chain and building one costs extra steps; the nitrile is already the aldehyde's masked form.
• WORKED EXAMPLE 3 — Cannizzaro bookkeeping. Problem: 2 mol of benzaldehyde are treated with excess concentrated KOH. State the products and the amount of each.
• Given: benzaldehyde — an aldehyde with no alpha-hydrogen — plus concentrated alkali: the Cannizzaro signature. Principle: disproportionation pairs the molecules; one is reduced, one oxidised. Steps: of the 2 mol, 1 mol accepts hydride and becomes benzyl alcohol; 1 mol donates hydride and becomes potassium benzoate (benzoic acid after acidic work-up). Answer: 1 mol benzyl alcohol and 1 mol potassium benzoate.
• Reasoning: the 2-to-1-to-1 ratio is built into disproportionation — the aldehyde is simultaneously the only oxidant and the only reductant present. Mistake: writing 2 mol of each product (violates conservation), or sending benzaldehyde down the aldol road (it has no alpha-hydrogen to enolise).
• WORKED EXAMPLE 4 — The three-tube identification. Problem: unlabelled tubes contain ethanal, benzaldehyde and propanone. Using only Tollens reagent, Fehling solution and 2,4-DNP, identify each tube.
• Given: an aliphatic aldehyde, an aromatic aldehyde and a ketone, and three tests with known verdict tables. Principle: apply the tests in discriminating order — DNP confirms carbonyl, Tollens splits aldehyde from ketone, Fehling splits aliphatic from aromatic aldehyde. Steps: all three tubes give an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNP (all are carbonyls). Tollens: ethanal and benzaldehyde give silver mirrors; propanone stays clear — propanone identified. Fehling, heated: the ethanal tube throws a red Cu2O precipitate; the benzaldehyde tube does nothing — ethanal and benzaldehyde identified. Answer: DNP all positive; Tollens separates propanone; Fehling separates ethanal from benzaldehyde.
• Reasoning: each test is aimed at the distinction it alone can make; benzaldehyde's Fehling silence is the decisive observation, not an experimental failure. Mistake: concluding "no Fehling reaction, so not an aldehyde" for the benzaldehyde tube — that is the exact misconception this classic practical is designed to expose.
• WORKED EXAMPLE 5 — Aldol prediction with dehydration. Problem: propanal is stirred with dilute NaOH and the product is then heated. Give the final product and the route.
• Given: an aldehyde with alpha-hydrogens (the CH2 beside CHO) and dilute base — aldol conditions. Principle: enolate of one molecule attacks the carbonyl of another; dehydration then conjugates the new C=C with the carbonyl. Steps: hydroxide takes an alpha-H from C2 of one propanal, the enolate carbon bonds to the carbonyl carbon of a second propanal, and protonation gives 3-hydroxy-2-methylpentanal; on heating, water leaves (OH from C3, H from C2) to give the alpha,beta-unsaturated aldehyde. Answer: 2-methylpent-2-enal.
• Reasoning: mark the enolate carbon and the electrophile carbon, join them, then delete water across the alpha-beta bond — the branched skeleton is the signature of the join. Mistake: dehydrating towards the terminal side to give 2-methylpent-1-enal styles of product; the double bond forms between the alpha and beta carbons of the aldol (C2 and C3), conjugated with the aldehyde, because that is where the OH sits.
• WORKED EXAMPLE 6 — Choosing the deep-reduction reagent. Problem: a ketone that also carries an acid-labile cyclic acetal group must be converted to the corresponding CH2 compound, acetal intact. Choose between Clemmensen and Wolff-Kishner and justify.
• Given: a substrate with two sensitive features — the target ketone and an acetal that hydrolyses in acid but survives base. Principle: both reductions reach CH2; they differ only in the medium they drag the molecule through (concentrated HCl versus hot KOH). Steps: Clemmensen's refluxing concentrated HCl would hydrolyse the acetal while reducing the ketone — collateral damage. Wolff-Kishner conditions (hydrazine, then KOH in ethylene glycol at about 453 K) never expose the molecule to acid, and acetals are base-stable, so only the ketone is reduced. Answer: Wolff-Kishner, because the substrate is acid-sensitive.
• Reasoning: the choice is read off the substrate's other functional group, not off the carbonyl — both reagents perform identically on the C=O itself. Mistake: choosing Clemmensen out of habit (or for its Friedel-Crafts association) and destroying the protecting group you were meant to preserve.
• WORKED EXAMPLE 7 — The haloform conversion. Problem: convert acetophenone into benzoic acid in one reaction step, naming the visible observation.
• Given: acetophenone, C6H5-CO-CH3 — an aryl methyl ketone, carrying the CH3-CO unit. Principle: the haloform reaction cleaves a methyl ketone to the carboxylate with one carbon fewer, surrendering the methyl carbon as haloform. Steps: warm acetophenone with I2 and NaOH; the methyl group is tri-iodinated and expelled as iodoform while the benzoyl fragment becomes sodium benzoate; acidify the solution to precipitate benzoic acid. The observation during the step: a yellow precipitate of CHI3. Answer: benzoic acid, via haloform cleavage, with yellow iodoform as the visible signature.
• Reasoning: the reaction does double duty — it is simultaneously the conversion and the confirmatory test for the CH3-CO group you started from. Mistake: proposing KMnO4 oxidation of acetophenone as a one-step equivalent — strong oxidation of a ketone needs forcing conditions and cleaves unselectively; the haloform route is the clean, named, examinable answer.

• Q6 — Arrange in decreasing strength: acetic acid, propanoic acid, benzoic acid, trifluoroacetic acid. Give the pKa of the strongest.
• Q7 — Which of propanoic acid, benzoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid does NOT undergo the HVZ reaction, and why not?

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• A1 — Chromyl chloride, CrO2Cl2, in CS2, followed by hydrolysis — the Etard reaction. The benzaldehyde is trapped inside the brown chromium complex as it forms, so the oxidant never gets a second shot at it; hydrolysis releases it unchanged. Misconception caught: answering "controlled oxidation with any mild oxidant" — without the complex trap, aldehyde oxidation runs to benzoic acid.
• A2 — Ethanol fails: it has no carbonyl group, and 2,4-DNP responds only to the C=O of aldehydes and ketones. The failure proves the absence of an aldehyde/ketone carbonyl (not merely the absence of an aldehyde — propanone and benzaldehyde precipitate just as ethanal does). Misconception caught: treating DNP as an aldehyde test; it is a carbonyl test.
• A3 — Benzyl alcohol (the reduced half) and potassium benzoate (the oxidised half): the Cannizzaro reaction. Benzaldehyde has no alpha-hydrogen, so concentrated alkali forces disproportionation. Misconception caught: expecting benzoic acid directly, or expecting aldol products from an aldehyde that cannot enolise.
• A4 — The compound is propanone: the iodoform verdict requires CH3-CO, and the silent Tollens excludes the aldehyde isomer. The other isomer, propanal, gives NO iodoform (its carbonyl carries an ethyl, not a methyl) and a POSITIVE Tollens silver mirror. Misconception caught: assuming C3H6O isomers share test verdicts — the two tests read different structural features, so the isomers split perfectly.
• A5 — Butane, by Clemmensen reduction: Zn-Hg/concentrated HCl takes the carbonyl all the way down to CH2, so butan-2-one becomes the parent alkane. Misconception caught: answering butan-2-ol — that is the hydride depth; Clemmensen is a deep reduction.
• A6 — Trifluoroacetic acid (pKa 0.23) > benzoic acid (4.20) > acetic acid (4.76) > propanoic acid (4.87). Three fluorines haul charge off the anion; benzoic edges acetic on the sp2 carbon's pull; propanoic trails because its ethyl donates slightly more than methyl. Misconception caught: ranking by pKa size instead of by value — the strongest acid owns the smallest number.
• A7 — Benzoic acid does not undergo HVZ: its -COOH is attached directly to the benzene ring, so there is no alpha carbon and no alpha-hydrogen to brominate. Propanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid both have alpha-hydrogens and react normally. Misconception caught: assuming every carboxylic acid qualifies — the reaction lives on the alpha carbon, and benzoic acid does not own one.
• A8 — First 3-hydroxybutanal (the aldol, from self-condensation of two ethanal molecules), then on heating but-2-enal (crotonaldehyde), the alpha,beta-unsaturated aldehyde. Misconception caught: reporting only the dehydrated product and skipping the aldol itself — examiners ask for the intermediate because it proves you know dehydration is a second, separate step.

Chapter Summary and Revision Checklist
• The carbonyl group is the chapter's sun: flat, polar, delta-positive at carbon. Nucleophiles attack it in a two-step addition through an alkoxide; reactivity falls as alkyl and aryl groups crowd and feed it, from formaldehyde down to benzophenone. The addition menu is one mechanism with many nucleophiles — CN- (needs base), HSO3- (sterically fussy, endlessly useful), Grignard reagents (alcohol builders), alcohols (acetals, the protecting groups), and nitrogen derivatives that crystallise carbonyls into identifiable derivatives, crowned by the 2,4-DNP test.
• Preparations are braking problems: aldehydes are intermediates that every reagent wants to overrun, so the named gateways exist to stop the climb — PCC by dryness, Rosenmund by a poisoned catalyst, Stephen at an imine, Etard inside a chromium complex, Gattermann-Koch by direct formylation. Ketones arrive by assembly (nitrile plus Grignard), by acylation, or by the calcium-salt patterns with their formate rule.
• The alpha-hydrogen forks the chapter's base chemistry: present, it makes enolates and aldol condensation (the one true C-C construction here, finished by dehydration to conjugated enals); absent, concentrated alkali forces Cannizzaro disproportionation, with formaldehyde always the oxidised partner when crossed.
• Tests and reductions are verdict tables: Tollens for aldehydes (fructose and formic acid registered as exceptions), Fehling for aliphatic aldehydes only — benzaldehyde is the planted failure — iodoform for the CH3-CO unit in either oxidation dress, and the reduction depths split between hydrides (alcohol) and Clemmensen/Wolff-Kishner (methylene), the pair chosen by the substrate's acid tolerance.
• Carboxylic acids close the ladder: dimeric, high-boiling, made by oxidation, by chain extension (nitrile hydrolysis, Grignard carboxylation) or by unmasking (trihalide hydrolysis). Their strength is anion stability expressed as pKa — two-oxygen resonance, strengthened by withdrawers placed close, weakened by donors, and twisted upward by the ortho effect — and their chemistry edits chains: salts and bicarbonate effervescence, Fischer esters, derivatives, HVZ at the alpha carbon, and the one-carbon deletions of soda lime, Kolbe and Hunsdiecker.
• Self-assessment — be honest, tick only what you can do unaided:
• I can rank any set of aldehydes and ketones by nucleophilic-addition reactivity and defend the order with steric and electronic reasons
• I can name the gateway (Rosenmund, Stephen, Etard, Gattermann-Koch) from its reagents alone and state each one's substrate and limit
• I can write the two-step addition mechanism and predict products for CN-, HSO3-, Grignard, alcohol and 2,4-DNP nucleophiles, including who fails and why
• I can decide aldol versus Cannizzaro from the alpha-hydrogen alone, predict both product sets, and handle the crossed versions
• I can run the identification drill — DNP, Tollens, Fehling, iodoform — and place ethanal, benzaldehyde, a ketone and an alcohol in their boxes without hesitating on benzaldehyde and Fehling
• I can choose a reduction by depth and by acid tolerance, and state exactly which groups NaBH4 and LiAlH4 will and will not touch
• I can rank carboxylic acids by pKa logic — substituent, distance, resonance and the ortho effect — and convert between acids, esters, chlorides and amides
• I can edit a chain with HVZ, soda lime, Kolbe and Hunsdiecker, stating each reaction's scope limit before its product
★ HIGHLIGHT — If that checklist runs cold, this chapter is yours: the IAT recycles a tight cast — carbonyl polarity, four gateways, one addition mechanism, the alpha-hydrogen fork, three verdict tests, two reduction depths and one acidity ladder — and every member of the cast is now in your hands.
