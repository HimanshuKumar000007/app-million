Introduction — The Molecules That Run You
• Open any living cell and four families of molecules do nearly all the work: carbohydrates burn for energy and store it, proteins build structures and run chemistry as enzymes, nucleic acids store and execute the instructions, and vitamins fine-tune the machinery in trace amounts. This chapter is the chemistry of life itself — and it is one of the most fact-dense, memorisation-heavy chapters in the IAT syllabus.
• DEFINITION — Biomolecules are the organic compounds found in living organisms — carbohydrates, proteins, enzymes, vitamins, nucleic acids and hormones — whose structures and reactions sustain life processes.
• WHY this chapter scores — almost every question here is a classification, a linkage or a matching fact: which sugar is non-reducing, which bond is broken, which vitamin matches which disease. There is very little calculation, so the winning strategy is a perfect table in your head, not fast arithmetic.
• EXAMPLE — The energy in your breakfast toast is starch (a carbohydrate), the muscle you lift the toast with is protein, the instructions that built that muscle are DNA, and the amylase that starts digesting the toast in your mouth is an enzyme. One bite, four biomolecule families.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The chapter in one breath: carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones classified by hydrolysis, amino acids join by peptide bonds into proteins whose shape decides their job, enzymes are protein catalysts of extreme specificity, vitamins are diet-essential helpers split by solubility, and nucleic acids pair A with T and G with C to store the code of life.
• A word on hormones, the fifth family — chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands. Insulin, a protein hormone from the pancreas, keeps blood glucose in check; adrenaline, the emergency hormone, readies the body for fight or flight. They get cameo roles here because the detailed endocrinology belongs to biology — but remembering those two examples wins easy marks.
• Here is the map. Carbohydrates first — glucose in depth, then di- and polysaccharides. Then amino acids and the four levels of protein structure, denaturation and enzymes. Vitamins next. Nucleic acids last, ending with the double helix and the central dogma.

Carbohydrates — Classification Done Right
• DEFINITION — Carbohydrates are optically active polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds that produce such units on hydrolysis. The old name came from hydrates of carbon, Cn(H2O)n — glucose C6H12O6 fits it, but the definition above is the correct modern one.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The formula Cn(H2O)n is neither necessary nor sufficient. Rhamnose, C6H12O5, is a carbohydrate that breaks the formula, while acetic acid, C2H4O2, fits the formula perfectly yet is no carbohydrate at all. Trust the polyhydroxy aldehyde or ketone definition, never the formula.
• The master classification runs on hydrolysis. DEFINITION — Monosaccharides cannot be hydrolysed further into simpler sugars (glucose, fructose, ribose). Oligosaccharides give 2 to 10 monosaccharide units on hydrolysis — disaccharides like sucrose give exactly 2. Polysaccharides give a very large number of units (starch, cellulose).
• The second axis is the carbonyl group. DEFINITION — An aldose carries an aldehyde group (glucose, ribose); a ketose carries a ketone group (fructose). Combined names follow: glucose is an aldohexose (aldehyde + six carbons), fructose a ketohexose.
• The third axis is the one examiners probe hardest — reducing power. DEFINITION — A reducing sugar reduces Fehling's solution and Benedict's reagent (giving red copper(I) oxide) and Tollens' reagent (giving a silver mirror); it must possess a free aldehyde group, or a group that can open into one.
✔ KEY RULE — ALL monosaccharides are reducing sugars, aldoses and ketoses alike. Among disaccharides, those with a free anomeric carbon (maltose, lactose) are reducing; sucrose, whose anomeric carbons are both locked, is non-reducing.
• WHY ketoses still reduce — fructose has no aldehyde, but under the alkaline conditions of Fehling's and Benedict's tests it rearranges (through an enediol intermediate) into glucose and mannose, which do carry aldehydes. That is why the ketose fructose passes every reducing test — a favourite assertion-reason trap.
• EXAMPLE — Honey is mostly glucose plus fructose from hydrolysed sucrose, so honey reduces Fehling's solution; table sugar itself does not. Same sweetness family, opposite test result, and the difference is one glycosidic bond.
• YOUR TURN — Classify ribose, sucrose and starch on both axes (hydrolysis and carbonyl/reducing where relevant). Answers — ribose: monosaccharide, aldose (aldopentose), reducing; sucrose: disaccharide, non-reducing; starch: polysaccharide built from an aldose (glucose), and effectively non-reducing because only one end unit in a huge chain has a free anomeric carbon.

Glucose — The Star Monosaccharide
• DEFINITION — Glucose, C6H12O6, is an aldohexose — a six-carbon polyhydroxy aldehyde — also called dextrose because it is dextrorotatory (rotates plane-polarised light to the right). It occurs free in grapes and honey and combined in sucrose, starch and cellulose.
• The open-chain structure was proven reaction by reaction, and each proof is a standalone exam fact. WORKED PATTERN — read glucose's reactions as detective evidence:
• Evidence 1 — prolonged heating with HI gives n-hexane: the six carbons form one straight, unbranched chain.
• Evidence 2 — glucose reacts with hydroxylamine to form an oxime, and adds HCN to form a cyanohydrin: a carbonyl group is present.
• Evidence 3 — acetylation with acetic anhydride gives a pentaacetate: exactly five OH groups sit on five different carbons.
• Evidence 4 — mild oxidation with bromine water gives gluconic acid, a six-carbon acid: the carbonyl is an aldehyde, and only the aldehyde carbon was oxidised.
• Evidence 5 — strong oxidation with nitric acid gives saccharic acid, a dicarboxylic acid: the far end of the chain is a primary alcohol, CH2OH, which HNO3 oxidised too.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The verdict from the evidence: OHC-CHOH-CHOH-CHOH-CHOH-CH2OH — an aldehyde at C1, four secondary alcohols at C2 to C5, and a primary alcohol at C6. Bromine water touches only the aldehyde (gluconic acid); nitric acid takes both ends (saccharic acid). That contrast is the single most reused glucose fact in entrance papers.
• Glucose does reduce Tollens' reagent to a silver mirror and Fehling's solution to red copper(I) oxide — exactly what its aldehyde group predicts.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — But glucose gives NO colour with 2,4-DNP and does NOT react with sodium bisulphite, although aldehydes should do both. This contradiction is the clue that glucose does not live mainly in its open-chain form — the next section resolves it.
• YOUR TURN — A hexose gives a tetracetate on acetylation instead of a pentaacetate. What changes in your structural conclusion? Answer — it carries only four OH groups, so it cannot be glucose; the pentaacetate is glucose's fingerprint of five hydroxyls.

▸ Cyclic Glucose — Anomers and Mutarotation
• DEFINITION — In solution, glucose curls into a ring: the OH group on C5 attacks the aldehyde carbon C1 internally, forming a six-membered ring of five carbons and one oxygen — a pyranose ring — and C1 becomes a new stereocentre called the anomeric carbon.
• Two rings are possible because the new OH on C1 can point two ways. DEFINITION — Anomers are stereoisomers that differ only in the configuration at the anomeric carbon. In alpha-D-glucose the anomeric OH lies on the opposite side to the CH2OH group (drawn DOWN in the Haworth projection); in beta-D-glucose it lies on the same side (drawn UP).
• In the Haworth projection the ring is drawn flat with the oxygen at the back right corner; groups that were on the right in the Fischer projection go down, groups on the left go up. For D-glucose this gives: C1 OH down (alpha) or up (beta), C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH down, and CH2OH up at C5 — see this section's figure for the full Fischer-to-Haworth conversion.
• The cyclic form explains the missing aldehyde reactions: only a tiny trace of the open-chain aldehyde exists at any instant, too little for 2,4-DNP or bisulphite tests — yet enough for Tollens' and Fehling's, because as the trace reacts, more ring opens to replace it.
• DEFINITION — Mutarotation is the spontaneous change in the specific rotation of a freshly dissolved sugar as its anomers interconvert through the open-chain form until equilibrium is reached.
✔ KEY RULE — Pure alpha-D-glucose starts at a specific rotation of +112 degrees and pure beta-D-glucose at +19 degrees; both settle at +52.7 degrees, the equilibrium mixture of about 36 percent alpha and 64 percent beta. Mutarotation is possible ONLY because the anomeric carbon is free — lock it, and rotation can never change.
• WORKED PATTERN — verifying the 36/64 split: let x be the alpha fraction; then 112x + 19(1 minus x) = 52.7, so 93x = 33.7 and x = 0.36. Two data points and one weighted average — this is the only real calculation in the carbohydrate half of the chapter.
• YOUR TURN — Why does sucrose show no mutarotation although both its units are sugars? Answer — mutarotation needs a free anomeric carbon to open and reclose; in sucrose both anomeric carbons are consumed by the glycosidic bond, so neither ring can open.

Fructose and the Monosaccharide Family
• DEFINITION — Fructose, C6H12O6, is a ketohexose — the carbonyl sits at C2 as a ketone — found in fruits and honey, laevorotatory, and the sweetest of all sugars. It is also called fruit sugar.
• Fructose shares glucose's straight-chain proofs (n-hexane with HI, pentaacetate) but oxidation cuts it differently: as a ketose it has no aldehyde to oxidise mildly, yet it still reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents through the alkaline rearrangement you met in the classification section.
• In its free form fructose also cyclises, but as a five-membered furanose ring (four carbons plus one oxygen), and in sucrose it is this furanose form that appears. Its anomeric carbon is C2, not C1 — because the carbonyl carbon of a ketose becomes the anomeric centre.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Glucose versus fructose, the headline contrast: aldehyde at C1 versus ketone at C2; dextrorotatory versus laevorotatory; six-membered pyranose ring when free versus five-membered furanose ring in sucrose; anomeric carbon C1 versus C2. Four contrasts, one favourite two-mark question.
• The wider family, each with its signature job: ribose — the aldopentose of RNA; 2-deoxyribose — the sugar of DNA, missing one oxygen at C2; galactose — a monosaccharide unit of lactose, differing from glucose only at C4; mannose — the C2 epimer of glucose appearing in the fructose rearrangement.
• DEFINITION — Epimers are stereoisomers differing at exactly one stereocentre. Glucose and galactose are C4 epimers; glucose and mannose are C2 epimers. The anomers of glucose are simply its C1 epimers, given their own name because C1 is the carbonyl-born centre.
• YOUR TURN — Which monosaccharides appear when (a) sucrose and (b) lactose are hydrolysed? Answers — (a) glucose + fructose; (b) glucose + galactose.

Disaccharides — Sucrose, Maltose and Lactose
• DEFINITION — A glycosidic linkage is the ether-like C-O-C bond formed when two monosaccharides join with the loss of one water molecule, the anomeric carbon of one unit bonding through oxygen to a carbon of the other. Hydrolysis with dilute acid or an enzyme reverses it.
• Sucrose, common table sugar, C12H22O11, joins alpha-D-glucose and beta-D-fructose. The linkage runs from C1 of glucose to C2 of fructose — head to head, anomeric carbon to anomeric carbon.
✔ KEY RULE — Sucrose is NON-REDUCING because both anomeric carbons are locked inside its glycosidic bond: neither ring can open to expose an aldehyde or alpha-hydroxy ketone, so Fehling's, Benedict's and Tollens' tests all stay negative — and it shows no mutarotation either.
• Sucrose is dextrorotatory (+66.5 degrees), but hydrolysis flips the sign of the mixture: glucose contributes +52.7 degrees while fructose pulls strongly at minus 92 degrees, so the hydrolysed mixture is net laevorotatory. DEFINITION — This sign flip is called inversion of sugar, the enzyme responsible is invertase, and the product mixture is marketed as invert sugar.
• Maltose, the sugar of germinating grain and brewing, joins two alpha-D-glucose units: C1 of the first to C4 of the second. One anomeric carbon stays free, so maltose IS reducing and does show mutarotation.
• Lactose, milk sugar, joins beta-D-galactose (C1) to beta-D-glucose (C4). The glucose unit keeps a free anomeric carbon, so lactose is reducing; the enzyme lactase splits it, and people lacking lactase are lactose-intolerant.
• WORKED PATTERN — the reducing test verdict for any disaccharide takes three seconds: ask whether ANY anomeric carbon is unused by the glycosidic bond. Free anomeric carbon present — reducing (maltose, lactose). Both anomeric carbons consumed — non-reducing (sucrose, the only common case). There is no third answer.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Do not argue "sucrose contains glucose, and glucose is reducing, so sucrose must reduce Fehling's." Reducing power belongs to the free anomeric carbon, not to the ingredient list — once locked in the bond, the glucose unit inside sucrose has lost its reducing power completely.
• YOUR TURN — A disaccharide gives a positive Benedict's test and on hydrolysis yields only glucose. Name it and defend the answer. Answer — maltose: only-glucose hydrolysis points to maltose, and its C1-to-C4 linkage leaves the second glucose's anomeric carbon free, hence the positive test.

Polysaccharides — Starch, Cellulose and Glycogen
• DEFINITION — Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic bonds — hundreds to thousands of units — serving as energy stores (starch, glycogen) or structural material (cellulose). They are not sweet and are colloquially called non-sugars.
• Starch, the plant energy store, is a polymer of alpha-D-glucose in two components. DEFINITION — Amylose (about 15 to 20 percent) is a long unbranched chain of C1-to-C4 alpha linkages that coils into a helix; it is the part that turns blue-black with iodine, because iodine molecules lodge inside the helix.
• DEFINITION — Amylopectin (about 80 to 85 percent) has the same C1-to-C4 alpha chains but with branch points where a C1 joins a C6 of another chain — alpha-1,6 linkages every 24 to 30 units. The branching lets enzymes attack many ends at once, releasing glucose fast.
• Glycogen, animal starch, is amylopectin's more extreme cousin: the same alpha-1,4 and alpha-1,6 architecture but branched every 8 to 12 units, stored in liver and muscle as the animal's rapid glucose reserve.
• Cellulose flips one stereochemical switch and changes everything. DEFINITION — Cellulose is a straight, unbranched chain of beta-D-glucose units joined by beta-1,4 linkages; neighbouring chains hydrogen-bond into tough fibres. Cotton is almost pure cellulose.
✔ KEY RULE — Alpha versus beta decides digestibility: human enzymes (amylases) hydrolyse alpha-1,4 and alpha-1,6 linkages but NOT beta-1,4, so we digest starch and glycogen yet cannot digest cellulose — it passes through as dietary fibre. Grazing animals manage only because gut microbes supply the missing cellulase.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The polysaccharide table in one line: starch = alpha glucose, coiled, storage, plants; glycogen = alpha glucose, most branched, storage, animals; cellulose = beta glucose, straight ribbons, structure, plants. Same monomer, three architectures, three jobs — decided purely by the linkage geometry in this section's comparison figure.
• Because one giant molecule has effectively one free anomeric end among thousands of units, polysaccharides behave as non-reducing in practice and give no Fehling's or Tollens' response worth measuring.
• YOUR TURN — Iodine solution turns blue-black with one polysaccharide but not the others. Which, and why? Answer — starch (specifically amylose): iodine slips into the amylose helix and the trapped iodine changes colour; cellulose's straight ribbon has no helix to host it.

Amino Acids — The Alphabet of Proteins
• DEFINITION — Amino acids are organic molecules carrying both an amino group (NH2) and a carboxyl group (COOH). In the alpha-amino acids of proteins, both groups sit on the SAME carbon — the alpha carbon — which also carries a hydrogen and a variable side chain R. The whole diversity of proteins comes from varying that one R group.
• The general picture: NH2-CHR-COOH. Glycine, with R = H, is the simplest amino acid and the only common one whose alpha carbon is NOT a stereocentre — so glycine is optically inactive, while all other standard alpha-amino acids are optically active and (in proteins) of the L-configuration.
• EXAMPLE — Read the R groups as personalities: alanine R = CH3 (plain hydrocarbon), serine R = CH2OH ( carries an extra OH), cysteine R = CH2SH (sulphur — the disulphide-bond maker), glutamic acid R = CH2CH2COOH (an extra acid), lysine R = (CH2)4NH2 (an extra base).
• DEFINITION — Essential amino acids cannot be synthesised by the human body and must arrive through diet; non-essential ones the body can make itself. Essential does NOT mean more important — it means outsourced.
✔ KEY RULE — The ten essential amino acids: valine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, threonine, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, histidine and arginine. Every other standard amino acid — glycine, alanine, glutamic acid, aspartic acid and the rest — is non-essential.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Eggs and milk are called complete or biologically perfect proteins because they supply all essential amino acids in the right proportions; most single plant proteins are short of at least one. "Essential" questions quietly test the diet meaning, not the chemistry meaning.
• Amino acids are colourless crystalline solids with high melting points and good water solubility for organic molecules — suspiciously salt-like behaviour, which the next section explains with the zwitterion.

▸ Zwitterions and the Classification Grid
• DEFINITION — A zwitterion (German for hybrid ion) is a dipolar ion carrying both a positive and a negative charge in the same molecule, yet neutral overall. In aqueous solution an amino acid's COOH donates its proton to its own NH2 group, giving NH3(+)-CHR-COO(-).
• The zwitterion explains the salt-like physics: strong ionic attractions in the crystal mean high melting points, good water solubility and near-insolubility in organic solvents. Amino acids behave as internal salts because, literally, they are.
• DEFINITION — The isoelectric point is the pH at which the amino acid exists almost entirely as the neutral zwitterion and does not migrate in an electric field. Add acid and the COO(-) picks up a proton — the molecule becomes a net cation that migrates to the cathode; add base and the NH3(+) loses its proton — a net anion migrating to the anode.
✔ KEY RULE — Amino acids are amphoteric: acidic medium makes them cations (+NH3, COOH), basic medium makes them anions (NH2, COO-), and at the isoelectric point they are zwitterions with zero net charge. Examiners ask all three states; draw the proton shuffle, do not memorise blindly.
• Classification by side chain, the second grid to memorise: neutral amino acids have one NH2 and one COOH (glycine, alanine, valine); acidic ones carry an extra COOH (glutamic acid, aspartic acid); basic ones carry an extra NH2 (lysine, arginine, histidine — the isoelectric points of basic amino acids lie high, of acidic ones low).
• Two special families worth flagging: sulphur-containing amino acids — cysteine and methionine — and aromatic ones — phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan — whose ring side chains absorb ultraviolet light, which is how protein concentration is often measured in the lab.
• YOUR TURN — At pH 1, in what form does glycine mainly exist, and towards which electrode will it move? Answer — the cation form NH3(+)-CH2-COOH, because acid protonates the COO(-); it migrates towards the cathode (the negative electrode).

Peptide Bonds and Primary Structure
• DEFINITION — A peptide bond (peptide linkage) is the amide bond, CO-NH, formed when the COOH group of one amino acid condenses with the NH2 group of the next, eliminating one water molecule. It is a covalent bond, and breaking it again requires hydrolysis.
• Counting pattern, used constantly: two amino acids make a dipeptide with ONE peptide bond; a chain of n amino acids holds n minus 1 peptide bonds and was built by losing n minus 1 water molecules. A polypeptide is a long such chain; a chain of more than about a hundred residues, with molar mass above 10,000 units, earns the name protein — the boundary is a guideline, not a wall.
• DEFINITION — The primary structure of a protein is the exact sequence of amino acids in its chain — which amino acid follows which, from the N-terminal (free NH2 end) to the C-terminal (free COOH end). Peptide bonds alone hold it together.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Sequence is destiny's first draft: change ONE amino acid and the protein can fail — in sickle-cell haemoglobin, a single glutamic acid swapped for valine (position 6 of the beta chain) deforms red blood cells into sickles. Primary structure questions love this example.
• Frederick Sanger won a Nobel Prize for determining the first complete primary structure — insulin, 51 amino acids in two chains — proving proteins have exact, knowable sequences rather than statistical mixtures.
• WORKED PATTERN — peptide arithmetic: a pentapeptide (5 amino acids) contains 4 peptide bonds; forming it from free amino acids releases 4 water molecules; complete hydrolysis consumes 4 waters and returns the 5 amino acids. Scale the same idea: a protein of 200 residues has 199 peptide bonds.
• YOUR TURN — A tripeptide is built from glycine, alanine and serine in that order. How many peptide bonds, and what are its two ends? Answer — 2 peptide bonds; the N-terminal is glycine (free NH2) and the C-terminal is serine (free COOH).

▸ Secondary, Tertiary and Quaternary Structure
• DEFINITION — The secondary structure is the local folding pattern of the backbone, stabilised by hydrogen bonds between the C=O of one residue and the NH of another. It has two celebrity shapes, drawn side by side in this section's four-level figure.
• The alpha-helix: the chain twists into a right-handed screw, like a coiled spring, with hydrogen bonds running INSIDE the same chain between turns. Keratin of hair and wool is largely alpha-helical, which is why hair stretches and springs back.
• The beta-pleated sheet: chain segments lie stretched out side by side, zipped together by hydrogen bonds BETWEEN neighbouring segments. Silk fibroin is beta-sheet rich — strong, soft and barely stretchable, the opposite personality to keratin.
✔ KEY RULE — Alpha-helix hydrogen bonds are intramolecular (within one chain); beta-sheet hydrogen bonds are intermolecular (between chains or segments). This single distinction is asked relentlessly — do not swap them.
• DEFINITION — The tertiary structure is the complete three-dimensional folding of one whole polypeptide chain — helices and sheets packed into one compact shape, often globular. It represents the overall folding, including how distant parts of the sequence come close in space.
• The forces holding tertiary structure, all worth listing: hydrogen bonds between side chains, disulphide bonds (covalent S-S bridges between two cysteine residues — the strongest of the set), ionic or salt bridges between acidic and basic side chains, hydrophobic interactions (greasy side chains hiding inside, away from water) and van der Waals contacts.
• DEFINITION — The quaternary structure exists only in proteins made of several polypeptide chains: it is the arrangement of those subunits relative to each other, held by the same non-covalent forces (hydrogen bonds, electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions).
• EXAMPLE — Haemoglobin, the oxygen carrier of blood, is the textbook quaternary protein: four subunits — two alpha chains and two beta chains — each cradling an iron-containing haem group, assembled into one working machine. Insulin, by contrast, has two chains joined by disulphide bonds, a quaternary-flavoured arrangement at miniature scale.
• WHY the levels matter as a ladder — each level is built from the one below and held by weaker or equal forces: primary (covalent peptide bonds) is the hardest to undo, quaternary and tertiary the easiest. Remember that ordering, because denaturation — two sections from now — is nothing but this ladder being kicked from the top.
• YOUR TURN — A protein has one chain only, folded into a ball, and boiling destroys its activity without changing its molecular mass. Which structure levels can it NOT possess, and which were destroyed? Answer — it cannot possess quaternary structure (that needs multiple chains); boiling destroyed its secondary and tertiary structures while the primary sequence (and hence mass) survived.

Fibrous versus Globular — and Denaturation
• DEFINITION — Fibrous proteins have polypeptide chains running parallel, held together by hydrogen and disulphide bonds, forming fibre-like structures that are generally INSOLUBLE in water and serve structural roles: keratin (hair, nails, wool), myosin (muscle) and collagen (tendons, skin).
• DEFINITION — Globular proteins have chains folded into compact spherical shapes and are generally SOLUBLE in water, serving functional roles: insulin, albumin (egg and blood), haemoglobin — and virtually all enzymes.
• WORKED PATTERN — classify by job: if the protein's role is rope, sheet, armour or scaffold, it is fibrous; if its role is transport, catalysis, regulation or defence in solution, it is globular. Role predicts shape, and shape predicts solubility.
• DEFINITION — Denaturation is the unfolding and disorganisation of a protein's secondary, tertiary (and quaternary) structure under heat, pH change or chemicals, WITHOUT breaking its peptide bonds. The primary structure survives intact; the biological activity does not.
✔ KEY RULE — In denaturation: secondary and tertiary structures are destroyed, primary structure is retained, and biological activity is lost. Any option claiming peptide bonds break during simple boiling is wrong — breaking peptide bonds is hydrolysis, a different, harsher process.
• EXAMPLE — Boiling an egg: heat denatures the albumin, the clear soluble protein tangling into an opaque insoluble white — irreversibly, in the egg's case. Curdling of milk: bacteria convert lactose to lactic acid, the acid denatures the milk protein casein, and it separates as curd.
• Renaturation is sometimes possible — a few small proteins refold perfectly when the denaturing agent is removed, proving that the primary sequence alone carries the folding instructions. But scrambled egg never unscrambles: most real-life denaturation is effectively one-way.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Denatured protein loses its primary structure" is the most planted false statement in this chapter. Primary structure is NEVER lost in denaturation; it is lost only when peptide bonds are hydrolysed, for example by strong acid, heat over long periods, or protease enzymes.
• YOUR TURN — A boiled egg's white has mass unchanged and amino acid sequence unchanged, yet albumin no longer behaves as before. Which statement explains it? Answer — denaturation: the 2-degree and 3-degree folds are destroyed while the 1-degree sequence (hence mass) remains, so function is lost without any change in composition.

Enzymes — Biology's Specialist Catalysts
• DEFINITION — Enzymes are globular proteins that catalyse biochemical reactions with extraordinary speed and specificity, remaining unchanged at the end. Nearly every reaction in a cell — digestion, respiration, replication — runs on enzymes; without them, life chemistry would crawl.
• DEFINITION — The active site is the pocket on the enzyme's surface where the substrate binds and reacts. Two models describe the fit: the lock-and-key model (Emil Fischer) treats the site as a rigid mould matching the substrate exactly, while the induced-fit model (Koshland) lets the site flex and wrap around the substrate on contact — the modern refinement.
• WHY enzymes are catalysts in the chemist's sense — they lower the activation energy of the reaction by providing an alternative pathway, so far more molecules can cross the barrier per second at body temperature. They change the RATE, never the equilibrium or the energy difference between reactants and products.
✔ KEY RULE — Enzyme fingerprint: extreme specificity (maltase hydrolyses maltose only, not lactose or sucrose), optimum temperature near body temperature (about 310 K), optimum pH, activity rising with substrate concentration until the sites saturate, and denaturation by high heat — an enzyme is a protein first, a catalyst second, so boiling kills it.
• Naming runs two systems. The everyday system adds -ase to the substrate or reaction: sucrase (invertase) hydrolyses sucrose, maltase hydrolyses maltose, lipase hydrolyses lipids. The systematic system sorts enzymes into classes — oxidoreductases run oxidation-reduction between two substrates, transferases move groups, hydrolases break bonds with water, lyases split without water, isomerases rearrange, ligases join.
• Some enzymes need a helper — a non-protein cofactor (a metal ion or a small organic coenzyme, often vitamin-derived); the protein part alone is the apoenzyme, and only the complete holoenzyme works. This is one quiet bridge between the vitamin section and this one.
• EXAMPLE — Your saliva's amylase starts starch digestion in the mouth, pauses in the stomach's acid (wrong pH), and pancreatic amylase resumes it in the intestine — a living demonstration of optimum pH switching an enzyme off and a second supply switching the job back on.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — "Enzymes are used up in the reaction" and "enzymes shift the equilibrium towards products" are both false. Enzymes emerge unchanged and accelerate forward and reverse reactions equally; they decide how FAST equilibrium arrives, never where it lies.
• YOUR TURN — Why does a high fever become dangerous beyond about 41 degrees C, in enzyme language? Answer — essential enzymes begin to denature as temperature climbs past their optimum, unfolding their active sites; reaction networks the body depends on slow or fail even though the enzymes' primary structures are still present.

Vitamins — Tiny Doses, Serious Deficiencies
• DEFINITION — Vitamins are organic compounds required in very small amounts in the diet, which the body cannot synthesise (or not enough), essential for specific biological functions; their absence causes specific deficiency diseases. They provide no energy themselves — they are enablers, not fuel.
• The master split is solubility, and it predicts storage. DEFINITION — Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) dissolve in fats, are absorbed with dietary fat, and CAN be stored in the liver and fatty tissues — so they need not be eaten daily, but can accumulate to toxicity. Water-soluble vitamins (B-complex and C) dissolve in water, are NOT stored to any useful extent, and are excreted in urine — so they must be supplied regularly.
✔ KEY RULE — A, D, E, K are fat-soluble; B and C are water-soluble. Storage follows solubility: only the fat-soluble four are stockpiled in liver and adipose tissue. "Vitamin A is stored in the kidney" or "vitamin C is stored in the liver" are both planted errors.
• The deficiency table — memorise it as triples of vitamin, chemistry note, disease: Vitamin A — night blindness and xerophthalmia (hardening and drying of the cornea, which can progress to total blindness); sources include fish liver oils, carrots and green leafy vegetables.
• Vitamin B1 (thiamine) — beriberi, the disease of appetite loss, weak nerves and fluid retention on polished-rice diets. Vitamin B2 (riboflavin) — cheilosis (cracking and sores at the corners of the mouth), digestive disorders and a burning sensation of the skin. Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) — convulsions. 
★ HIGHLIGHT — Vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin) is chemically unique: it is the only vitamin containing a metal — COBALT — at its centre, and its deficiency causes pernicious anaemia, in which red blood cells end up deficient in haemoglobin. Cobalt plus pernicious anaemia plus B12 is a three-link chain examiners pull constantly.
• Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) — scurvy: bleeding, swollen gums and wounds that will not heal, the sailor's disease cured by citrus fruit and amla; being water-soluble and easily oxidised, it is also destroyed by cooking and storage faster than any other vitamin.
• Vitamin D — the sunshine vitamin, made in skin under sunlight: deficiency gives rickets in children (soft, bending bones) and osteomalacia in adults (soft bones and pain). Note the age pairing — rickets belongs to children, osteomalacia to adults; swapping them is a classic option-set trap.
• Vitamin E — deficiency increases the fragility of red blood cells and causes muscular weakness. Vitamin K — deficiency increases blood clotting time, so wounds bleed longer; K is the clotting vitamin, made partly by gut bacteria.
• EXAMPLE — The sailor's trio for a one-mark question: scurvy = vitamin C, beriberi = vitamin B1, night blindness = vitamin A. Add rickets = D in children, pernicious anaemia = B12, slow clotting = K, cheilosis = B2 — and you own the entire matching column.
• YOUR TURN — A patient's blood takes far too long to clot after a cut, and another patient, a small child, has bowed legs from soft bones. Name each deficiency. Answers — vitamin K (increased blood clotting time); vitamin D (rickets in children).

Nucleic Acids — From Nucleotide to Double Helix
• DEFINITION — Nucleic acids are the long-chain polymers of nucleotides that store and transmit hereditary information: deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA). Every inherited trait you carry is written in their base sequences.
• Build from the bottom. DEFINITION — A nucleoside is a nitrogenous base joined to a pentose sugar through an N-glycosidic bond. A nucleotide is a nucleoside plus one or more phosphate groups. A nucleic acid is nucleotides linked in a chain by phosphodiester bonds. Nucleotide equals nucleoside plus phosphate — that single plus sign settles a hundred exam questions.
• The sugar: RNA carries beta-D-ribose; DNA carries beta-D-2-deoxyribose, identical except it lacks the oxygen on carbon 2. That one missing OH is why DNA is chemically more stable — the 2-prime OH of RNA makes RNA reactive and short-lived, which suits RNA's temporary-message job.
• The bases come in two shapes. DEFINITION — Purines are double-ring bases: adenine (A) and guanine (G), present in both DNA and RNA. Pyrimidines are single-ring bases: cytosine (C, both), thymine (T, DNA only) and uracil (U, RNA only).
• Nucleotide chains link the 3-prime carbon of one sugar through a phosphate to the 5-prime carbon of the next — the phosphodiester backbone runs along the outside, bases stick inward. A free 5-prime phosphate end and a free 3-prime OH end give every strand a direction, written 5-prime to 3-prime by convention.
• Watson and Crick's 1953 model: DNA is two such strands coiled into a right-handed double helix, running in OPPOSITE directions (antiparallel), with the sugar-phosphate backbones outside and bases paired inside like rungs — the structure drawn in this section's figure.
✔ KEY RULE — Base pairing is strict and exclusive: adenine pairs with thymine through TWO hydrogen bonds (A = T), and guanine pairs with cytosine through THREE hydrogen bonds (G triple-bond C). The pairing keeps the helix diameter uniform — one purine always faces one pyrimidine.
• Chargaff's rule is the numerical shadow of that pairing. DEFINITION — In any DNA, the amount of adenine equals the amount of thymine and guanine equals cytosine (A = T, G = C); consequently total purines equal total pyrimidines, while the (A + T)/(G + C) ratio is a constant fingerprint for each species.
• WORKED PATTERN — Chargaff arithmetic: given any one base percentage, its partner is fixed, and the other pair splits the remainder equally. A = 30 percent forces T = 30 percent, leaving 40 percent for G + C, so G = C = 20 percent. Three moves, never more.
• WHY complementarity is the genius of the design — each strand carries the full information to rebuild the other. Split the helix, and each half templates a new partner: that is replication, accurate copying from complementary templates, the molecular basis of heredity.
• YOUR TURN — A DNA sample shows 22 percent guanine. Find the other three percentages. Answer — G = C, so cytosine is 22 percent; the remaining 56 percent splits evenly, giving adenine = thymine = 28 percent.

▸ DNA versus RNA and the Central Dogma
• DEFINITION — DNA is the permanent master archive of genetic information, mostly locked in the nucleus; RNA is the working copy system — made from DNA, used to build proteins, and disposed of. One library, many photocopies.
• The comparison table in words: sugar — deoxyribose versus ribose; unique base — thymine versus uracil; strands — double versus usually single; stability — high (no reactive 2-prime OH) versus lower and reactive; location — mainly nucleus versus nucleus and cytoplasm; lifespan — lifelong versus short-lived.
• The three RNA types, each with one job: mRNA (messenger RNA) carries the gene's code from DNA to the ribosome; tRNA (transfer RNA) reads the code and physically brings the matching amino acid — the adapter molecule, with an anticodon at one end and the amino acid at the other; rRNA (ribosomal RNA) forms the structural core of ribosomes and even catalyses peptide-bond formation.
★ HIGHLIGHT — One letter swaps between the alphabets: wherever RNA reads U, DNA would read T; the pairing logic is otherwise identical (A pairs with U in RNA contexts). And one job-split solves most RNA questions: mRNA = message, tRNA = truck, rRNA = factory floor.
• DEFINITION — The central dogma states the one-way flow of genetic information: DNA replicates itself, DNA is transcribed into RNA, and RNA is translated into protein. DNA to RNA to protein — information flows outward from the archive to the product.
• EXAMPLE — Insulin production in your pancreas walks the whole dogma: the insulin gene in DNA is transcribed to mRNA, the mRNA travels to ribosomes, tRNAs deliver amino acids in the coded order, and the resulting chain folds into insulin — gene to hormone in three steps.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — RNA does contain adenine, guanine and cytosine — the ONLY base difference between DNA and RNA sets is thymine (DNA) versus uracil (RNA). Options claiming RNA lacks cytosine, or DNA contains uracil, are instant rejects.
• YOUR TURN — A short DNA strand segment reads 5-prime A T G C C T 3-prime. Write its complementary strand and its RNA transcript bases (ignoring direction conventions beyond pairing). Answers — complementary DNA: T A C G G A (with A = T, G triple-bond C pairing); transcript: A U G C C U — same as the coding idea, with U in place of T.

Rules Sheet — Every Classification on One Page
✔ KEY RULE — Carbohydrate classes by hydrolysis: monosaccharides (no hydrolysis) — glucose, fructose, ribose; disaccharides (2 units) — sucrose, maltose, lactose; polysaccharides (very many units) — starch, cellulose, glycogen. Aldose = aldehyde sugar (glucose); ketose = ketone sugar (fructose)
✔ KEY RULE — Reducing verdicts: ALL monosaccharides reduce; maltose and lactose reduce (free anomeric carbon); sucrose does NOT (both anomeric carbons locked, C1 glucose to C2 fructose); starch and cellulose are effectively non-reducing
✔ KEY RULE — Linkage map: sucrose alpha,beta-1,2; maltose alpha-1,4; lactose beta-1,4 (galactose to glucose); amylose alpha-1,4; amylopectin alpha-1,4 plus alpha-1,6 branches; glycogen alpha-1,4 plus frequent alpha-1,6; cellulose beta-1,4
✔ KEY RULE — Glucose proofs: HI gives n-hexane (straight chain); hydroxylamine oxime and HCN cyanohydrin (carbonyl); acetic anhydride pentaacetate (five OH); bromine water gives gluconic acid (aldehyde only); nitric acid gives saccharic acid (both ends); NO 2,4-DNP and NO bisulphite reaction (cyclic form hides the aldehyde)
✔ KEY RULE — Mutarotation data: alpha-D-glucose +112 degrees, beta-D-glucose +19 degrees, equilibrium mixture +52.7 degrees (about 36 percent alpha, 64 percent beta); sucrose +66.5 degrees turns laevorotatory on hydrolysis (inversion)
✔ KEY RULE — Amino acid grids: essential ten = Val, Leu, Ile, Lys, Thr, Met, Phe, Trp, His, Arg; acidic = Glu, Asp; basic = Lys, Arg, His; sulphur = Cys, Met; aromatic = Phe, Tyr, Trp; glycine alone is optically inactive
✔ KEY RULE — Protein ladder: primary = sequence, peptide bonds; secondary = helix and sheet, hydrogen bonds; tertiary = overall fold, H-bonds plus disulphide, ionic, hydrophobic; quaternary = subunit arrangement; denaturation destroys secondary and tertiary, NEVER primary
✔ KEY RULE — Protein types: fibrous and insoluble (keratin, myosin, collagen) versus globular and soluble (insulin, albumin, haemoglobin, enzymes); haemoglobin has 4 subunits, 2 alpha plus 2 beta chains
✔ KEY RULE — Vitamin storage law: fat-soluble A, D, E, K are stored in liver and fat; water-soluble B-complex and C are excreted and need regular supply
✔ KEY RULE — Vitamin disease map: A night blindness and xerophthalmia; B1 beriberi; B2 cheilosis; B6 convulsions; B12 pernicious anaemia (contains cobalt); C scurvy; D rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults; E fragile red blood cells and muscular weakness; K increased clotting time
✔ KEY RULE — Nucleic acid alphabet: purines A and G (double ring), pyrimidines C, T and U (single ring); DNA sugar deoxyribose with T; RNA sugar ribose with U; nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate; backbone = phosphodiester bonds, 3-prime to 5-prime
✔ KEY RULE — Helix law: strands antiparallel and complementary; A = T (two hydrogen bonds), G triple-bond C (three hydrogen bonds); Chargaff: A equals T, G equals C, purines equal pyrimidines; RNA jobs: mRNA message, tRNA transfer, rRNA ribosome

Common Mistakes and Exam Traps
• Mistake 1 — Calling sucrose a reducing sugar because glucose and fructose are. Why it fails — reducing power needs a FREE anomeric carbon; sucrose locks both in its C1-to-C2 glycosidic bond. Correct principle — judge the finished molecule's anomeric carbons, not its ingredients.
• Mistake 2 — Declaring fructose non-reducing since it is a ketose with no aldehyde. Why it fails — in the alkaline test medium fructose rearranges into glucose and mannose through an enediol. Correct principle — all monosaccharides, aldoses and ketoses, are reducing sugars.
• Mistake 3 — Confusing a nucleoside with a nucleotide. Why it fails — they differ by phosphate; mixing them upscrambles every structure question. Correct principle — nucleoside = base + sugar; nucleotide = base + sugar + phosphate.
• Mistake 4 — Saying denaturation breaks peptide bonds or destroys primary structure. Why it fails — boiling unfolds folds; it does not hydrolyse amide bonds. Correct principle — denaturation destroys secondary and tertiary structure only; primary structure and activity part company — sequence stays, activity goes.
• Mistake 5 — Putting thymine in RNA or uracil in DNA, or claiming RNA has no cytosine. Why it fails — the base sets differ by exactly one member. Correct principle — DNA: A, G, C, T; RNA: A, G, C, U. Deoxyribose pairs with T; ribose pairs with U.
• Mistake 6 — Chargaff errors: writing A = G, or total bases A + T = G + C always. Why it fails — pairing fixes only partners: A = T and G = C, so purines (A + G) equal pyrimidines (T + C), but the (A + T) share varies freely between species. Correct principle — partners equal, pair totals free.
• Mistake 7 — Swapping the polysaccharide linkages — calling cellulose an alpha-1,4 polymer or starch beta-linked. Why it fails — the alpha/beta label is the whole story of digestibility. Correct principle — starch and glycogen are ALPHA (coiled, digestible); cellulose is BETA-1,4 (straight, indigestible to humans).
• Mistake 8 — Reading "essential amino acid" as "very important amino acid". Why it fails — the term is about synthesis, not importance; non-essential ones are just as vital in the chain. Correct principle — essential = body cannot make it, diet must supply it.
• Mistake 9 — Believing water-soluble vitamins are stored and fat-soluble ones are excreted daily. Why it fails — storage follows solubility in fat depots. Correct principle — A, D, E, K stored in liver and adipose; B and C pass out in urine and need regular intake.
• Mistake 10 — Swapping the hydrogen bonds of alpha-helix and beta-sheet, or calling peptide bonds the glue of tertiary structure. Why it fails — each level has its own bond signature. Correct principle — helix: intramolecular H-bonds; sheet: intermolecular H-bonds; tertiary: side-chain forces (H-bonds, disulphide, ionic, hydrophobic); primary alone belongs to peptide bonds.

Worked Examples — Basic to Challenging
• EXAMPLE 1 — READING GLUCOSE'S EVIDENCE FILE
• Problem — Glucose on oxidation with bromine water gives a monocarboxylic acid, but with nitric acid gives a dicarboxylic acid of the same carbon count. What does each result prove about the glucose molecule?
• Given — Bromine water is a mild oxidant; nitric acid is a strong oxidant; both products retain six carbons
• Principle — A mild oxidant converts only an aldehyde into a carboxylic acid; a strong oxidant can additionally convert a terminal primary alcohol into a carboxylic acid
• Steps — (1) Bromine water oxidised exactly one end, so one end is an aldehyde group, CHO; (2) nitric acid oxidised both ends, so the opposite end must be a primary alcohol, CH2OH, oxidised in the second product; (3) combined with the pentaacetate (five OH) and n-hexane (straight chain) results, the chain is OHC-(CHOH)4-CH2OH
• Answer — Glucose carries an aldehyde at C1 and a primary alcohol at C6; bromine water proves the aldehyde, nitric acid additionally proves the terminal CH2OH
• Reasoning — The two acids differ by exactly which ends were oxidised — gluconic acid is the aldehyde's signature, saccharic acid is both ends' signature
• Mistake to avoid — Claiming nitric acid proves a second aldehyde; HNO3 oxidises primary alcohols too, and the pentaacetate count already capped the aldehydes at one carbonyl
• EXAMPLE 2 — THE MUTAROTATION BALANCE
• Problem — Freshly dissolved alpha-D-glucose shows a specific rotation of +112 degrees and the beta anomer +19 degrees. At equilibrium the mixture reads +52.7 degrees. Find the percentage of each anomer at equilibrium
• Given — Equilibrium rotation is the weighted average of the two anomers' rotations
• Principle — If x is the alpha fraction, the mixture rotation = 112x + 19(1 minus x)
• Steps — 112x + 19 minus 19x = 52.7; 93x = 33.7; x = 0.362; beta fraction = 1 minus 0.362 = 0.638
• Answer — About 36 percent alpha and 64 percent beta at equilibrium
• Reasoning — The beta anomer dominates because its bulky groups all sit equatorial in the ring, the more comfortable arrangement — equilibrium rewards stability, not the starting material
• Mistake to avoid — Averaging 112 and 19 to claim a 50-50 mixture; the equilibrium value must be weighted by the actual fractions
• EXAMPLE 3 — THE REDUCING VERDICT
• Problem — Four sugars are tested with Benedict's reagent: glucose, fructose, maltose and sucrose. Predict the result for each, with the structural reason
• Given — Benedict's reagent is reduced (blue to brick-red precipitate) by any sugar with a free anomeric carbon or an aldehyde that can form
• Principle — Monosaccharides always reduce; a disaccharide reduces only if one anomeric carbon is not part of the glycosidic bond
• Steps — (1) Glucose — free anomeric carbon plus aldehyde: positive; (2) fructose — ketose, but rearranges in the alkaline reagent to glucose and mannose: positive; (3) maltose — C1-to-C4 linkage leaves the second glucose's anomeric carbon free: positive; (4) sucrose — C1-to-C2 linkage locks both anomeric carbons: negative
• Answer — Glucose, fructose and maltose reduce Benedict's reagent; sucrose alone does not
• Reasoning — The verdict never depends on sweetness, solubility or composition — only on whether an anomeric carbon survived the bonding
• Mistake to avoid — Marking fructose negative for lacking an aldehyde; the alpha-hydroxy ketone rearrangement under alkali is precisely why ketoses count as reducing
• EXAMPLE 4 — GLYCINE UNDER ACID AND BASE
• Problem — Describe the dominant form of glycine in (a) strongly acidic solution, (b) strongly basic solution and (c) at its isoelectric point, and state its direction of migration in an electric field in each case
• Given — Glycine can exist as cation, zwitterion or anion depending on protonation of its NH2/NH3(+) and COOH/COO(-) groups
• Principle — Acid protonates COO(-) back to COOH; base strips NH3(+) down to NH2; the isoelectric point balances both charges inside one molecule
• Steps — (a) Acid: NH3(+)-CH2-COOH, a cation — migrates to the cathode; (b) base: NH2-CH2-COO(-), an anion — migrates to the anode; (c) isoelectric: NH3(+)-CH2-COO(-), the zwitterion, net charge zero — no migration
• Answer — Cation in acid (to cathode), anion in base (to anode), stationary zwitterion at the isoelectric point
• Reasoning — The amino acid is amphoteric precisely because it owns one proton-giving and one proton-taking group; the medium decides which one wins
• Mistake to avoid — Drawing glycine in water as the uncharged NH2-CH2-COOH form; in aqueous solution the zwitterion dominates, which is also why amino acids are high-melting, water-soluble solids
• EXAMPLE 5 — COUNTING PEPTIDE BONDS
• Problem — A small protein consists of a single chain of 146 amino acid residues (the size of a haemoglobin beta chain). How many peptide bonds does it contain, and how many water molecules were released when the chain was assembled from free amino acids? If the protein is boiled until fully denatured, how many peptide bonds break?
• Given — A linear chain of n residues contains n minus 1 peptide bonds; each bond's formation releases one water
• Principle — Bond counting is positional: the bonds sit BETWEEN residues, so they always number one less than the residues
• Steps — (1) Peptide bonds = 146 minus 1 = 145; (2) waters released = 145; (3) on boiling, the secondary and tertiary folds unravel but every peptide bond survives — bonds broken = 0
• Answer — 145 peptide bonds, 145 water molecules released during assembly, and zero peptide bonds broken by denaturation
• Reasoning — The third part is the chapter's favourite booby trap hiding inside a counting exercise: denaturation is unfolding, not hydrolysis
• Mistake to avoid — Answering 146 bonds (forgetting the minus one) or claiming heat breaks peptide bonds (confusing denaturation with digestion)
• EXAMPLE 6 — CHARGAFF IN ACTION
• Problem — In a DNA sample, adenine makes up 30 percent of the bases. Find the percentages of thymine, guanine and cytosine, and the number of hydrogen bonds holding together a stretch of 10 base pairs that contains 4 A-T pairs
• Given — Chargaff's rule: A = T and G = C; A-T pairs have 2 hydrogen bonds, G-C pairs have 3
• Principle — Partner percentages are equal; the leftover percentage splits equally between G and C
• Steps — (1) T = A = 30 percent; (2) remaining = 100 minus 60 = 40 percent for G + C, so G = C = 20 percent; (3) the 10 pairs hold 4 A-T pairs (4 x 2 = 8 bonds) and 6 G-C pairs (6 x 3 = 18 bonds), total 26 hydrogen bonds
• Answer — T 30 percent, G 20 percent, C 20 percent; the stretch is held by 26 hydrogen bonds
• Reasoning — GC-rich stretches are harder to melt apart — three bonds per pair — which is why high-GC DNA denatures at higher temperatures
• Mistake to avoid — Setting G = 40 percent by dumping the whole remainder on one base; Chargaff forces G and C to share equally
• EXAMPLE 7 — THE VITAMIN CLINIC
• Problem — Four patients: (1) a sailor with bleeding gums and unhealing wounds; (2) a child with soft, bowed legs; (3) an adult whose blood clots abnormally slowly; (4) an anaemic patient whose red cells are short of haemoglobin despite adequate iron. Name the deficient vitamin in each case
• Given — Each deficiency disease in this chapter maps to exactly one vitamin
• Principle — Match the signature symptom cluster to the deficiency table, watching the age and metal clues
• Steps — (1) Bleeding gums plus poor healing = scurvy = vitamin C; (2) soft bones in a CHILD = rickets = vitamin D; (3) increased clotting time = vitamin K; (4) pernicious anaemia unresponsive to iron = vitamin B12, the cobalt-containing vitamin
• Answer — C, D, K and B12 respectively
• Reasoning — Case 2's age is the planted discriminator: the same vitamin D deficiency in an adult is called osteomalacia, not rickets; case 4's failed iron therapy points away from iron and towards B12
• Mistake to avoid — Prescribing iron for case 4 (the stem says iron is already adequate) or calling case 2 osteomalacia (that term belongs to adults)

Practice Questions — Self-Test Set
• Q1 — Which of the following is a non-reducing sugar? (A) maltose (B) lactose (C) sucrose (D) glucose
• Q2 — The glycosidic linkage in sucrose joins: (A) C1 of glucose to C4 of glucose (B) C1 of glucose to C2 of fructose (C) C1 of galactose to C4 of glucose (D) C1 of glucose to C6 of fructose
• Q3 — The optically inactive alpha-amino acid is: (A) alanine (B) valine (C) glycine (D) leucine
• Q4 — When an egg is boiled hard, the primary structure of its albumin is: (A) completely destroyed (B) retained intact (C) doubled in length (D) converted to secondary structure
• Q5 — In a DNA sample, cytosine accounts for 18 percent of the bases. The percentage of adenine is: (A) 18 percent (B) 36 percent (C) 32 percent (D) 64 percent
• Q6 — Vitamin B12 is unique among vitamins because it contains: (A) iron (B) zinc (C) cobalt (D) copper
• Q7 — The beta-pleated sheet structure of proteins is stabilised mainly by: (A) peptide bonds (B) intermolecular hydrogen bonds (C) disulphide bonds (D) phosphodiester bonds
• Q8 — The function of tRNA is to: (A) carry the genetic code from nucleus to cytoplasm (B) form the structural core of ribosomes (C) bring amino acids to the ribosome (D) store hereditary information

▸ Answer Key with Explanations
• Q1 — (C) sucrose. Its glycosidic bond consumes the anomeric carbon of BOTH units, so no ring can open and no reducing group can appear. Misconception: choosing glucose (D) by thinking "simple means reducing only sometimes" — in fact every monosaccharide, glucose included, is reducing.
• Q2 — (B) C1 of glucose to C2 of fructose. The anomeric carbon of the ketose fructose is C2, and that head-to-head link is exactly why sucrose is non-reducing. Misconception: option (A) describes maltose's linkage and option (C) describes lactose's — each disaccharide owns a different address.
• Q3 — (C) glycine. Its alpha carbon carries two hydrogens (R = H), so it is not a stereocentre and cannot rotate plane-polarised light. Misconception: picking alanine (A) because it is "small" — alanine's alpha carbon still bears four different groups and is optically active.
• Q4 — (B) retained intact. Boiling denatures albumin — secondary and tertiary structures unfold — but no peptide bond breaks, so the sequence survives. Misconception: option (A) confuses denaturation with hydrolysis; heat alone does not cut amide bonds.
• Q5 — (C) 32 percent. C = G = 18 percent, so A + T = 100 minus 36 = 64 percent, and A = T halves it: 32 percent. Misconception: option (B) stops at 36 percent — that is G + C restated, not A; the equal split step is the one examiners monetise.
• Q6 — (C) cobalt. Cyanocobalamin is built around a cobalt ion; no other vitamin carries a metal centre. Misconception: option (A) iron belongs to haemoglobin and haem enzymes, not to any vitamin — the classic cross-wire in this question.
• Q7 — (B) intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Neighbouring stretched segments zip together through H-bonds between chains. Misconception: option (A) — peptide bonds hold the primary sequence, not the sheet; option (C) disulphide bonds belong to tertiary structure.
• Q8 — (C) bring amino acids to the ribosome. tRNA is the adapter: anticodon reads the mRNA code, the other end ferries the amino acid. Misconception: option (A) is mRNA's job description, and option (B) is rRNA's — the question tests the three-way job split.
