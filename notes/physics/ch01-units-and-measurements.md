Units, Measurement and the SI System
• Welcome to the chapter every topper respects and every casual student skips — and pays for. Units, errors and dimensions look like bookkeeping, but they are the grammar of all physics: every chapter after this one speaks this language, and the examiner tests it directly.
• DEFINITION — Physical quantity: anything that can be measured and expressed as a number multiplied by a unit, Q = n × u. Length, mass, time, force, charge are physical quantities; "beauty" is not, because nobody can measure it.
• DEFINITION — Unit: a fixed standard of the same kind as the quantity, chosen by agreement, against which the quantity is compared. A measurement is complete only as number × unit — "5" means nothing, "5 metres" means everything.
• Try changing the clothes: the quantity itself never changes when the unit changes, because n₁u₁ = n₂u₂. Shrink the unit and the number inflates in the same ratio — 1 km and 1000 m are the same walk; 1 inch and 2.54 cm are the same thumb-width.
• DEFINITION — Fundamental (base) quantities: the small set of quantities fixed by international agreement as independent of one another. Derived quantities: everything else, built by multiplying or dividing base quantities — speed is length ÷ time, force is mass × length ÷ time².
• A quick history, because examiners ask: the world tried CGS (cm, g, s), FPS (foot, pound, second) and MKS (m, kg, s) before agreeing on the SI (adopted 1960, scheme fixed 1971). Then came the plot twist: in November 2018 the 26th CGPM voted to re-found the ENTIRE system on constants of nature — effective 20 May 2019, no SI unit depends on a physical object anymore.
★ HIGHLIGHT — What makes a good standard? It must be well defined, reproducible in any laboratory, stable forever, and of convenient size. The 2019 SI is the first system in history to satisfy all four perfectly — that is the story of the next section.

▸ The Seven SI Base Units — the 2019 Definitions
• Here is the revolution in one breath: humanity stopped defining units by objects (a metal bar, a metal cylinder) and started defining them by the universe itself. Each base unit is now anchored to a constant of nature whose value is fixed exactly, forever, everywhere.
• Metre (m) — length: built on the speed of light, c = 299,792,458 m/s exactly. One metre is the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second.
• Kilogram (kg) — mass: built on the Planck constant, h = 6.62607015 × 10⁻³⁴ J s exactly (with the metre and second). The famous platinum-iridium cylinder in Paris — "Le Grand K" — is officially retired; the kilogram is now a recipe, not an object.
• Second (s) — time: the duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the caesium-133 atom's transition; the caesium frequency Δν_Cs = 9,192,631,770 Hz exactly. This is why atomic clocks drift by about a second in millions of years.
• Ampere (A) — current: built on the elementary charge e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C exactly. Kelvin (K) — temperature: built on the Boltzmann constant k_B = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K exactly.
• Mole (mol) — amount of substance: exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities (the Avogadro constant N_A). Candela (cd) — luminous intensity: built on the luminous efficacy K_cd = 683 lm/W at frequency 540 × 10¹² Hz.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Memorise the seven anchors as one family: c, h, e, k_B, N_A, Δν_Cs, K_cd — seven constants carrying seven units. Any civilisation in the universe could rebuild our entire measurement system from these numbers alone. (Fig. 1.5 collects them.)
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Post-2019 plot twist that traps the unwary: μ₀ is NO LONGER exactly 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m. It is now a measured quantity (CODATA 2022: 1.25663706127 × 10⁻⁶ N/A²), and so is ε₀ = 8.8541878188 × 10⁻¹² F/m — although ε₀μ₀ = 1/c² stays exact. Any book still printing μ₀ as an exact defined value is pre-2019.

▸ Derived Units, Supplementary Units, Prefixes and Writing Rules
• Derived units are just base units in combination — learn to READ them and half of dimensional analysis becomes free. Force — newton (N) = kg m s⁻² • Work/energy — joule (J) = kg m² s⁻² • Power — watt (W) = kg m² s⁻³ • Pressure — pascal (Pa) = kg m⁻¹ s⁻² • Frequency — hertz (Hz) = s⁻¹ • Charge — coulomb (C) = A s • Potential — volt (V) = kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹ • Resistance — ohm (Ω) = kg m² s⁻³ A⁻² • Capacitance — farad (F) = kg⁻¹ m⁻² s⁴ A².
• DEFINITION — Supplementary units: the radian (rad) for plane angle — the angle whose arc equals the radius — and the steradian (sr) for solid angle, its 3D counterpart. Both are ratios of lengths, so both are dimensionless — but they still carry unit names.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Dimensionless does NOT mean unitless: rad and sr are dimensionless yet genuine units. In the other corner, strain and refractive index are dimensionless AND unitless. Four combinations, two traps — sort them once here.
• Prefixes are powers of ten wearing name tags: pico (p) 10⁻¹² • nano (n) 10⁻⁹ • micro (μ) 10⁻⁶ • milli (m) 10⁻³ • centi (c) 10⁻² • deci (d) 10⁻¹ • kilo (k) 10³ • mega (M) 10⁶ • giga (G) 10⁹ • tera (T) 10¹². Your phone already speaks this: gigabytes, nanometre chips, milliwatts.
• The length ladder of real science: 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m (nucleus) • 1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m (atom) • 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m • 1 μm = 10⁻⁶ m • 1 light year = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m • 1 AU = 1.496 × 10¹¹ m • 1 parsec = 3.08 × 10¹⁶ m — the longest unit in this list. Plus 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
• Writing rules that examiners genuinely test: no full stop after a symbol (5 kg, not 5 kg.); no plurals (10 N, not 10 Ns); a space between number and unit; units named after scientists take capital SYMBOLS (N, J, W, Pa) but small letters when spelt out (newton, joule, watt).
• YOUR TURN — Which of these is written correctly: (a) 5 kgs (b) 5 kg. (c) 5kg (d) five kilograms in a physics answer? (Answer: only (b). Silly marks live in exactly these corners.)

Significant Figures — What They Mean
• You measure a pencil with a school ruler and write 14.0 cm. A friend writes 14.000 cm from the same ruler. Who is lying? Your friend — the ruler cannot see thousandths of a centimetre. Significant figures are physics' honesty system, and this section teaches you to speak it.
• DEFINITION — Significant figures: all the digits in a measurement that are known reliably, PLUS the first uncertain (estimated) digit. A length of 23.023 cm claims 5 significant figures; the final 3 is the doubtful one, and that doubt is information, not weakness.
• So the count of significant figures is a precision report: more figures = a finer instrument = a stronger claim. Writing 23.0230 cm from a millimetre scale invents knowledge — in a lab that is misconduct, in an exam it is a lost mark.
• Unchanging truth: switching units never changes the count. 2.308 cm = 0.02308 m = 23.08 mm — 4 significant figures in every outfit (NCERT's own example).
• DEFINITION — Order of magnitude: the power of ten nearest to a quantity, by the NCERT rule — write N = a × 10ᵇ; if a ≤ 5 the order is b, if a > 5 it is b + 1. Earth's diameter 1.28 × 10⁷ m → order 7; a hydrogen atom 1.06 × 10⁻¹⁰ m → order −10.
• EXAMPLE — Why bother with orders of magnitude? Because they are a 5-second lie detector: if your calculated answer for a car's speed comes out as 10⁸ m/s, the order of magnitude alone tells you it is nonsense before you hunt the algebra error.

▸ Counting Significant Figures — the 6 Rules
• Six rules, learnt once, used in every numerical you will ever write. Run each rule on its example as you read.
• Rule 1: All non-zero digits are significant. 237 → 3; 2371 → 4.
• Rule 2: Zeros trapped BETWEEN non-zero digits are significant — they were measured, not placed. 2007 → 4; 4.002 → 4.
• Rule 3: LEADING zeros are never significant — they only park the decimal point. 0.0042 → 2; 0.000105 → 3.
• Rule 4: TRAILING zeros in a number WITH a decimal point are significant — someone measured them. 3.500 → 4; 0.00120 → 4; 12.000 → 5.
• Rule 5: In a whole number WITHOUT a decimal point, trailing zeros are ambiguous. Honesty demands scientific notation: 2500 could be 2.5 × 10³ (2 figures) or 2.500 × 10³ (4 figures) — the notation must declare which.
• Rule 6: Powers of ten never count, and EXACT numbers — counted objects, defined constants like c — carry unlimited significant figures and never limit a calculation.
• YOUR TURN — Count: (a) 0.00508 (b) 60.0 (c) 4005. (Answers: (a) 3 — the leading zeros are scenery; (b) 3 — the trailing zero after the point was measured; (c) 4 — the zeros are trapped.)

▸ Rounding Off and Arithmetic with Significant Figures
• DEFINITION — Rounding off: reducing a number to a chosen number of significant figures by dropping the rest, following fixed rules so the reported value stays as honest as the original.
• The basic pair: digit to drop is LESS than 5 → last kept digit stays (3.24 → 3.2). MORE than 5 → last kept digit grows by one (3.27 → 3.3).
• Exactly 5? Here NCERT parts company with school habit: use the EVEN-ODD rule — if the last kept digit is even, leave it (3.25 → 3.2); if odd, raise it (3.35 → 3.4). NCERT's own pair: 2.745 → 2.74 and 2.735 → 2.74. The point is fairness: always rounding 5 up slowly biases long calculations upward.
• Subtraction can be brutal: 12.9 g − 7.06 g = 5.8 g (not 5.84) and 0.307 m − 0.304 m = 0.003 m — ONE significant figure survives. Subtracting nearly-equal numbers destroys precision; instrument designers and examiners both know it.
• Two different games, two different rules — say them aloud: ADDITION/SUBTRACTION obey DECIMAL PLACES (436.32 + 227.2 + 0.301 = 663.821, reported 663.8 because 227.2 knows only one decimal place). MULTIPLICATION/DIVISION obey SIGNIFICANT FIGURES (2.456 × 1.2 = 2.9472, reported 2.9 because 1.2 carries only 2 figures).
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Never round in the middle of a calculation. Carry extra digits all the way and round ONCE, at the very end — mid-way rounding is a silent mark-leak in multi-step numericals.

Measurement and Least Count
• Every instrument has a smallest whisper it can hear. Below that size, it is deaf — and pretending otherwise is how fake precision is born.
• DEFINITION — Least count (LC): the smallest change in a quantity that an instrument can detect and display. It fixes the precision of every reading the instrument will ever give: a metre scale (LC 1 mm) can never honestly report 12.34 cm.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Finer instrument → smaller LC → more significant figures per reading. The ladder of this chapter: metre scale ~10⁻³ m, Vernier calipers ~10⁻⁴ m, screw gauge ~10⁻⁵ m.
• Why can't we just keep adding divisions forever? NCERT's precision ranking (Exercise 1.6) answers: optical instruments resolve about a wavelength of light and beat everything mechanical; then the screw gauge (~0.001 cm); then Vernier (~0.01 cm); then the metre scale (0.1 cm). For the screw gauge, mechanical play — backlash — sets the floor, not the engraving.
• EXAMPLE — Choosing the instrument IS the first step of any experiment: a hair's thickness (~10⁻⁴ m) laughs at a metre scale, is routine for a screw gauge, and is coarse for a microscope. Match the LC to the job before you measure anything.

▸ Vernier Calipers — Least Count, Reading and Zero Error [Fig. 1.1, Fig. 1.4]
• Meet the first precision instrument of your life. A Vernier calipers squeezes a second, sliding scale next to the main scale, and the tiny mismatch between their divisions lets you read a tenth of a millimetre with your eyes alone. Lower jaws grip outsides, upper jaws measure insides, and the tail rod probes depths.
• DEFINITION — Vernier least count: the difference between one main scale division and one vernier scale division, LC = 1 MSD − 1 VSD.
✔ KEY FORMULA — If 10 vernier divisions coincide with 9 main scale divisions and 1 MSD = 1 mm, then 1 VSD = 0.9 mm and LC = 1 mm − 0.9 mm = 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Reading = Main scale reading (last full division before the vernier zero) + (Coinciding vernier division × LC) − Zero error.
• WORKED PATTERN — Main scale reads 12 mm, the 7th vernier division coincides, LC = 0.1 mm: reading = 12 + 7 × 0.1 = 12.7 mm = 1.27 cm. Three moves: read, find the match, add.
• DEFINITION — Zero error: the reading an instrument shows when the true value is zero (jaws fully closed). It must be subtracted — WITH ITS SIGN — from every reading: corrected = observed − zero error.
• Reading the sign like a professional: jaws closed, vernier zero to the RIGHT of main zero → the instrument over-reads → positive zero error = +(n × LC). Vernier zero to the LEFT → negative zero error ZE = −[(N − n) × LC], where N is the total vernier divisions and n the coinciding one; its size is then effectively added back. One master rule covers both: corrected = observed − ZE (signed).
• Variant alert: if (N + 1) vernier divisions coincide with N main divisions, LC = 1 MSD ÷ (N + 1). Read the coincidence sentence in the question word by word before choosing your formula.
• YOUR TURN — A Vernier has LC = 0.01 cm. Main scale reading 2.3 cm, 6th division coincides, zero error +0.02 cm. The corrected length? (Answer: observed = 2.3 + 6 × 0.01 = 2.36 cm; corrected = 2.36 − 0.02 = 2.34 cm.)

▸ Screw Gauge — Least Count, Reading and Zero Error [Fig. 1.2, Fig. 1.4]
• If the Vernier reads tenths of millimetres, the screw gauge reads hundredths. A precision screw converts rotation into tiny forward steps: turn the cap once and the spindle advances exactly one pitch — subdivide that turn into 50 or 100 parts and you are measuring wire diameters and foil thicknesses to 10⁻⁵ m.
• DEFINITION — Pitch: the distance the spindle advances in one complete rotation of the cap. Least count of a screw gauge: LC = Pitch ÷ number of circular scale divisions.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Pitch 0.5 mm over 50 divisions → LC = 0.01 mm. Pitch 1 mm over 100 divisions → LC = 0.01 mm. Reading = Main scale reading + (Coinciding circular division × LC) − Zero error.
• WORKED PATTERN — Main scale 3 mm, 32nd circular division coincides, LC = 0.01 mm: reading = 3 + 32 × 0.01 = 3.32 mm.
• Zero error, screw-gauge edition: at contact, circular zero BELOW the reference line → positive ZE = +(n × LC). Circular zero ABOVE the line → negative ZE = −[(N − n) × LC]. Concrete: N = 100, 95th division coincides at contact → ZE = −5 × 0.01 = −0.05 mm, so you ADD 0.05 mm to every reading.
• EXAM-GRADE PATTERN (NEET style): LC = 0.001 cm, main scale 5 mm, 25th division coincides, ZE = −0.004 cm. Observed = 0.500 + 0.025 = 0.525 cm; corrected = 0.525 − (−0.004) = 0.529 cm. Note the double negative doing real work — this exact pattern is why zero-error signs are drilled.
• DEFINITION — Backlash error: the lag (play) in a worn screw thread that makes the spindle hesitate when the rotation direction reverses. Defence: always approach the final reading turning in ONE direction only, and let the ratchet — never your grip — make the final contact.
• The same screw principle powers the spherometer, which measures the sagitta h of a curved surface: R = l²/(6h) + h/2, with the same LC = pitch ÷ divisions.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The ratchet is not decoration. Squeezing the cap by hand crushes the wire thinner and falsifies every diameter — technique errors are the ones no formula can repair.

▸ Measuring the Very Large and the Very Small — Parallax and Ranges (NCERT Supplement)
• A promise kept: this section restores material the 2023 NCERT rationalisation deleted from the textbook — but it survives in NCERT's own exercises (1.6, 1.8) and in JEE/NEET papers every year. Treat it as fully in the game.
• DEFINITION — Parallax: the apparent shift in position of a nearby object against a distant background when the observer's viewpoint changes. The separation between the two viewpoints is the basis b; the angular shift is the parallax angle θ.
• You use parallax daily: hold a finger up and blink alternate eyes — your finger "jumps" against the wall. Astronomers scale that blink up to planets: two observatories, one star, one angle, and a distance falls out.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Distance by parallax: D = b/θ (θ in radians, valid when b ≪ D) • Angular size: an object of size d at distance D subtending angle α obeys d = αD.
• WORKED PATTERN (NCERT): the Moon, viewed from the two ends of Earth's diameter (basis b = 1.276 × 10⁷ m) with parallax 1°54′, sits at D = 3.84 × 10⁸ m. The Sun's angular diameter 1920″ fed into d = αD gives its diameter ≈ 1.39 × 10⁹ m — the size of a star from an angle and a distance.
• The size ladder of the universe, for your intuition: instruments resolve from metre scales (10⁻³ m) down through Vernier, screw gauge and optical microscopes to electron microscopes below 1 Å — while nature runs from ~10⁻¹⁴ m (a nucleus) to ~10²⁶ m (the observable universe). Physics measures across 40 orders of magnitude; this chapter is the toolbox.
• Estimating the invisible (NCERT classic): spread an oleic-acid monolayer on water and its thickness — one molecule — comes out near 10⁻⁹ m. A tray, a ruler, and cleverness: a molecule, measured.
• Mass and time anchors: 1 unified atomic mass unit u = (1/12) of a carbon-12 atom = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg; atomic masses come from the mass spectrograph, planetary masses from gravity itself. For time, the caesium atomic clock — the very transition that defines the second — holds about 1 part in 10¹³.

Errors in Measurement — Types and Measures
• Take ten readings of the same length and you will rarely get ten identical numbers. That disagreement is not failure — it is data about your measurement, and this section teaches you to report it honestly: result = mean ± uncertainty, a = a_mean ± Δa.
• DEFINITION — Error: the difference between a measured value and the true value of a quantity. Absolute error of a single reading = |measured − true|; the reported Δa is the mean of the absolute errors. Relative (fractional) error = Δa/a_mean; percentage error = (Δa/a_mean) × 100.
• DEFINITION — Systematic errors: errors that push every reading the same way by roughly the same amount — a zero error, a shrunken scale, heat leaking the same way in every trial. Repetition does NOT cure them; better instruments and better technique do.
• DEFINITION — Random errors: errors that scatter readings on both sides of the true value with no pattern — tiny fluctuations in conditions and judgement. Repetition DOES cure these: average many readings and the scatter shrinks.
• And the third family: gross errors — pure carelessness (misreading a scale, copying a digit wrong). No statistics can save them; the reading is simply rejected and taken again, carefully.
• Least count error sits with the instrument: no reading can be trusted finer than one least count, however steady your hand.
• EXAMPLE — A thermometer that reads 0.5° high in ice water reads 0.5° high everywhere (systematic — subtract it). A stopwatch started a split-second early or late each trial scatters around the truth (random — average it).

▸ Accuracy versus Precision
• Two words students swap carelessly and examiners never do. Learn them as a picture: a dartboard.
• DEFINITION — Accuracy: how close the mean of your readings is to the TRUE value — darts clustered around the bullseye. Limited by systematic errors. Precision: how closely your readings agree WITH EACH OTHER — darts clustered tightly anywhere. Limited by random errors and least count.
• So an instrument can be precise but wrong (tight cluster, off-target: a miscalibrated screw gauge), or accurate on average but sloppy (darts scattered around the bullseye: a coarse scale read many times). The professional wants both — and knows which enemy attacks which.
★ HIGHLIGHT — The bridge rule between errors and significant figures: every reported result ends at its first uncertain digit. Mean ± Δa and significant figures are two dialects of one language — honesty about doubt.

Propagation of Errors — the Rules
• Your measurement is rarely the final answer: you measure a length and a time and report a speed; measure sides and report a volume. How do the input errors flow into the output? One working principle runs everything at this level: in maximum-error estimates, errors always ADD — never hope they cancel.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Sum and difference (Z = A ± B): ΔZ = ΔA + ΔB. Read it again — even for a DIFFERENCE, the absolute errors add. Subtraction is where errors go to grow.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Product and quotient (Z = AB or A/B): ΔZ/Z = ΔA/A + ΔB/B — relative errors add, and the denominator's error counts just like the numerator's.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Power rule (Z = Aⁿ): ΔZ/Z = n × (ΔA/A). Powers are amplifiers: square a quantity and its percentage error doubles; cube it and it triples.
✔ KEY FORMULA — The master rule (Z = AᵃBᵇ/Cᶜ): ΔZ/Z = |a|(ΔA/A) + |b|(ΔB/B) + |c|(ΔC/C) — multiply by 100 for the percentage error. Every quantity contributes its relative error times its power.
• EXAMPLE — Why the power rule runs laboratories: measure a sphere's radius to 1% and its volume (r³) inherits 3%. Measure the side of a cube to 1% and the surface area (L²) inherits 2%. Big powers are where precision budgets are won and lost.

▸ Propagation — Worked Examples and Laboratory Strategy
• Example 0 (NCERT): temperatures (50 ± 0.5)°C and (20 ± 0.5)°C. Their difference is 30 ± 1 °C — the errors ADDED even though the values subtracted. Commit this one to instinct.
• Example 1 (density of a cube): mass error 2%, each side error 1.5%. ρ = m/L³ → percentage error in ρ = 2 + 3 × 1.5 = 6.5%. The cubed length shouts loudest.
• Example 2 (pendulum, g = 4π²L/T²): L to 0.1% and T to 0.5% gives error in g = 0.1 + 2 × 0.5 = 1.1%. NCERT's canonical numbers — L at 2%, T at 1% — give Δg/g = 2 + 2 × 1 = 4%. The laboratory moral follows instantly: to improve g, improve T — and the classic trick is to time 50 oscillations and divide, so the timing error divides by 50 too.
• Example 3 (reporting like a professional): a sheet 16.2 ± 0.1 cm by 10.1 ± 0.1 cm has area 163.62 cm² with 1.6% uncertainty. Report it as 164 ± 3 cm² — round the ERROR to one significant figure first, then match the value's last digit to the error's place.
• A research footnote, so you know the landscape: these rules give the maximum permissible error — the exam standard. Research labs instead combine independent random errors in quadrature: ΔZ/Z = √[(p ΔA/A)² + (q ΔB/B)² + (r ΔC/C)²], always kinder than the worst case. Know it exists; use the maximum rule unless told otherwise.
★ HIGHLIGHT — Laboratory strategy in one line: find the term with the largest (power × relative error) and improve THAT measurement first. Everything else is polishing the wrong link of the chain.

Dimensions of Physical Quantities
• Now for the chapter's second superpower. Strip a quantity down to its recipe — how much mass, how much length, how much time — and you can audit equations, convert units and even derive laws. That recipe is called its dimensions.
• DEFINITION — Dimensions of a physical quantity: the powers to which the base quantities must be raised to represent the quantity. Written in brackets: [M] mass, [L] length, [T] time, [A] current, [K] temperature, [mol] amount, [cd] luminous intensity. Velocity is [L T⁻¹] because it is a length divided by a time.
• DEFINITION — Dimensional formula: that bracketed recipe itself. Dimensional equation: the statement setting a quantity equal to its formula — [Force] = [M L T⁻²], read straight off F = ma as mass × (length ÷ time²).
✔ KEY FORMULA — The universal recipe-finder: write the defining equation of the quantity, replace every quantity in it by its dimensions, simplify. Every entry in the master lists ahead is produced by exactly this three-step move — you never need to memorise what you can derive in fifteen seconds.
• Dimensions wear the fingerprint of your chosen fundamental set — a pattern past papers love: take force (F), velocity (V), time (T) as fundamental and mass becomes [F V⁻¹ T]; take energy (E), velocity, time as fundamental and surface tension becomes [E V⁻² T⁻²]. Same physics, different alphabet.
• YOUR TURN — Find the dimensions of pressure from scratch: force ÷ area = [M L T⁻²] ÷ [L²] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²]. Fifteen seconds, as promised.

▸ Master List — Mechanics and Fluids
• Read this list actively: each entry should feel derivable, not memorised. Velocity [L T⁻¹] • Acceleration [L T⁻²] • Momentum [M L T⁻¹] • Impulse [M L T⁻¹] • Force [M L T⁻²] • Work, energy, torque [M L² T⁻²] • Power [M L² T⁻³].
• Pressure, stress, Young's modulus, energy density — one family at [M L⁻¹ T⁻²] • Density [M L⁻³] • Frequency [T⁻¹] • Angular velocity [T⁻¹] • Angular acceleration [T⁻²] • Angular momentum [M L² T⁻¹] • Moment of inertia [M L²].
• Surface tension = surface energy per unit area [M T⁻²] • Coefficient of viscosity η [M L⁻¹ T⁻¹] • Gravitational constant G [M⁻¹ L³ T⁻²] — derive it from F = G m₁m₂/r² the moment you doubt it: [G] = [F][L²]/[M²].

▸ Master List — Heat, Electricity, Magnetism and Modern Physics
• Heat family: specific heat capacity [L² T⁻² K⁻¹] • Latent heat and gravitational potential [L² T⁻²] • Gas constant R [M L² T⁻² K⁻¹ mol⁻¹] • Boltzmann constant k_B [M L² T⁻² K⁻¹] • Molar heat capacity [M L² T⁻² K⁻¹ mol⁻¹] • Coefficient of thermal expansion [K⁻¹] • Stefan constant σ [M T⁻³ K⁻⁴] (σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴) • Thermal conductivity [M L T⁻³ K⁻¹] • Entropy and heat capacity [M L² T⁻² K⁻¹].
• Electricity and magnetism: charge [A T] • Electric field [M L T⁻³ A⁻¹] • Potential and EMF [M L² T⁻³ A⁻¹] • Resistance [M L² T⁻³ A⁻²] • Capacitance [M⁻¹ L⁻² T⁴ A²] • Inductance [M L² T⁻² A⁻²] • Magnetic field B [M T⁻² A⁻¹] • Magnetic flux [M L² T⁻² A⁻¹] • Permittivity ε₀ [M⁻¹ L⁻³ T⁴ A²] • Permeability μ₀ [M L T⁻² A⁻²] • Planck constant h [M L² T⁻¹].
• Modern physics gems: Rydberg constant R∞ [L⁻¹] = 1.097373 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ — for an infinitely heavy nucleus; hydrogen's own R_H = 1.09678 × 10⁷ m⁻¹ is slightly different, and mixing them is a designed trap. Work function Φ [M L² T⁻²] — it is an energy, so it must be.
• A research landmark that IAT actually asked (2021): the von Klitzing constant R_K = h/e² ≈ 25,812.8 Ω. Offered e²/h, e/h, h/e and h/e², only h/e² carries the dimensions of resistance — one line, if you think dimensionally.

▸ Identical Dimensions, Trap Pairs and Dimensionless Quantities
• Some quantities are dimensional twins — same recipe, different physics. Memorise the famous pairs: work & torque • momentum & impulse • pressure, stress & Young's modulus • frequency, angular velocity & velocity gradient • Planck constant & angular momentum • surface tension & spring constant.
• The dimensionless club (recipe = nothing): plane angle, solid angle, strain, refractive index, relative density, coefficient of restitution, coefficient of friction, Poisson's ratio, efficiency — and the Reynolds number Re = ρvL/η, which you can verify cancels to 1: [M L⁻³][L T⁻¹][L] ÷ [M L⁻¹ T⁻¹] = 1.
• A time-dimension trio beloved of matching questions: L/R, RC and √(LC) all carry [T] — inductance/resistance, resistance × capacitance, and the LC square root are all secretly clocks.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Identical dimensions do NOT mean identical nature: torque is a vector, work is a scalar, and dimensions cannot tell them apart. The tool audits recipes, not meanings.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — The impostor pair of the chapter: tension [M L T⁻²] and surface tension [M T⁻²] do NOT share dimensions, however similar their names (an NCERT Exemplar favourite). Likewise strain (dimensionless) is not stress [M L⁻¹ T⁻²].

Principle of Homogeneity — Checking Equations
• Imagine a shopkeeper adding 3 kilograms + 2 seconds and announcing 5. Absurd — yet students write exactly such sums inside equations every day. The principle of homogeneity is the law that forbids it, and it doubles as the fastest equation-auditor in physics.
• DEFINITION — Principle of homogeneity: a physical equation can be correct only if every term on both sides has exactly the same dimensions. Only quantities of identical dimensions may be equated, added or subtracted.
• Corollary with teeth: the arguments of sin, cos, eˣ and ln must be dimensionless, and so are the functions themselves. In x = A sin(ωt), [ωt] = 1 forces [ω] = [T⁻¹] — no exceptions, ever.
• Check 1 (kinematics): s = ut + ½at². [ut] = [L T⁻¹][T] = [L]; [at²] = [L T⁻²][T²] = [L]. Both terms match [s] = [L] — the equation passes the audit.
• Check 2 (photon energy): E = hν. [hν] = [M L² T⁻¹][T⁻¹] = [M L² T⁻²] = [E] ✓ — and the audit pays a bonus: h emerges with the dimensions of angular momentum.
• Check 3 (van der Waals): in (P + a/V²)(V − b) = RT, the term a/V² must be a pressure, so [a] = [M L⁵ T⁻²]; and b must be a volume, [b] = [L³]. Method for multi-term equations: audit every added term separately — that IS the whole technique.
• Check 4 (spot the impossible): y = (a/T) sin(t/a) cannot be a displacement, because [t/a] = [T L⁻¹] is not dimensionless. In the healthy wave form y = A sin(ωt − kx), the same audit hands you [ω] = [T⁻¹] and [k] = [L⁻¹] for free.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — Homogeneity is necessary, NOT sufficient: s = ut + 2at² passes every dimensional test yet is false (the true constant is ½). Dimensionless constants are invisible to the audit — a passed equation is "not proven wrong", never "proven right".

Dimensional Analysis — Deriving Relations
• Now the showpiece: deriving laws from dimensions alone. The recipe: assume the target quantity depends on the plausible parameters raised to unknown powers, write the dimensional equation, equate the powers of M, L and T on both sides, and solve the three equations. Watch it work five times.
• Derivation 1 — Pendulum: T = k lᵃ gᵇ mᶜ. M-powers: c = 0 — the period cannot involve the bob's mass. T-powers: −2b = 1 → b = −½. L-powers: a + b = 0 → a = ½. Result T = k√(l/g); experiment alone fixes k = 2π.
• Derivation 2 — Stokes' viscous force: F = k ηᵃ rᵇ vᶜ. M: a = 1. T: −a − c = −2 → c = 1. L: −a + b + c = 1 → b = 1. Result F = kηrv — and experiment crowns it with k = 6π: Stokes' law, F = 6πηrv.
• Derivation 3 — Centripetal force: F = k mᵃ vᵇ rᶜ gives a = 1, b = 2, c = −1: F = k mv²/r with k = 1 from theory — the very formula Chapter 3 will meet from geometry.
• Derivation 4 — Speed of sound: v = k Bᵃ ρᵇ with [B] = [M L⁻¹ T⁻²], [ρ] = [M L⁻³] gives a = ½, b = −½: v = k√(B/ρ) — the Newton–Laplace v = √(γP/ρ) in disguise, the dimensionless γ hiding inside k.
• Derivation 5 — Planck units (the IAT speciality; IAT 2019 asked for energy built from h, c, G): the same three-equation machine, fed c, G, h, returns length √(hG/c³), time √(hG/c⁵), mass √(hc/G) and energy √(hc⁵/G). Constructing quantities from fundamental constants is IAT's fingerprint — practise it until it is reflex.
⚠ EXAM TRAP — True Planck units are built with ħ = h/2π, while school questions use h. Dimensions are identical, so dimensional answers agree — but the numerical Planck length ≈ 1.616 × 10⁻³⁵ m belongs to ħ. Never mix h and ħ inside a numerical value.

▸ Dimensional Analysis — Converting Units Between Systems
• The same dimensional formula doubles as a currency converter between unit systems — the third job of dimensional analysis, after auditing and deriving.
✔ KEY FORMULA — For a quantity with dimensional formula [Mᵃ Lᵇ Tᶜ]: n₂ = n₁ × (M₁/M₂)ᵃ × (L₁/L₂)ᵇ × (T₁/T₂)ᶜ converts its numerical value from system 1 to system 2. Bigger unit, smaller number — the formula just automates that instinct.
• Conversion 1: 1 newton into dynes. Exponents (1, 1, −2): n₂ = (kg/g) × (m/cm) = 1000 × 100 = 10⁵. So 1 N = 10⁵ dyne.
• Conversion 2: 1 joule into ergs. Exponents (1, 2, −2): n₂ = (kg/g) × (m/cm)² = 1000 × 10⁴ = 10⁷. So 1 J = 10⁷ erg.
• Conversion 3: mercury's density 13.6 g/cm³ into SI. n₂ = 13.6 × (g/kg) × (cm/m)⁻³ = 13.6 × 10⁻³ × 10⁶ = 13,600 kg/m³. Pocket version of the same fact: 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.
• YOUR TURN — Convert 72 km/h into SI using pure unit sense: 72 × (1000 m)/(3600 s) = 20 m/s. (The 5/18 shortcut from Chapter 2 is this same conversion, pre-computed.)

▸ Limitations of Dimensional Analysis
• Every superpower has its kryptonite — and examiners test the limits as happily as the method. Learn the five walls of dimensional analysis.
• Limit 1: dimensionless constants are invisible. The method yields the FORM of a law — never its 2π, ½ or 6π.
• Limit 2: it cannot handle relations built from sums of different terms, or from exponential, logarithmic and trigonometric functions. For N = N₀e^(−λt) it can only tell you [λ] = [T⁻¹]; the exponential law itself stays out of reach.
• Limit 3: it cannot separate dimensional twins — work from torque, momentum from impulse.
• Limit 4: with only three equations (M, L, T), it fails when a quantity depends on more than three independent parameters.
• Limit 5: it is blind to nature — scalar versus vector, and to which physical mechanism is really at work.

Apparatus in Focus — Thomson's e/m Experiment [Fig. 1.3]
• The finale of the chapter is a detective story. By 1897 physicists knew cathode rays carried negative charge, but the electron's charge e and mass mₑ individually were unmeasurable. Their RATIO, however, could be caught — and J. J. Thomson caught it, with the apparatus in the figure.
• Tour the apparatus: a cathode ray tube whose cathode and anode connect to a high-voltage source, a magnet arranged around the tube, and a fluorescent screen where the beam lands as a glowing spot.
• Run the experiment in three acts. Electric field alone: the spot deflects to point A, dragged towards the positive plate. Magnetic field alone: the spot bends to point C, pushed the opposite way by the Lorentz force. Both fields balanced: the spot returns to the centre, point B — the beam flies straight, undeflected.
✔ KEY FORMULA — Step 1 (accelerate through voltage V): eV = ½mₑv² • Step 2 (balance the fields — a velocity selector): eE = evB, so v = E/B • Step 3 (combine): e/mₑ = E²/(2VB²).
• The elegance: every quantity on the right is directly measurable in the lab, so the ratio comes out at e/mₑ ≈ 1.76 × 10¹¹ C/kg — about 1836 times the same ratio for a hydrogen ion. That single comparison announced that the electron is vastly lighter than any atom: the first subatomic particle had a measured fingerprint.
• EXAMPLE — Notice the strategy, because it recurs across physics: when two quantities cannot be measured separately, measure a well-chosen COMBINATION of them. e/m here, μ₀ε₀ giving c elsewhere — the same trick, different costumes.

Solved Numericals
• Five exam-room rehearsals. Cover each answer, work the problem, then compare — the method, not the number, is the training.
• Numerical 1 (NCERT Example 1.1): a cube of side 7.203 m (4 significant figures). Surface area = 6 × (7.203)² = 311.3 m²; volume = (7.203)³ = 373.7 m³ — both reported to 4 figures, honouring the input.
• Numerical 2 (NCERT Example 1.2): mass 5.74 g (3 figures) in volume 1.2 cm³ (2 figures). Density = 5.74/1.2 = 4.782…, reported as 4.8 g/cm³ — the volume's 2 figures cap the answer.
• Numerical 3 (errors): R = V/I with V = 100 ± 5 V and I = 10 ± 0.2 A. R = 10 Ω; percentage error = 5% + 2% = 7%; so R = 10.0 ± 0.7 Ω.
• Numerical 4 (dimensions): kinetic energy ½mv² has [M][L T⁻¹]² = [M L² T⁻²]; work = force × displacement has [M L T⁻²][L] = [M L² T⁻²]. Identical — exactly as the work–energy theorem demands.
• Numerical 5 (auditing): v² = u² + 2as. [v²] = [u²] = [L² T⁻²]; [as] = [L T⁻²][L] = [L² T⁻²]. Term by term, the equation is dimensionally sound.
